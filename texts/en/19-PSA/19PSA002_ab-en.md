## Kapitel 2


### The Laughter of the One Who Cannot Be Threatened

\d [^1]

**1** Why do the nations make their noise,[^2]
> and peoples plan what comes to nothing?

**2** The kings of the earth take their stand;
> rulers conspire together
> against the LORD and against his anointed —\f + \fr 2:2 \fk KULTUR \fq his anointed \ft The Hebrew word *mashiaḥ* (from which "Messiah" and "Christ" are derived) is here a political royal title: "the one set apart by the oil-anointing ceremony" — the designated king, YHWH's vassal. Alter: "The Hebrew emphasis of this concept seems to be more political than theological." There is nothing eschatological in this word as used here; the text is describing the current king in Jerusalem, not a future savior. — Rashi (1040–1105 CE), the greatest medieval Jewish commentator, read this psalm as referring to David's anointing and the Philistine rebellion against it (2 Samuel 5:17–25) — an explicitly anti-Christological historical reading he wrote as deliberate counter-polemic against Christian supersessionist claims. The New Testament later applies the psalm to Jesus (Acts 4:25–26, 13:33; Hebrews 1:5; Revelation 2:27). Both readings are legitimate responses to the text (Susan Gillingham, *A Journey of Two Psalms*, OUP 2013). The Hebrew itself requires neither.\f*

**3** "Let us break their chains apart
> and throw their ropes from us!"

**4** The One enthroned in the heavens laughs;[^3]
> the Lord holds them in contempt.

**5** Then in his anger he speaks to them;
> in his burning wrath he terrifies them:

**6** "But I — I have installed my king[^4]
> on Zion, my holy mountain."

**7** I will tell of the LORD's decree.[^5]
> He said to me: "You are my son;
> today I begot you.\f + \fr 2:7 \fk KULTUR \fq You are my son; today I begot you \ft This is the coronation adoption-formula of the ancient Near East. Egyptian royal ideology declared the pharaoh the physical son of Amun-Re, born through divine union. Israel borrowed the form but transformed the content: in Psalm 2, the king becomes YHWH's son not through divine biology but through a legal-relational *declaration* — a speech-act of appointment, not a claim of divine paternity. The "today" is the coronation moment, not a reference to eternal generation. — Two significant misreadings to avoid: (1) This is not about biological divine parentage. (2) It is not automatically a prediction about Jesus, though early Christian interpreters applied it that way. Acts 13:33 reads the resurrection as one fulfillment of "today I have begotten you." Hebrews 1:5 reads it as establishing Christ's superiority over angels. These are later applications, not the Hebrew text's original intention. Augustine read "today" as the eternal divine generation of the Son (pro-Nicene theology) — also later reception, not the coronation context the Hebrew inhabits. — The Jewish messianic tradition reads this as a promise of the future King Messiah, a human figure from David's line; the Christian tradition reads it Christologically. Both are interpretations of the same Hebrew text.\f*

**8** Ask it of me and I will give you the nations as your inheritance,
> the ends of the earth as your possession.\f + \fr 2:8 \fk UNGLAUBLICH \fq the ends of the earth as your possession \ft "Ends of the earth" is an ancient Near Eastern formula for universal sovereignty — a hyperbole meaning "all the earth, without limit." It is YHWH's authority over all creation that is being declared, not a divine real-estate grant to any human political entity. — This phrase has a documented colonial harm history that must be named. The Spanish *requerimiento* (the colonial proclamation read to indigenous peoples before conquest, authorizing war if they refused to submit to the Spanish crown and the Pope) drew directly on this verse as theological justification. British imperial mission theology and South African apartheid theology also deployed this verse to sanctify territorial conquest. The people who wrote Psalm 2 were almost certainly a colonized minority living under Ptolemaic imperial rule (c. 300 BCE), not an imperial court — they were the occupied people, not the occupiers. They did not write this as a land-grant for human empire. The harm done by those who used it that way is real and requires naming. (See Hossfeld and Zenger, *Psalms 1*, Hermeneia, Fortress Press, 2005.)\f*

**9** You will break them with an iron rod,[^6]
> shatter them like a potter's vessel."

**10** So then, kings, be wise;
> accept correction,\add rulers of the earth.\add*

**11** Serve the LORD with reverence;
> tremble and celebrate.

**12** Come in submission,[^7]
> or his anger may flare
> and you perish on the way,
> for his wrath can ignite in a moment.

> Happy are all who take refuge in him.\f + \fr 2:12 \fk KONTEXT \fq Happy are all who take refuge in him \ft The Hebrew *ashrei* is the wisdom-beatitude formula — the same word that opens Psalm 1 ("How happy — the one who..."). Its reappearance at the very end of Psalm 2 is the editorial bracket that binds the two psalms together as a single prologue to the entire Psalter: *ashrei* at Psalm 1:1, *ashrei* at Psalm 2:12. — This closing line performs an act of radical democratization. The entire psalm has been about kings, nations, rulers — an exclusively political-royal drama. Now, at the final moment, the category collapses: not "the king" who will receive the nations, not "Israel" who composed the psalm, but "all who take refuge" — anyone, without qualification, who shelters in this God. The one who laughed at the powerful is the one in whom anyone may take refuge. The same God whom the nations tried to overthrow is the one who holds the door open. This is the psalm's pastoral gift.\f*

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *No superscription*: Psalm 2 has no Hebrew superscription — one of only two exceptions in the entire first book of the Psalter (Psalms 1–41; the other is Psalm 1). This is not an omission but an editorial decision. By removing Psalm 2 from the Davidic biographical collection (Psalms 3–41 all carry "of David" headers), the editors elevated it to a universal-theological register: this psalm belongs to no particular moment in David's life but to the fundamental question of the entire Psalter — who holds sovereignty over the earth? Together with Psalm 1, it forms a two-part prologue: Psalm 1 sets Torah piety as the reader's posture; Psalm 2 sets divine kingship as the Psalter's theological center. Crucially, the same Hebrew word *ashrei* ("happy") that opens Psalm 1 closes Psalm 2 (v. 12) — an editorial bracket binding both psalms as a unit (Talmud *Berakhot* 9b–10a preserves the tradition that the two were originally counted as one psalm). The Greek translation (LXX) adds a superscription "of David," which early Christian interpreters used to apply the psalm to Jesus. The Hebrew does not.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *make their noise / plan what comes to nothing*: The Hebrew verb *ragash* describes a disorderly political tumult — Dahood compares it to Ugaritic *rgs* ("forgather"), a military assembly. Goldingay: "like a gang of thieves making a ruckus." *Why?* is not a genuine question but a rhetorical cry of astonishment — the incredulous "Are you kidding me?" of someone watching powerful people act stupidly. — The second colon uses the verb *hagah* meaning "murmur, plot, meditate." This is the same verb used in Psalm 1:2, where the righteous person *meditates* (same word) on God's instruction day and night. Here the nations *meditate* (*hagah*) emptiness (*riq*). The editorial design is deliberate: the nations are doing what the righteous do, but with nothing at their center. This wordplay between Psalm 1:2 and 2:1 is untranslatable in English — both use identical Hebrew but point in opposite directions. Every European translation loses it. It can only be preserved in this note.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *laughs*: The divine laughter here is not cruelty or malice — it is the laughter of the one whose power is so absolute that the conspiracy against it is simply absurd. Cassiodorus (sixth century): "God judges without getting upset." Goldingay: "YHWH does not take them with the seriousness with which they take themselves." The laugh is at the arrogance of power, not at the suffering of the powerless. For any reader who has ever felt small before overwhelming institutional power, this laughter is not threatening but clarifying: the powerful are not as powerful as they think. — Note the two-stage divine response: laughter (v. 4) escalates into blazing anger (v. 5). The contempt becomes wrath when God speaks.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *But I — I*: The Hebrew *waʾănî* carries an emphatic pronoun (*ʾănî* = "I, myself") that is the theological pivot of the entire psalm. NICOT (deClaissé-Walford, Jacobson, Tanner): "But *I* — I have installed my king." The double emphasis underlines that divine action has already happened: the king is installed. The conspiracy is futile not because armies will stop it but because the world has already moved on. Past tense: *nāsakhti* — completed action.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *the LORD's decree*: The Hebrew *hoq* is not a general law but a specific royal protocol document — the written charter read at the king's enthronement to declare his legitimacy. NICOT: "a technical term from royal law — the document of legitimacy, the royal protocol that was written down at the enthronement." It is comparable to the Mesopotamian "tablet of destinies" given to a new king at coronation. This is not Deuteronomic Torah-law but a specific appointment deed.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *iron rod / potter's vessel*: The violent imagery of this verse is almost certainly drawn from an ancient Near Eastern coronation ceremony, not from a military order. Temple walls, stelae, and scarabs throughout the ancient Near East depicted the king swinging an iron mace above bound enemies — a symbolic assertion of authority, not a literal description of mass slaughter. More specifically: a probable coronation ritual inscribed the names of enemies on clay pots and shattered them — a performative declaration that hostile forces were under royal control. Hossfeld and Zenger: "These images must be understood from their historical-cultural location; they are no legitimation of aggressive, destructive violence." — The Greek translation (LXX) reads the verb as "shepherd" (*rāʿâ*) rather than "break" (*rāʿaʿ*) — the root letters are almost identical. Kidner (Tyndale commentary): "The rod has dual functions: it shepherds and it smashes." The text holds both possibilities. — This psalm was written by a colonized minority under Ptolemaic rule. For them, the image of their God smashing the pots named for their oppressors was liberating fantasy, not imperial program. Those who later used this verse to authorize actual conquest did violence to its original social location.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *Come in submission*: This verse contains the most difficult textual problem in the entire psalm. The Hebrew phrase *naššĕqû-bar* has three proposed interpretations: (1) "Kiss the son" — if *bar* is Aramaic for "son." The Reformers and the King James Version follow this reading. But the psalm uses the standard Hebrew word for "son" (*ben*) in verses 7 and 12; the sudden appearance of the Aramaic *bar* here is suspicious, and this reading imports Christological freight ("the Son") unwarranted by the Hebrew grammar. (2) "With purity be armed" — Robert Alter (W.W. Norton, 2007) revocalizes *bar* as *bor* ("purity") without changing the consonants and reads the verb in a sense found in Psalm 78:9 ("be armed/equipped"). The resulting phrase is unusual but textually defensible. (3) "Seize instruction" — the Greek translation (LXX), probably a paraphrase. — The translation here renders the verse functionally as political submission: *naššĕqû* is the act of bowing to touch lips to the ground before a king's feet — the standard ancient Near Eastern gesture of political homage (cf. the Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III, showing the Israelite king Jehu prostrating before the Assyrian king). The mouth that opened in rebellion in verse 3 closes in submission here. — Note also the near-identical Hebrew roots at play: "Serve" (*ʿābad*) and "perish" (*ʾābad*) differ by one vowel. The nations are offered a choice between two almost identical words with opposite outcomes.

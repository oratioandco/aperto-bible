## Kapitel 4


### When I Call

\d For the choirmaster. With stringed instruments. A psalm of David.[^1][^2]

**1** Answer me when I call,[^3]
> God who stands up for what's right.
> When I was in a tight place, you gave me room.\f + \fr 4:1 \fk KULTUR \fq tight place / you gave me room \ft The Hebrew word pair *ṣar* and *rāḥab* — narrow and wide — is the psalm's foundational image. *Ṣar* is the cramped wadi, the locked cell, the squeeze of social or economic constraint, the place where you cannot move. *Rāḥab* is the wide open space where breath returns. The biblical scholar William P. Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*, Westminster John Knox, 2002) has shown that this spatial pair runs as a central metaphor through the entire Psalter: God as the one who creates room. The pair carries faint maternal-birthing resonance — the same vocabulary in Psalm 18:19 and Psalm 22:9–10 describes divine action that elsewhere is the work of midwives and nursing mothers. The verse is in the past tense: *you gave me room*. The speaker is grounding tonight's plea on last year's deliverance — the prayer is not a leap into hope but a return to a remembered mercy.\f*
> Have mercy on me. Hear my prayer.

**2** You powerful ones — how long?[^4]
> How long will you drag my name through the dirt?\f + \fr 4:2 \fk TEXT \fq drag my name through the dirt \ft The Hebrew *kāḇôd* has a double register: it can mean a person's social honor — their standing in the community, the weight their word carries — and it can mean the divine glory of God. The biblical scholar Jerry Hwang (*Old Testament Essays* 30/3, 2017) argues that the two registers are deliberately woven together in this verse: to attack the speaker's *kāḇôd* is implicitly to attack YHWH's *kāḇôd*, because the speaker has anchored their identity in YHWH. The interpreter James Mays writes that in ancient Israel, "the loss of honor had tragic consequences for self-esteem and social competence. Shaming and humiliating a person was violence against them — worse than physical harm." That is what is happening here. The speaker's social existence is being destroyed.\f*
> You love what is empty.
> You go after lies.\f + \fr 4:2 \fk KONTEXT \fq what is empty / lies \ft John Goldingay (*Baker Commentary*, 2006) suggests these words may carry a Baal-polemic edge. The wealthy class — whose grain and wine (v. 7) abound — may be Israelites who have turned to Baal worship during agricultural crisis, looking to the storm-god for the harvest YHWH has not produced. On this reading "what is empty" and "lies" name the alternative religious resources that the speaker considers fraudulent. The closing exclusivity of v. 8 — "for you alone, LORD" — directly answers Baal: only YHWH gives the safety of sleep. Even without the Baal context, the words name every substitute for YHWH that ultimately does not hold.\f*
\qs Selah

**3** But know this:[^5]
> the LORD has set apart his loyal one for himself.
> The LORD hears when I call.

**4** Tremble — and do not sin.[^6]
> Speak in your own hearts on your beds.\f + \fr 4:4 \fk KULTUR \fq speak in your own hearts on your beds \ft The Talmud (*Berakhot* 4b–5a) cites this exact verse as the scriptural mandate for the Jewish bedtime *Shema* — the rabbinic obligation to recite the *Shema* prayer before sleep. Psalm 4 is not part of the bedtime *Shema* liturgy; it is the *justification* for the practice. For more than a thousand years, this verse has authorized the Jewish nightly act of carrying one's day to God before sleep. — Basil the Great, in his *Long Rules* (Question 37, 4th century CE), applies the same verse to the Christian practice of nightly *examen* — the bed as the place of honest self-examination before God. Both traditions, Jewish and Christian, read this verse as authorizing what the speaker is doing in v. 8: lying down to give the day's unfinished business to God before sleep.\f*
> And be silent.
\qs Selah

**5** Bring what is just.[^7]
> And trust the LORD.

**6** So many are asking:[^8]
> "Who will show us anything good?"
> Let the light of your face shine on us, LORD.\f + \fr 4:6 \fk TEXT \fq let the light of your face shine on us \ft This phrase echoes the Aaronic Blessing of Numbers 6:24–26 — "The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face shine on you and be gracious to you" — the priestly benediction spoken at the close of the daily Temple service. The speaker is asking, on behalf of the hungry community, for the same divine presence that the Temple priests would have invoked over the people. — Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* (c. 392–420 CE) read this verse as a foundational image: the human person as God's coin, marked with the divine image (*imago Dei*), defaced through sin and re-minted through baptism. The image is theologically rich but rests on a Greek translation: the Septuagint reads *esemeiothe* ("was sealed") in v. 7 rather than the Hebrew text's *nĕsâ* ("lift up"), and Augustine built his coin imagery on the Greek. The Hebrew text is doing something quieter — asking for the priestly benediction's *light* to shine where the community is hungry and afraid.\f*\f + \fr 4:6 \fk APOLOGETICS \fq the light of your face shine on us \ft The priestly blessing this verse echoes (Numbers 6:24–26) is the oldest piece of biblical text we possess in physical form. In 1979, the archaeologist Gabriel Barkay excavated two tiny silver scrolls — the Ketef Hinnom amulets — from a burial cave just outside Jerusalem's Old City. Rolled around the necks of the dead, the scrolls carry the Hebrew words *yāʾēr YHWH pānāyw ʾēlēkā* — "may the LORD make his face shine on you" — engraved in pre-exilic paleo-Hebrew script. Carbon dating and paleographic analysis place them in the late seventh century BCE, roughly six hundred years before the earliest Dead Sea Scroll. The text on those amulets is essentially identical to the Hebrew text translated into modern Bibles today. — What the Ketef Hinnom find demonstrates is not that the priestly blessing "is true" — silver scrolls cannot prove theology. What they demonstrate is textual stability across two and a half millennia. The Hebrew words ancient Israelites wore as a death-protection amulet, the words quoted in this psalm a few centuries later, the words spoken at synagogue and church services this Sabbath, are recognizably the same words. The translation history of the Bible is sometimes told as a game of telephone in which the original message is lost. The archaeology suggests the opposite. — The find also confirms what the psalm assumes: the priestly blessing was a living, daily-life formula in pre-exilic Israel, not a late literary invention. When Psalm 4:6 cites it, the citation is not an antiquarian gesture. It is a quotation of a phrase the speaker's hungry community would have heard from priests as recently as last week.\f*

**7** You have put joy in my heart[^9][^10]
> more than they have
> when their grain and wine abound.

**8** In peace, all at once,[^11]
> I lie down and sleep —
> for you alone, LORD,\f + \fr 4:8 \fk KONTEXT \fq for you alone, LORD \ft The Rule of St. Benedict (chapters 17–18, c. 530–540 CE) prescribes Psalm 4 as a fixed Compline psalm — to be recited every night, year-round, without rotation. For more than fifteen centuries, in Benedictine communities across the world, this verse has been the last words of the day. Few biblical texts have shaped more Christian lives through repetition. — In Jewish tradition, the Talmud (*Berakhot* 4b–5a) cites Psalm 4 as the scriptural mandate for the bedtime *Shema* — the rite that closes the Jewish day. Both traditions, Jewish and Christian, have chosen this psalm to authorize what they do at the end of the day. — Athanasius (*Letter to Marcellinus*, 4th century) preserves a precise nuance about how this psalm should be prayed: not as a petition in the middle of trouble, but as a thanksgiving at trouble's end. The peace of v. 8 is not a wish; it is a witness to what trust looks like once it has been performed all the way through.\f*\f + \fr 4:8 \fk LEBEN \fq I lie down and sleep \ft Timothy Keller (*Songs of Jesus*, 2015) identifies three obstacles to the kind of sleep this verse describes: a *divided* heart (where success, reputation, or relationships function as the actual center of life — v. 2: the powerful "love what is empty"); a *bitter* heart (unprocessed resentment carried into the night — v. 4: "speak in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent"); and the *absence of God's face* (the sense of distance from God — v. 6: "let the light of your face shine on us"). The psalm offers a three-movement nightly practice: self-examination (v. 4, the rabbinic and Basilian tradition), seeking God's presence (v. 6), and somatic relinquishment (v. 8, the Compline tradition). This is not a religious technique. It is the structure that turns trouble into rest.\f*\f + \fr 4:8 \fk UNGLAUBLICH \fq for you alone, LORD \ft The line is often heard as a peaceful bedtime blessing — and there are sermon traditions, particularly in American evangelicalism, that lean into this lullaby reading. The psalm itself resists it. The speaker's circumstances at v. 8 have not improved. The wealthy slanderers are still slandering (v. 2 has not been undone). The community is still hungry (v. 6's question hangs in the air, unanswered). The speaker is still poor (v. 7's grain and wine still belong to others). What has changed is not the world. What has changed is where the speaker has located their security: *for you alone, LORD*. *Lĕḇādād* — alone — is exclusive. It is not "you primarily" or "you above all"; it is the repudiation of every alternative resource. Goldingay reads this as YHWH's answer to Baal: only YHWH gives the safety of sleep. Read this way, v. 8 is not a lullaby. It is a costly theological claim made with the body. — In *The Spirituals and the Blues*, the theologian James Cone wrote about how Christians under American slavery and Jim Crow read the Psalter's peace passages not as guarantees-of-safety but as testimonies of presence. *In peace, all at once, I lie down* is what the speaker chooses while the conditions that made the prayer necessary have not changed.\f*
> make me dwell in safety.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *For the choirmaster. With stringed instruments*: The Hebrew superscription has two musical directions. *Lamenatzeach* — "for the choirmaster" or "to the music director" — names the leader of the temple's musical performance. The Greek translation (the Septuagint) renders this *eis to telos*, "to the end" or "to the goal," and on this Greek reading the entire patristic tradition (especially Augustine) interpreted Psalm 4 as an eschatological text pointing to Christ. The Hebrew is doing something more concrete: a specific musician at the temple, not an end-times horizon. Rashi, the eleventh-century French Jewish commentator, explicitly defended this reading against Christian reinterpretation. — *Benigginot*, "with stringed instruments," indicates accompaniment by the *kinnor* or *nevel* — lyre or harp. This sets the psalm's sonic register: chamber music, intimate, lyrical — not battle hymn or fanfare. — Psalm 4 is the evening half of a deliberate diptych with Psalm 3, the morning prayer. The two psalms share a vocabulary cluster — *kāḇôd* (honor), *šāḵaḇ* (lie down), *rabbîm ʾōmrîm* ("many are saying"), *qrʾ/šmʿ* (call/hear) — that the Psalter's editors used to mark the two as a deliberate morning–evening pair. The opening of the Psalter, in the Hebrew shape, is a "day of the soul": Psalm 3 in the morning, Psalm 4 at night.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *Selah*: The word *Selah* appears twice in this psalm — after v. 2 and after v. 4 — and seventy-one times across the Psalter. Its meaning is uncertain. It is probably a musical or liturgical direction: a pause, an interlude, perhaps a moment for the lyre or harp to swell while the singers held silent. The medieval Spanish-Jewish commentator Ibn Ezra read it as "lift up" (a call for the music to rise). It is not a word to be pronounced as part of the prayer. It is a marker in the score. The translation preserves it in italics where it appears.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *God who stands up for what's right*: The Hebrew title is *Elohê ṣidqî* — literally "God of my righteousness." This is a legal address. The speaker is calling on YHWH not as a generalized helper but as the courtroom vindicator who clears the falsely accused. *Ṣedeq* in the Psalter is not personal virtue but the rightness God owes to the slandered, the poor, and the wrongly accused — what an honest judge would do for someone whose case has been buried. The German biblical scholars Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger title the entire psalm "the prayer of a poor person for the demonstration of God's justice." The plea in v. 1 is not pious. It is a court summons.
[^4]: **KULTUR** — *you powerful ones*: The Hebrew *bĕnê ʾîš* — literally "sons of a man" — is a status term for the wealthy and influential members of a community. It is deliberately distinguished from the more common *bĕnê ʾādām*, which means ordinary humanity (Psalm 49:2; Psalm 62:9). Most English Bibles render *bĕnê ʾîš* as "sons of men" — which collapses a class marker into a generic universal and erases the psalm's social setting. The speaker is not addressing humanity in general; they are confronting a specific class of people: the wealthy slanderers whose "grain and wine abound" (v. 7) while the speaker is poor and slandered. Hossfeld and Zenger read the whole psalm as the prayer of a poor person against the powerful: "they should practice social solidarity and stop exploiting the poor." Without the class marker, the psalm becomes generic moralizing. With it, the psalm is a confrontation.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *his loyal one / has set apart*: The Hebrew word *ḥāsîd* comes from the root *ḥesed* — covenant loyalty, the sustained mutual faithfulness between God and the people. The *ḥāsîd* (singular) is the person who lives inside that covenant relationship and has experienced God's *ḥesed*. The English Bible tradition has rendered it variously as "saint," "godly one," or "holy one" — but each of these carries baggage that the Hebrew does not. "Saint" sounds Catholic-canonical; "holy one" sounds set-apart-in-virtue; "godly" sounds pietistic. "Loyal one" preserves the relational covenant root: it names someone bound to God by faithfulness, not someone elevated by personal religious achievement. — The Hebrew verb *hiplâ* is strong: God has *singled out*, *set apart for special treatment*, the loyal one. It is past completed action — not "may God choose me," but "God has already chosen." This declaration is the theological hinge of the psalm. The peaceful sleep of v. 8 rests on v. 3's certainty.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *Tremble*: This is the most consequential translation decision in the entire psalm. The Hebrew word *rigzû* means *tremble*, *quake* — it belongs to the vocabulary of theophanic awe, the trembling of the people at Sinai (Exodus 19:16–18) and of the nations before YHWH's holiness (Psalm 99:1). It is not the language of anger management. — The Greek translation (the Septuagint, c. 250 BCE) renders *rigzû* as *orgizesthe* — "be angry." This Greek reading is the version Paul quotes word-for-word in Ephesians 4:26: *"Be angry but do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your wrath."* For two thousand years, this verse has been read by English-speaking Christians as Paul's instruction on managing anger before bedtime — a counsel against grudges. The Hebrew original is doing something different. It is not addressed to believers handling their emotions. It is addressed to powerful slanderers, calling them to *tremble* before the holy God they have ignored. The instruction is theophanic awe, not interpersonal mood-regulation. — The English-speaking reader who has known this verse only as "Be angry but do not sin" is meeting it for the first time in its Hebrew form. The Greek reading is not wrong as Paul deploys it; it has its own legitimate ethical force in Ephesians. But the underlying psalm is a different text, doing a different thing.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *bring what is just*: The Hebrew phrase *ziḇḥê-ṣedeq* is conventionally translated "right sacrifices" or "sacrifices of righteousness," which makes it sound like cultic ritual — the proper temple offerings. The biblical scholars Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger have argued that in the context of this psalm — a poor person addressing wealthy slanderers — the phrase carries a different meaning. *Ṣedeq* in the Psalter is the rightness God owes to the marginalized; the powerful are being asked to *do justice*, not to bring better offerings. The instruction sits in the prophetic tradition (Amos 5:21–24; Isaiah 1:10–17; Micah 6:6–8) where God refuses ritual from people who have failed at social solidarity: stop exploiting the poor; share what you have; restore the slandered to their place in the community. — The verb *biṭḥû* (trust) at the end of the verse uses the same Hebrew root (*bṭḥ*) that returns in v. 8, "make me dwell in safety." The speaker is asking the wealthy to live by the same trust they themselves practice in the closing line of the psalm.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *anything good*: The Hebrew word *ṭôḇ* — translated here "good" — has, in this verse, a concrete material sense. It does not mean an abstract philosophical good. It means food. Harvest. The year's grain. The difference between eating and not eating. John Goldingay's *For Everyone* commentary on the Psalms makes this concrete: "There is no Hispanic store, no farmers' market, no supermarket — mostly people eat what they grow; no grow, no eat." The "many" who are asking *who will show us anything good?* are not philosophical skeptics. They are hungry. The community is in subsistence crisis. The question is not *what is the meaning of life?*; it is *who will produce the food?* This makes the speaker's response in vv. 7–8 unusually bold: the speaker does not argue with the hunger or pretend it is not real. They claim that the joy God has put in their heart is *more than* what the wealthy have when their grain and wine abound — a counter-claim against the dominant value hierarchy, not a denial of material need.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *more than they have when their grain and wine abound*: This is one of the boldest claims in the entire Psalter. The speaker is poor. The wealthy slanderers (v. 2) have grain and wine in abundance — a real, demonstrable harvest, not a metaphor. The speaker does not deny this. Instead, the speaker makes a counter-claim: the joy God has put in their heart is *more* than what the wealthy possess in material abundance. Not "enough." *More.* The Latin American liberation theologian Elsa Tamez (*Bible of the Oppressed*, 1982) calls this kind of move a gesture of resistance: the poor person refuses the dominant value hierarchy and claims an alternative currency. J. Clinton McCann distills the psalm's whole argument into one line: "Belonging to God changes everything — values, priorities, lifestyle." This is not consolation for poverty. It is a refusal to accept that abundance is the highest good.
[^10]: **LEBEN** — *joy in my heart*: The verse is a counter-claim, not a feeling-report. The speaker is not saying *I happen to feel happy tonight*. The speaker is making a public, verbal claim about a value hierarchy: that the inner gift God has given is worth more than the visible material abundance of the wealthy. — The formation work this verse asks for is therefore not the cultivation of pleasant emotion. It is the daily practice of refusing the value scale that surrounds the speaker — the scale that says *the people with grain and wine are the people who are doing well*. To say *you have put joy in my heart, more than theirs* is to perform, with one's mouth, the disowning of the dominant scale. Across traditions — Catholic *examen*, Reformed thanksgiving, Quaker silence, Pentecostal testimony — there is a shared practice of nightly naming what God has given, by name, before sleep. The naming is the formation. Not because the words have power, but because the act of speaking the alternative scale aloud is what slowly trains the heart to actually live by it.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *in peace, all at once*: The Hebrew word for peace here is *šālôm*, and "peace" is the standard English rendering — but it is too narrow. *Šālôm* is wholeness of being: not merely the absence of conflict, but the presence of integrity, well-being, soundness. Robert Alter renders the line "In peace, all whole, let me lie down and sleep," using "all whole" to capture the Hebrew adverb *yaḥdāw* — which means *together*, *all at once*, *as one* — the totality of the act. The lying-down and sleeping happen as a single complete movement, not as a gradual drift. The body relaxes because the theology has held.

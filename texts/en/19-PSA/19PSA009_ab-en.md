## Kapitel 9

\d For the lead player; according to muth-labben. A psalm of David.[^1][^2]

### With My Whole Heart


**2** With my whole heart I praise you, \nd LORD—
> I will tell every impossible thing you have done.
**3** I rejoice in you. I celebrate in you.
> I sing to your name, Most High.\f + \fr 9:3 \fk KULTUR \fq Most High \ft The Hebrew title *ʿelyôn* — "Most High" — is not a generic name for God. It is a sovereignty title, drawn from the ancient Ugaritic divine name El Elyon, identifying YHWH as the supreme authority above every empire, every nation, every competing claim to power. When this psalm addresses God as "Most High," it is making a political claim: no human power is ultimate. The title appears in Genesis 14:18–20 (the Melchizedek encounter) and becomes central to the Psalter's theology of divine sovereignty over all earthly regimes.\f*

### The Throne Set for Judgment


**4** My enemies turn back—
> they stumble, they perish before you.
**5** You have taken up my cause, my right.[^3]
> You have taken the seat — the righteous judge.

**6** You rebuked the nations. You destroyed the wicked.[^4]
> Their name — erased. Forever and beyond.
**7** The enemy is finished. Ruins, permanent.
> Their cities uprooted — their very memory, gone.

### Fortress for the Crushed


**8** The \nd LORD sits forever—
> his throne set for judgment.
**9** He judges the world with justice,
> the peoples with equity.

**10** And the \nd LORD is a fortress for the crushed—[^5]
> a fortress in times of distress.
**11** Those who know your name will trust you.
> You have never abandoned those who seek you, \nd LORD\nd*.

### He Does Not Forget


**12** Sing to the \nd LORD, enthroned in Zion.
> Declare his deeds among the peoples.
**13** The one who avenges blood remembers—[^6]
> he does not forget the cry of the afflicted.

### The Gates


**14** Be gracious to me, \nd LORD.
> See my suffering at the hands of those who hate me—\f + \fr 9:14 \fk KONTEXT \fq the pivot \ft The psalm breaks here. For twelve verses, the speaker has been building a case for confidence — praising, testifying, declaring. Now, abruptly: "Be gracious to me." The abyss opens. All the praise was held over ongoing danger. The speaker is still at the gates of death while singing. This is not bad structure — it is honest psychology. The pivot is jarring because life is jarring.\f*
> you who lift me from the gates of death—\f + \fr 9:14 \fk KULTUR \fq gates of death \ft In ancient Israel, Sheol — the realm of the dead — was imagined as a walled underground city with gates. The "gates of death" were a spatial threshold: to approach them was to stand at the edge of non-existence. The image here is physical rescue — being pulled upward from the entrance of the underworld. — In v.15, the contrasting "gates of Daughter Zion" complete the spatial argument: one set of gates leads to silence and forgetting; the other leads to community, testimony, and praise. The movement from one to the other is what salvation looks like in this psalm.\f*
**15** so I can tell of all you are
> in the gates of Daughter Zion,\f + \fr 9:15 \fk KULTUR \fq Daughter Zion \ft The Hebrew *bat-ṣiyyôn* — "Daughter Zion" — is not simply another name for Jerusalem. It is a feminine personification of the covenant community: Jerusalem imagined as a woman, in her vulnerability and her capacity to receive testimony. The city gates were the place of public justice, commerce, and communal life — where the elders sat, where cases were heard, where survivors could speak. The psalmist brings praise to *her* gates — the community in her personhood receives the survivor's voice. Feminist scholars (Kathleen O'Connor, Ulrike Bail) have noted this theological figure's significance: the community that hears the cry is figured as feminine.\f*
> so I can rejoice in your rescue.

### Their Own Net


**16** The nations have sunk in the pit they made.
> In the net they hid, their own foot is caught.
**17** The \nd LORD is known by the judgment he makes:
> the wicked are snared by the work of their own hands.\f + \fr 9:17 \fk TEXT \fq Higgaion. Selah. \ft The Hebrew text adds *higgāyôn selāh* here — a unique double liturgical direction that appears only in this psalm and in Psalm 92. *Higgāyôn* may mean "meditation" or "murmuring" — a soft vocal sound. *Selāh* likely indicates an instrumental interlude. Together they seem to say: pause. Let what was just said settle. The wicked destroying themselves by their own mechanisms is worth contemplation. — The meaning of both terms was already uncertain in antiquity. The ancient Greek translation did not attempt to render them.\f*

> \add Higgaion.\add* Selah.

### Not Forgotten


**18** The wicked return to Sheol—[^7]
> every nation that forgets God.
**19** For not forever will the needy be forgotten.
> Not for all time will the hope of the afflicted perish.

### Arise


**20** Arise, \nd LORD. Do not let mortals prevail.[^8][^9]
> Let the nations be judged before you.
**21** Put fear in them, \nd LORD—
> let the nations know: they are mortal.

> Selah.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *muth-labben*: The Hebrew reads *mût labbēn* — a musical or liturgical direction whose meaning was already lost in antiquity. Jerome and Augustine read it as "for the death of the Son" (*pro morte Filii*) and built elaborate Christological interpretations on it. This reading is a documented hermeneutical harm: it displaces the psalm's actual concern — the crushed crying for justice — with proof-texting. Modern scholarship (Alter, Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger) treats it as an unrecoverable melodic or instrument designation. — The psalms were performed music. This note tells us the psalm had a known tune or register; what that tune was, we no longer know.
[^2]: **KONTEXT** — *A psalm of David*: Psalm 9 is the first half of a broken alphabetic acrostic — a poem designed to run from A to Z in the Hebrew alphabet. But the alphabet is fractured: several letters are missing or displaced. The missing letters *dalet* and *kap* together spell the word *dak* (\fq דַּךְ\fq*) — "the crushed" — the very word that appears in verse 9 as the psalm's key term for the oppressed. Whether deliberate or scribal accident, the effect is structural theology: the poem's own disorder enacts the broken lives of the people it describes. The acrostic spans MT Psalms 9 and 10 together (one poem in the ancient Greek translation). Psalm 9 is the first movement — praise that pivots into urgent petition. Psalm 10 is the second — raw lament that circles back to trust.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *my cause, my right*: The Hebrew *mišpāṭî wĕdînî* — "my judgment and my cause" — is courtroom language. The psalmist is not merely recounting a battle but testifying from a courtroom: God has heard the case and ruled in their favor. In the ancient Near East, the king's "sitting on the throne" was the formal legal act that opened judicial proceedings — the moment the judge took the bench. What follows in v.5b is that moment.
[^4]: **KULTUR** — *Their name — erased*: In the ancient Near East, a person's name was not a label — it was their existence in memory, their ongoing reality after death. Kings inscribed their names on monuments to ensure eternal remembrance. To erase someone's name was the ultimate imprecation: not merely death but cosmic annihilation — the revocation of their existence from history itself. When the psalm says "their name — erased," it is deploying the most serious possible language for destruction. The contrast with vv.18–19 (God will NOT forget the needy) inverts this logic: the powerful are unmade from memory; the crushed are kept.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *fortress for the crushed*: Two words collide here with physical force. *Miśgāb* — "fortress" — is an elevated, inaccessible military stronghold, a high tower no enemy can reach. *Dak* — "the crushed" — is a rare word (only four times in the entire Hebrew Bible) meaning someone physically pulverized, ground to dust by forces larger than themselves. The spatial contrast is the theology: the one at ground level, crushed flat, is raised to an inaccessible height. The word "fortress" appears twice in this verse — not for stylistic variation but for structural emphasis. The repetition hammers the certainty: fortress. Fortress. The same word, because the claim needs the weight of being said again. — This verse is cited by the liberation theologian Elsa Tamez as foundational evidence that YHWH is "the hope which makes possible the struggle for a new order of things."
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *avenges blood*: In ancient Israel, when someone was murdered and had no family member to pursue justice, YHWH took on the role of *gōʾēl haddām* — the blood-avenger. This was a specific legal function: the family's designated pursuer of justice for the murdered. The psalm says God claims that role for the afflicted who have no one else. — This verse became part of the *Av Harachamim* (\fq אב הרחמים\fq*, "Father of Mercies") — the Jewish memorial prayer for martyrs. It was composed after the Rhineland pogroms of 1096 (the First Crusade massacres of Jewish communities in Mainz, Worms, and Speyer) and has been prayed over Jewish communities killed in every subsequent persecution, including the Shoah. "He does not forget the cry of the afflicted" — prayed over those whose very existence others tried to erase. The verse carries this weight.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *the chiasm on forgetting*: Verses 18–19 form the theological heart of the psalm — a chiasm (mirror structure) built on the single verb *šākach* (forget): the wicked forget God and return to non-existence (v.18); the needy will NOT be forgotten by God and their hope will NOT perish (v.19). The same Hebrew root appears in both verses — the same word, turned inside out. Those who forget are un-existed. Those whom God refuses to forget are preserved. The two temporal markers — *lāneṣaḥ* (forever) and *lāʿad* (to eternity) — amplify the certainty through synonymous doubling. This is the psalm's central claim and its most quietly certain moment.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *Arise*: The Hebrew *qûmāh* is an energic imperative — the most forceful form of command the language possesses. It carries a technical legal sense: "arise for judgment" (cf. Psalm 76:10, Numbers 10:35). This is not a gentle request — it is the cry of the crushed for immediate divine judicial intervention. The civil rights tradition recognized this register: "How long, O Lord?" is the same grammar of urgent appeal to the only court that remains uncorrupted.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *mortals*: The Hebrew word *ʾĕnôš* does not mean "person" in a neutral sense. It emphasizes human frailty, finitude, and breakability — the mortal who will die, whose power is temporary, whose body can be broken. The same word appears in Psalm 8:4 ("what is a mortal that you remember them?") — where it evokes wonder. Here it evokes challenge: let the nations discover that they are merely mortal. Let them feel their own smallness. The psalm's final demand is that arrogant human power be shown for what it is: temporary, fragile, not ultimate.

## Kapitel 10


### Why Do You Stand So Far Off


**1** Why do you stand so far off, LORD?[^1][^2]
> Why hide — \add in\add* the worst of it?

### Portrait of the Wicked


**2** In his arrogance the wicked hunts down the vulnerable —
> let him be caught in the schemes he's made.

**3** The wicked boasts about what he craves;
> the profiteer curses and scorns the LORD.
**4** The wicked, nose in the air:[^3]
> "God won't look into it."
> Everything he schemes says: there is no God.

**5** His plans always work.
> Your judgments — too high for him to see.
> His enemies? He scoffs at them all.
**6** He says to himself: "Nothing can shake me.
> Nothing bad can touch me. Not ever."

**7** His mouth: curses, lies, extortion.[^4]
> Under his tongue: ruin and cruelty.

**8** He crouches in ambush in the outskirts,[^5]
> in the shadows he murders the innocent —
> his eyes scan for the wretched.
**9** He lurks in hiding like a lion in a thicket,
> lurks to snatch the vulnerable,
> snatches them — drags them into his net.

**10** He crouches. Coils.[^6]
> The wretched collapse under his weight.

**11** He says to himself:
> "God has forgotten.
> He's turned away his face.
> He'll never look."\f + \fr 10:11 \fk KONTEXT \ft This is the wicked man's third and most devastating monologue. He weaponizes the psalmist's own opening question. In v.1, the psalmist asks "Why do you hide?" as an act of faith — a covenant challenge addressed to a God believed to be accountable. Here in v.11, the wicked asserts "God has hidden himself" as a license for exploitation. Same observation. Opposite theological orientation. The psalmist's question presupposes relationship; the wicked man's assertion presupposes abandonment. The difference between lament and cynicism is not what they observe — it is whom they are speaking to.\f*

### Arise


**12** Arise, LORD.[^7]
> God — lift your hand.
> Don't forget the humble.

**13** Why does the wicked man scorn God?
> Why does he tell himself: "You'll never come looking"?

**14** You have seen it — you have.[^8]
> You watch the violence and the grief
> and you take it into your own hands.
> To you the wretched commit themselves.
> You — you are the one who helps the fatherless.

**15** Break the arm of the wicked.[^9]
> Break the arm of the evildoer.
> Hunt down his wickedness until nothing is left.

### The LORD Is King


**16** The LORD is king.[^10]
> Forever. And always.
> The nations vanish from his land.

**17** You hear what the humble long for, LORD.
> You steady their hearts, you lean in close —
**18** to bring justice to the fatherless and the crushed,[^11]
> so that no one on earth
> can terrify them. Ever again.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** Psalm 10 has no superscription — the only untitled psalm between Psalms 3 and 32. This is a deliberate canonical signal: Psalms 9 and 10 were originally a single alphabetic acrostic poem covering all 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet. Psalm 9 covers letters 1–11 (aleph through kaph); Psalm 10 begins at letter 12 (lamed: the "L" of *lāmāh*, "Why?"). — The acrostic then deliberately BREAKS for six consecutive letters (mem through tsade) throughout vv.2–11 — the section describing the wicked oppressor. The missing letters enact what the psalm describes: the alphabetic order that symbolizes cosmic order comes apart precisely where the social order is fractured. When the acrostic resumes at v.12 — "Arise, LORD!" (the letter qoph) — the form recovers at the same moment the psalmist demands that God restore order. The broken acrostic is not textual corruption. It is the psalm's central literary device: the form IS the theology.
[^2]: **KULTUR** The double "Why?" (*lāmāh... lāmāh*) is not a philosophical question about whether God exists. It is a covenant challenge — a question spoken within a relationship, addressed to a God who has made promises. In the Hebrew Bible, when Israel addresses God with "Why?", the presupposition is that God committed to justice and appears to be violating that commitment. This is not doubt. It is the anger of intimacy. The question intensifies faith rather than dissolving it — you can only demand accountability from someone you believe is accountable.
[^3]: **UNGLAUBLICH** "God won't look into it" — the Hebrew literally reads "there is no God" (*ʾein ʾĕlōhîm*). But the meaning is NOT intellectual atheism — not a philosophical denial that God exists. The wicked man of Psalm 10 is a covenant insider who exploits the poor within his own community while functionally living as if no divine accountability existed. The scholar Mitchell Dahood: "the wicked man does not deny the existence of God but only his intervention in human affairs." Elsa Tamez (Bible of the Oppressed): this is the oppressor who requires "other gods who will lend justification to their sinful deeds" — the worship of self-gratification that inevitably produces exploitation. — The four inner monologues of the wicked (vv.4, 6, 11, 13) expose the ideological infrastructure that enables exploitation: first practical atheism, then self-deification, then weaponized divine hiddenness, then denial of accountability. The psalm gives the oppressor four direct speeches. The poor get none. This is textual power asymmetry — the powerful control the discourse even in the poem that condemns them.
[^4]: **KULTUR** The word translated "extortion" is the Hebrew *tok* — a rare term appearing only four times in the entire Hebrew Bible. In two of those occurrences (here and Psalm 55:11) it is specifically linked to deception and false speech. The liberation theologian Elsa Tamez identifies it as "oppression-by-deception" — not mere theft or violence, but the kind of exploitation that requires ideological concealment. The oppressor's power depends on people believing his lies. The trio "curses, lies, extortion" exposes the speech of structural power: the oath-curses that bind the poor, the deception that obscures the theft, and the structural fraud that makes exploitation look legitimate. — Romans 3:14 quotes this verse ("Their mouths are full of cursing and bitterness") in Paul's catena of universal human indictment. Paul universalizes the claim to all humanity. The psalm's original meaning is more specific: this is the speech of predatory economic power.
[^5]: **KULTUR** The imagery shifts between two predator types — lion (vv.9a) and human trapper with a net (v.9b). In ancient Near Eastern royal iconography, the lion hunt was a symbol of legitimate royal power — the king as heroic lion-subduer. Psalm 10 inverts this: the powerful man is not a heroic hunter of lions but a predatory hunter OF the poor. The shift from animal predator (lion instinct) to deliberate human schemer (calculated net-trap) is itself a moral judgment — this is worse than animal behavior because it is deliberate.
[^6]: **TEXT** The Hebrew text of this verse is among the most difficult in the psalm — the scholar Robert Alter documents it as textually uncertain, with the received Masoretic Text likely corrupted. The translation preserves the image that emerges from the best scholarly reconstruction: the predator crouching before the pounce, and the victims collapsing under overwhelming force. The roughness is not a translation failure — the Hebrew itself stutters here, and the roughness may be deliberate: the poem's syntax breaks down where the violence is most intense.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *ק*: The Hebrew word *qûmāh* — "Arise!" — is a battle cry. In the Psalter it appears at moments of maximum urgency, calling on God to stand up, wake up, act (cf. Psalm 3:7; 7:6; 9:19; 17:13; 44:26). Structurally, this is also the moment the broken acrostic resumes: the letter qoph (\fq ק\fq*) is the 19th letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and it begins both the word "arise" and the address to God. The acrostic's restoration is itself a symbol of divine re-ordering. Where the alphabet broke (vv.2–11), chaos reigned. Where it resumes (v.12), the psalmist demands that God restore what was broken.
[^8]: **KULTUR** "The fatherless" — the Hebrew *yātôm* does not mean only a child whose biological father has died. In the patriarchal social world of ancient Israel, a *yātôm* was anyone stripped of the protective structure of family: orphaned children, yes, but also widows without a male household head, daughters without a father's protection, anyone outside the kinship network that provided economic security and legal standing. The biblical "widow, orphan, and stranger" triad (Deuteronomy 24:17; Zechariah 7:10) names the three categories of people who, in a patriarchal society, had no structural protection. Women are present in this category even when the grammar renders them invisible.
[^9]: **LEBEN** "Break the arm" is imprecatory prayer — honest petition for the destruction of exploitative power. The "arm" in Hebrew is the icon of active capacity: the arm that reaches, grabs, strikes, hoards. To pray "break the arm" is to pray for the dismantling of the oppressor's ability to harm — structural power, not a person's literal body. Feminist scholarship on imprecatory psalms distinguishes between (a) calls for personal vengeance against individuals, and (b) calls for the dismantling of exploitative power structures. This is squarely type (b): a prayer for the end of the system. — The prayer is uncomfortable. It should be. Honest prayer includes the desire that unjust systems be destroyed. The psalm models this as theologically legitimate — not a failure of charity but an act of prophetic faith.
[^10]: **KONTEXT** This is the first "The LORD is king" (*YHWH melek*) declaration in the entire Psalter. It plants a theological seed that Books 4–5 will grow into full celebration in the Enthronement Psalms (93, 95–99). The formula participates in a triptych central to Rosh Hashanah liturgy: "The LORD reigns" (Psalm 10:16) / "The LORD has reigned" (Psalm 93:1) / "The LORD shall reign forever" (Exodus 15:18). — Spoken here, after everything vv.2–11 just described, this is not naive optimism. It is counter-factual proclamation — a political speech act claiming that the exploiters' reign is temporary and that ultimate accountability lies beyond earthly power. The psalm does not resolve the "Why?" of v.1. It proclaims who God is regardless of present appearance. Both the question and the proclamation coexist with full tension.
[^11]: **KULTUR** "The crushed" — Hebrew *dak*, from the root *dkk* meaning "to crush, to pulverize, to grind." The image is grain being ground to dust under a millstone. This is not merely "the poor" — it is specifically those who have been broken by systemic force, ground down by power structures larger than any individual. The same word appears in Psalm 9, linking the two psalms. — The psalm's final word is not a resolution but a statement of purpose: "so that no one on earth can terrify them again." The humble, the fatherless, the crushed — the psalm does not promise them escape from suffering but promises them a God who hears, who steadies, who leans close, and whose justice will end the reign of terror. The question of v.1 remains. The promise of vv.17–18 stands beside it.

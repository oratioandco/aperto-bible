## Kapitel 12


### A World of Empty Words

\d [^1]

**1** Help, LORD.[^2]
> The faithful are gone.
> The trustworthy have vanished from the human race.

**2** Hollow words — that is what they speak to each other.[^3]
> With slick lips, with a double heart, they speak.\f + \fr 12:2b \fk KULTUR \fq double heart \ft The Hebrew *lev va-lev* ("a heart and a heart") is a unique expression — not a common idiom but a deliberate coinage. The heart (*lev*) in Hebrew is the organ of thinking and willing, not primarily of feeling. A "double heart" is not mere inconsistency or occasional dishonesty but a structurally divided selfhood — a person whose cognition permanently operates in two contrary directions, saying one thing while willing another, until deception becomes architecture rather than incident. The same root for "slick" (*chalaqot* — smooth, slippery) appears in Proverbs 2:16 and 7:5 to describe the manipulative seductress's speech — language designed to bypass the listener's defenses.\f*

**3** May the LORD cut off every slick lip,[^4]
> every tongue that talks big —

**4** those who say: "We will prevail by our tongue.
> Our lips belong to us.
> Who is lord over us?"\f + \fr 12:4 \fk KULTUR \fq who is lord over us? \ft The three-part boast escalates: (1) military language applied to speech — "we will prevail" (*nagbir* — "we will be mighty"); (2) radical ownership claim — our speech answers to no one; (3) theological rebellion — no authority, human or divine, is acknowledged. The word "lord" (*adon*) here is not a divine name but a social term for a master or authority figure. The boast reveals the worldview behind manipulative speech: language used as a power tool requires no accountability above itself. This is the psalm's diagnosis of how deception functions — not as occasional weakness but as a philosophy of radical autonomy from truth.\f*

**5** "Because the poor are being plundered,[^5]
> because of the groaning of the needy —
> now I will rise," says the LORD.\f + \fr 12:5b \fk TEXT \fq now I will rise \ft The divine oracle at the psalm's structural center. Four elements demand attention: (1) God acts *because of* the devastation — divine motivation is the suffering, not the prayer. (2) "Now" (*attah*) is logical not chronological — it signals decisiveness, not speed. (3) "I will rise" (*aqum*) is holy war language — the same verb Israel cried at the Ark's departure: "Arise, LORD!" (Numbers 10:35; Psalms 3:7; 7:6; 9:19; 10:12). After many psalms of unanswered "arise!" cries, God finally says it of himself. (4) This oracle was likely delivered in the temple liturgy by a cultic prophet — a dramatic interruption of the human voices.\f*
> "I will put them in a safe place.
> I will be the one who speaks for them."\f + \fr 12:5c \fk TEXT \fq speaks for them \ft The Hebrew *yapiach* is the psalm's most disputed word. Two readings: (1) from *puach* I — "one who pants/longs for safety" (NRSV); (2) from *puach* II — "a witness" (Alter, Goldingay, NICOT). We follow the witness reading: in a psalm dominated by speech-combat (liars vs. truth), God's promise to provide a "true witness" for the oppressed completes the logic. God enters the courtroom on behalf of those whose testimony is not heard. The alternative reading ("I will give safety to the one who pants for it") is noted but less structurally satisfying.\f*

**6** The words of the LORD are pure words —[^6]
> silver refined in a crucible on the ground,
> purified seven times over.

**7** You, LORD — you will guard them.[^7]
> You will keep him safe from this generation forever.

**8** On every side the wicked walk freely,[^8]
> as what is vile is lifted high among the human race.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *For the music director. On the sheminith. A psalm of David.*: The superscription *sheminith* designates either a bass register for the lowest male voices or an eight-stringed instrument — both point to this psalm's somber, weighty character. Only two psalms carry this marking (Psalms 6 and 12), both laments from the depths. The Talmud (Sotah 49b) treats Psalm 12 as a generational diagnostic — every era must ask whether the faithful have truly vanished. Rav Nahman and Rav Yosef resist the most pessimistic reading, insisting "we ourselves still embody those qualities." Luther's 1523 paraphrase of this psalm ("Ach Gott, vom Himmel sieh darein") became one of the foundation stones of Protestant hymnody, later set by Bach (BWV 2).
[^2]: **TEXT** — *Help*: The Hebrew *hoshiah* is a bare imperative — "Help!" or "Deliver!" — with no object. No "me," no "us." The absence is deliberate: this is not a personal cry but an intercession for a world in crisis. The same root (*yasha*) reappears in v.5 when God promises to "put them in a safe place" — the psalm's inner arc moves from human cry to divine echo using the same verbal family. The communal lament form (no first-person singular anywhere except in God's own speech at v.5) supports the reading that a liturgist is praying on behalf of silenced others.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *hollow words*: The Hebrew *shav* carries both "falsehood" and "emptiness/nothingness." The same word appears in the Third Commandment (Exodus 20:7 — "do not take the name of the LORD in *shav*") and the Ninth Commandment formulation (Deuteronomy 5:20 — a "false/empty witness"). The psalm's indictment of lying speech is implicitly a Decalogue-level charge. The speech described is not merely incorrect information — it is morally void, speech that has no substance behind it.
[^4]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *cut off*: The imprecatory element — praying for God to "cut off" the lips and tongues of the wicked — is directed at the weapon being used against the powerless. The prayer runs the right direction for a liberation reading: the voiceless asking God to disarm those who are harming them through linguistic mastery. Erich Zenger (*A God of Vengeance?*): psalms of "cursing" are properly understood as the powerless expressing their desires before God rather than taking vengeance themselves. The violence of the verb is proportional to the violence of what smooth speech does to those who cannot counter it.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *the poor... the needy*: The Hebrew *ani* (poor/oppressed) and *evyon* (needy/destitute) are concrete socioeconomic categories, not spiritual metaphors for humility. Elsa Tamez (*Bible of the Oppressed*): these terms denote people experiencing material exploitation — indebted smallholders, day laborers, those without property or patronage. The "smooth-lipped" wielders of vv.2-4 are their oppressors — creditors, corrupt officials, those whose mastery of language (legal sophistication, flattery, manipulation) gives them economic leverage. God's motivation for acting is not the eloquence of the prayer but the suffering of the poor. The poor themselves never speak in this psalm — they only groan. The psalm's speech-economy enacts at the formal level what it protests: the oppressed have been silenced.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *silver refined in a crucible*: The silver-purification metaphor makes an epistemological claim, not merely a power claim. In the ancient Near East, divine speech was reliable because gods were powerful. Here, divine speech is reliable because it has been *tested* — like metal going through fire until every impurity is removed. The word *alil* (crucible) is a hapax legomenon (occurs only here in the Hebrew Bible) — likely an earthen smelting vessel used in silver refining. "Seven times" is a Semitic completeness intensifier: utterly, totally purified. The implicit contrast with the "slick lips" of vv.2-4 is structural: smooth speech that has never been tested versus refined speech that has survived every trial. In a world of empty words, God's word has been through fire.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *guard them... keep him safe*: The pronouns shift from "them" (plural) to "him" (singular) — this is intentional in the Hebrew, not a scribal error. The ambiguity is theologically productive: the antecedent may be (a) the poor and needy of v.5, or (b) the pure words of v.6. The Greek Septuagint resolves the ambiguity by reading "us" throughout — but the Hebrew resists resolution. The divine words and the protected poor are intertwined: guarding one is guarding the other. We preserve the shift.
[^8]: **KONTEXT** — *the wicked walk freely*: The psalm's closing verse refuses triumphalist resolution. After the divine oracle (v.5) and the luminous meditation on divine speech (v.6), the psalmist returns to the unchanged world: the wicked still walk about openly, not furtively. The Hebrew verb *yithallakun* (hithpael — iterative/continuous) signals ongoing, defiant striding in public — heads held high, unashamed. Walter Brueggemann classifies this as a "psalm of disorientation": it models the discipline of holding divine promise and unchanged world simultaneously. The community has heard God speak and must return to an unreformed reality. The closing phrase "among the human race" echoes v.1 ("from the human race"), forming an inclusio: the psalm begins and ends in the same human world. The crux *kerum zullut* ("as vileness is lifted high") is the psalm's hardest textual problem; we follow NJPS/Hossfeld-Zenger: what is vile occupies the place of honor — the exact inversion of the silver metaphor of v.6.

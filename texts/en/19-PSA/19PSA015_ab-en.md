## Kapitel 15


### Who May Dwell

\d A psalm of David.[^1]

**1** LORD — who finds shelter in your tent?[^2]
> Who makes their home on your holy mountain?

**2** The one who walks with integrity,[^3]
> who does what is right,
> who speaks truth from the heart.\f + \fr 15:2b \fk TEXT \fq from the heart \ft The Hebrew phrase *bilbābô* — "in his heart" — is the key. This is not merely correct speech but integrated truthfulness. The person's interior matches their exterior. What they say to others is what they say to themselves. The verse describes not someone who never makes mistakes but someone who is not performing — whose truth starts inside and works its way out.\f*

**3** Their tongue carries no slander.[^4]
> They do no harm to a neighbor.
> They heap no disgrace on those close to them.\f + \fr 15:3c \fk TEXT \fq disgrace \ft The Hebrew *ḥerpâ* is far stronger than English "reproach" or "taunt." NICOT (Tanner): "Our culture does not have a word that adequately conveys the power of *ḥerpâ*." It describes public speech that compromises a person's participation in community structures — their employment, legal standing, marriage prospects, access to the communal economy. This is not a personal insult. It is social destruction — speech that costs someone their place in the world.\f*

**4** Who looks down on the corrupt[^5]
> but honors those who honor the LORD.
> Who keeps an oath \add even at personal cost\add*\f + \fr 15:4b \fk TEXT \fq keeps an oath \ft A textual crux. The Hebrew MT reads *nišbaʿ lehārāʿ* — which appears to say "he swore to do evil and does not retract." This is almost certainly a scribal metathesis (letter reversal): *lehārāʿ* (to do evil) should read *lerēʿēhû* (to his neighbor). The Greek Septuagint, Syriac, and Peshitta all support "sworn to his neighbor." Aperto follows the ancient versions: the person has made a commitment to someone and keeps it — even when keeping it costs them. The integrity of oath-keeping, not retracted when circumstances change.\f*
> and does not back out.

**5** Who lends no money at usury.[^6]
> Who takes no bribe against the innocent.\f + \fr 15:5b \fk TEXT \fq bribe against the innocent \ft The administration of justice in ancient Israel happened at the city gate, where elders heard disputes. A wealthy person could bribe a judge or witness to obtain a favorable verdict — at the cost of an innocent person's life, land, or freedom. This verse — together with the usury prohibition — reveals who the psalm addresses: people with money to lend and access to judicial proceedings. Goldingay: "The prescriptions make clear that the question in v.1 does not relate to ordinary people... people with power and money are subject to particular temptations." The psalm disciplines the powerful. The poor are the protected, not the addressed.\f*

> The one who lives this way\f + \fr 15:5c \fk TEXT \fq will never be shaken \ft The closing promise shifts to future tense — after the past-tense track record of vv.3–5b, the assurance is forward-looking. Augustine noticed this: description in past and present, promise in future. This is covenantal assurance, not a mechanical retribution guarantee. It does not promise immunity from suffering — ethical people suffer constantly. NICOT: "Suffering and death come for all, but for those who live in God's presence, the shadows of earth become thin." Mays: "The righteous, whose life is based in the way of God, are secured against any ultimate undoing." The stability promised is orientation — a foundation that holds when everything else shakes.\f*
> will never be shaken.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *Entrance liturgy*: Psalm 15 belongs to a specific ancient genre: the entrance liturgy. A worshipper approaching the Jerusalem Temple would pose the question — "Who may enter?" — and a priest or Levite would answer with an ethical catalog. What makes this psalm extraordinary is what the answer does NOT include: no sacrifice, no genealogy, no ritual purity test. Only ethics. Only how you treat people. The rabbinic tradition read its 11 requirements as Torah in miniature — condensing all 613 commandments into a single holdable list. — The psalm opens the carefully structured Psalms 15–24 collection, paired with Psalm 24 as a frame. At the collection's center stands Psalm 19 (a Torah psalm). The movement from "who may enter?" (Ps 15) to "the King who enters" (Ps 24) traces the entire ethical and theological journey of the subcollection. — The structural parallel with the Egyptian Book of the Dead (Chapter 125) is striking: the deceased before Osiris recites a list of sins not committed. Three critical differences: Psalm 15 addresses the living, not the dead; its requirements are genuinely ethical, not magical declarations; and its list is deliberately selective (11 items), not exhaustive (42 items).
[^2]: **TEXT** — *finds shelter / makes their home*: Two Hebrew verbs carry a deliberate gradient. The first — *yāgûr* (find shelter, sojourn) — is the verb of the *gēr*, the resident alien, the refugee who has no inherent right to be somewhere. The questioner frames themselves not as an entitled citizen but as a dependent stranger, approaching God's presence the way a refugee approaches a host community: with no claim, only a question. The second verb — *yiškōn* (dwell, make one's home) — implies something more permanent, moving from transient guest toward established resident. The psalm's opening question is not posed from entitlement. It is posed from vulnerability.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *integrity*: The Hebrew *tām��m* does not mean "perfect" or "blameless." It is the same word used for an unblemished sacrificial animal (Leviticus 1:3, 10) — and its core meaning is wholeness, integration: no gap between inner conviction and outer action, no split between what is believed and what is done. Kidner: "implies what is whole, or whole-hearted, and sound." Goldingay: "moral consistency, constancy, and stability." The formation question this word poses is not "Am I good enough?" but "Is there a gap between what I believe and how I live?"
[^4]: **TEXT** — *slander*: The Hebrew *rāgal* is a denominative verb from *regel* (foot). Literally: "to go about on one's tongue" — as a spy moves on their feet through enemy territory, this person's tongue moves through the community gathering and spreading harm. The image is covert, intentional, physical: speech as espionage against a neighbor's reputation. A Qumran manuscript (4QPsa) omits this line entirely, but the MT's triadic structure (three negations paralleling v.2's three affirmations) has stronger poetic logic.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *looks down on the corrupt*: The Hebrew doubles the contempt: *nibzeh* (the despised one) is *nimʾās* (rejected, refused) in their eyes. This is ethical discrimination — the refusal to honor or enable those who harm the community. NICOT (Tanner) calibrates: the psalm's concern is "tolerance of oppression," not personal contempt toward individuals. Diodore of Tarsus (fourth century): despise the wicked rich, honor the pious poor. — This clause carries a documented harm history: the moral grammar of "despising the despicable" has been weaponized to authorize exclusion and violence against LGBTQ+ people, religious minorities, and anyone designated as "reprobate." The psalm's actual criteria are behavioral and economic — no identity category appears.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *usury*: The Hebrew *nešek* comes from the root meaning "to bite" (confirmed from Ugaritic *ntk*). This is not neutral banking or ordinary interest. In the Persian-period ancient Near East, interest rates of 33–50% were attested. The consequence of such lending was debt slavery — losing one's freedom or being forced to sell one's children. The Torah's loan laws (Exodus 22:24–27; Leviticus 25:35–38; Deuteronomy 23:20) exist to prevent exactly this. *Nešek* is structural predation: lending to desperate people at rates designed to extract maximum pain. — Note: this prohibition carries a specific harm history. Medieval Christian authorities forced Jewish communities into moneylending (one of the few economic activities permitted to them), then cited Psalm 15:5 to condemn Jewish moneylenders. The psalm condemns exploitation. It was weaponized against the exploited — people fulfilling roles forced on them by structural economic exclusion.

## Kapitel 16

\d A miktam of David.[^1]

### Guard Me


**1** Guard me, God.[^2]
> I have run to you.\f + \fr 16:1 \fk KULTUR \fq run to you \ft Hebrew *ḥāsîtî bāk* — "I have taken refuge in you." This is not metaphorical trust-language. In the ancient Near East, *taking refuge* was a physical, legal act — running to a temple precinct or a patron's house and claiming the right to protection. The perfect tense matters: the speaker has already fled. The asylum is already claimed. What follows is not the request; it is the proceedings.\f*

**2** I said to the LORD —[^3]
> you are my Lord.
> My good — there is none beyond you.

### The Holy Ones


**3** As for the holy ones in the land,[^4]
> the mighty ones —
> they are all my joy.

**4** Those who chase after another god —
> their wounds multiply.\f + \fr 16:4 \fk TEXT \fq their wounds multiply \ft The Hebrew is difficult and much debated. The word *'aṣṣĕbôtām* can mean "their sorrows" or "their idols" (from the same root as "pain" / "idol"). The verse describes the consequence of divided loyalty: the more gods you serve, the more fractured your life becomes. The "blood libations" that follow may refer to Canaanite altar practices or — in a recent reading by Verburg (2020) — to necromantic rituals: pouring blood to summon the dead. Under either reading, the psalmist refuses the entire system.\f*
> I won't pour their blood libations.
> I won't carry their names in my mouth.

### My Portion


**5** LORD, you are my portion. My cup.[^5]
> You are the one who holds my lot.

**6** Measuring lines have fallen for me
> in pleasant places.\f + \fr 16:6 \fk TEXT \fq pleasant places \ft The "measuring lines" (*ḥăbālîm*) are the surveyor's cords stretched across land to mark boundaries during tribal allocation. The psalm uses them for someone who has no literal land — making the "pleasant places" not a geographical claim but a declaration that YHWH's presence is the territory. This verse has been widely misappropriated in prosperity-gospel contexts (notably Chris Tomlin's worship song "Boundary Lines") as a divine promise of material blessing. The Levitical background corrects this: the speaker has no material "pleasant places." The inheritance is God.\f*
> This inheritance — it is lovely to me.\f + \fr 16:6 \fk TEXT \fq lovely \ft Hebrew *nĕ'îm* — from the same root as *nĕ'îmôt* ("pleasures") in v.11. The echo is deliberate: the pleasantness of the inheritance here (YHWH as land) transforms into the eternal pleasantness of YHWH's presence at the psalm's climax. Where possible, hear both together.\f*

### Night Counsel


**7** I will bless the LORD who counsels me.
> Even in the night, the depths of me speak correction.\f + \fr 16:7 \fk TEXT \fq the depths of me \ft Hebrew: "my kidneys" (*kilyôtāy*). In ancient Israelite body-mapping, the kidneys were the seat of deepest moral intuition — the organ of conscience that speaks when external noise stops. The Talmud (Berakhot 61a) develops this: the right kidney counsels virtue, the left evil. This is not metaphor; it is somatic theology — the claim that moral truth lives in the body. The verse describes the alignment of external divine counsel (daytime) with interior moral knowledge (nighttime): what God teaches from outside, the body confirms from within.\f*

**8** I have set the LORD before me. Always.
> He is at my right hand. I will not be shaken.\f + \fr 16:8 \fk KULTUR \fq right hand \ft The "right hand" was the position of support in battle, advocacy in court, and honor at table. YHWH at the psalmist's right hand (here) is answered by the psalmist at YHWH's right hand (v.11) — forming a structural bracket that encloses the psalm's entire climactic movement. The transition: from being protected *by* God to being honored *at* God's side. The same image, inverted, carries the whole arc.\f*

**9** So my heart is glad,
> my whole self sings,\f + \fr 16:9 \fk KULTUR \fq my whole self \ft Hebrew: "my glory" (*kĕbôdî*). Not vanity — the essential self, the weight of one's being. Some manuscripts read "my liver" (*kĕbēdî*); Robert Alter translates "my pulse." The verse builds a tricolon of the whole person from innermost to outermost: heart (volition) → essential self (the core) → flesh (the body). All three are accounted for. The joy is not disembodied.\f*
> and my body — my body rests secure.

### The Path


**10** You will not abandon my life to Sheol.
> You will not give your faithful one to the Pit.\f + \fr 16:10 \fk KULTUR \fq Sheol... the Pit \ft *Sheol* = the realm of the dead; *šāḥat* (the Pit) = the grave-pit, a parallel term. The Jewish plain-sense reading: YHWH will not allow the speaker to die prematurely — a prayer for preservation in this life, not a claim about resurrection. The ancient Greek translation rendered *šāḥat* as *diaphthora* ("corruption/decay"), and Peter quotes this Greek version in Acts 2:27 — the first Christian sermon — arguing that since David did die and decay, the psalm must refer to Jesus' resurrection. This is the starkest Jewish-Christian interpretive split in the entire Psalter. Both traditions are legitimate inheritors of the text. The Edinburgh thesis (2018) demonstrates that the messianic reading grew from within Second Temple Judaism before Christian appropriation. — "Your faithful one" (Hebrew *ḥāsîd*): the one who practices covenant loyalty — *ḥesed*.\f*

**11** You make known to me the path of life.[^6]
> In your presence, joy to the full.
> At your right hand — pleasure, forever.\f + \fr 16:11 \fk TEXT \fq pleasure, forever \ft The final word of the psalm is *neṣaḥ* — "forever." The psalm opens with an urgent cry for protection and closes with eternal joy at God's side. Three ascending lines, each longer in breath: (1) the path named, (2) the joy specified as rooted in presence, (3) the pleasure located at God's right hand and extended into eternity. This tricolon is the psalm's climax and must not be compressed. — Note the structural echo: "at your right hand" here answers "he is at my right hand" in v.8. The same image, reversed: God stood beside the speaker for protection; now the speaker stands beside God for joy. The inclusion is complete.\f*

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *miktam*: The meaning of *miktam* is uncertain. The ancient Greek translation rendered it *stēlographia* — "inscription," something engraved to last. Another possibility: from *ketem*, "gold" — a golden psalm. All six *miktam* psalms (16, 56–60) are attributed to David and share themes of crisis and trust. What is clear: this label signals something meant to endure — a declaration carved into permanence.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *God*: The Hebrew is *'ēl* — identical to the Canaanite supreme deity's name. In the ancient Near East, El was the father of the divine assembly and cosmic sovereign. Using this name while addressing YHWH (v.2) enacts a theological claim: the God of Israel absorbs and transcends the highest deity the surrounding cultures recognized.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *I said*: The standard Hebrew text reads "you said" (second person feminine — *'āmarĕt*), introducing an unidentified female speaker. Most scholars follow the ancient Greek, Syriac, and Latin translations — plus 21 Hebrew manuscripts — in reading "I said" (*'āmartî*). Feminist scholar Armand Barus (2023) reads the feminine form as evidence of a preserved female voice — possibly a widow who has lost her land-inheritance rights. We follow the first-person reading; the female-psalmist hypothesis is a live scholarly option.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *holy ones*: Hebrew *qĕdôšîm* — a word that can mean either (a) the faithful human community of YHWH-worshipers, or (b) divine beings / members of the heavenly council. The ambiguity is ancient and unresolved. Most modern scholars (Goldingay, NICOT) read it as the faithful community. Others (Dahood, Clifford) see a reference to Canaanite divine assembly members being named positively before the rejection in v.4. We preserve the ambiguity.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *portion... cup... lot*: Three precise land-allocation terms in two verses: *ḥēleq* (portion — the allocated share of tribal territory), *kôs* (cup — symbolic vessel of one's allotted destiny), *gôrāl* (lot — the stone cast to determine divine will in land distribution, Joshua 14–19). In ancient Israel, one tribe — the Levites — received no land. God declared to be their *portion* and *inheritance* instead (Numbers 18:20; Deuteronomy 10:9). For anyone to claim this language is to identify with the deliberately landless as the spiritually enriched. In an agrarian economy, landlessness meant food insecurity, social marginality, and existential vulnerability. The claim is therefore astonishing, not pious boilerplate: the speaker has nothing — and declares YHWH to be more than everything.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *the path of life*: Hebrew *'ōraḥ ḥayyîm* — wisdom vocabulary. In Proverbs (2:19; 5:6; 10:17; 15:24), "the path of life" is the specific trajectory offered by Woman Wisdom that avoids Sheol. Placing this phrase at the psalm's climax connects the trust declaration to the entire wisdom tradition: the path is not a lifestyle philosophy but a concrete track — walked, physical — that leads through death to God's presence. Hossfeld-Zenger: "The path of life does not end in death because the fellowship with God built through life cannot be destroyed by death."

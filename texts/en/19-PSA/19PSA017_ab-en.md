## Kapitel 17


### Guard Me — the Pupil of Your Eye

\d A Prayer of David.[^1]

**1** LORD, hear my just cause.[^2]
> Attend to my outcry.
> Give ear to my prayer — no deceit on my lips.

**2** Let my verdict come from your face.
> Let your eyes see what is straight.

**3** You have probed my heart,[^3]
> come upon me by night,
> tested me like metal in fire — you found nothing wrong.
**4** What I resolved, my mouth kept.
> As for human affairs —
> by what your lips command,
> I, yes I, have watched for the paths of the robber
> and walked clear.
**5** My steps hold firm in your tracks.
> My feet — they have not slipped.

**6** I, yes I, call on you, God — for you will answer.
> Turn your ear to me. Hear my words.
**7** Show your wonders of steadfast love —[^4]
> you who rescue with your right hand
> those who take refuge from their attackers.

**8** Guard me — the pupil of your eye.[^5]
> In the shadow of your wings, hide me
**9** from the wicked who would destroy me,
> my mortal enemies who press in close.

**10** They have shut up their bloated hearts.
> With their mouths they speak with grandeur.
**11** Now they have tracked us, they surround us,
> eyes set to bring us down to the earth.
**12** He is like a lion, hungry to tear —
> a young lion, crouching in ambush.

**13** Rise, LORD.[^6]
> Confront him. Bring him to his knees.
> Save my life from the wicked — your sword.
**14** By your hand, LORD — from these mortals,[^7]
> these mortals whose portion is this life only.
> But your treasured ones — fill their bellies.
> Let their children have enough;
> let them leave the rest to their little ones.

**15** But I — in justice — I will see your face.[^8]
> When I wake — let me be filled with your form.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *A Prayer of David*: The superscription uses the word *tepillah* — "prayer" or "petition" — rather than the more common *mizmor* ("psalm/song"). Only five psalms carry this designation (Pss 17, 86, 90, 102, 142), signaling a text dominated by urgent petition rather than praise. The Davidic attribution (*lĕdāwid*) functions as a collection-designation: this psalm belongs to the First Davidic Collection (Pss 3–41). Within the deliberately structured sequence of Psalms 15–24, Psalm 17 occupies position C in a chiastic architecture — paired with Psalm 22, its "darker twin." Psalm 16 ends in quiet security; Psalm 17 follows as that same trust pressed under mortal attack; Psalm 18 answers with royal deliverance. The canonical editors placed this psalm at the hinge between trust and triumph.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *hear my just cause*: The Hebrew opens with *šimʿāh YHWH ṣedeq* — three words that set the entire psalm's frame. *Ṣedeq* here is not abstract "righteousness" but a forensic term: a legitimate legal case, a just cause brought before the court. The speaker is not praising God or confessing sin — the speaker is filing a brief. The same word (*ṣedeq*) returns at v.15 as "in justice" — creating an envelope structure (inclusio) that transforms the word's meaning: what begins as a legal claim ends as the ground of eschatological encounter with God.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *You have probed my heart, come upon me by night, tested me*: This is the formal protestation of innocence — one of only three canonical specimens in the Psalter (Pss 7, 17, 26). It is not a boast of sinlessness. It is a specific legal act: the speaker formally invites divine scrutiny of the charges against them. The three verbs — *probed, come upon, tested* — use metallurgical language: this is gold refined by fire. The night setting ("come upon me by night") may reflect a temple vigil: sleeping at the holy place through the night, awaiting God's verdict at dawn. "You found nothing wrong" is the result of God's investigation — not self-congratulation but testimony about what the divine examiner discovered. Anyone falsely accused has the right to present a legal case. That is what this form does.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *steadfast love*: The Hebrew is *ḥesed* — one of the most important and untranslatable words in the Hebrew Bible. It is not "mercy" (which is *raḥamim*, a different word) and not generic "kindness." *Ḥesed* names the loyalty one party keeps to another within a covenant relationship — faithfulness that continues even when circumstances make it costly. It is simultaneously love and fidelity, warmth and commitment, active and unwavering. "Steadfast love" (NRSV) is the scholarly consensus rendering. The psalmist does not ask for God's generosity in general — the psalmist asks God to act according to God's covenant-bound character.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *the pupil of your eye... the shadow of your wings*: Two images compressed into the structural center of the psalm. — The Hebrew for "pupil" is *ishon bat-ayin*, literally "the little man, daughter of the eye." *Ishon* means the tiny human reflection you can see in another person's pupil — the miniature of yourself visible in someone else's gaze. To be this to God is to be impossibly close to God's attention: the thing protected before the mind decides, the way an eyelid blinks reflexively. — "The shadow of your wings" draws on ancient Near Eastern protective iconography. In Deuteronomy 32:10–11, God guards Israel "as the pupil of his eye" and then immediately "like an eagle that hovers over its young, spreads its wings." The same double movement — eye-center to wing-shadow — appears compressed into this single verse. The imagery is multistable: simultaneously bird-mother, temple cherubim, and metaphorical protection. The psalm's theological claim is that God's protective attention is so immediate and so reflexive that it acts before conscious thought — the way a body protects its most vulnerable point.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *Rise, LORD. Confront him. Bring him to his knees.*: The staccato imperatives of vv.13–14 are imprecatory prayer — the speaker asks God to act against the enemies. The critical theological move: the speaker does NOT pick up the sword. The prayer *replaces* personal revenge. Erich Zenger (*A God of Vengeance?*, 1994) argues that the imprecatory psalms function against the psalmist taking retaliation: by handing the enemy's fate to God, the speaker relinquishes control. This is anti-violence — the prayer of someone who has decided not to become the thing that attacked them. "Your sword" is God's, not the psalmist's. The legal case is transferred to the divine court for verdict and execution. For those under genuine mortal threat, this form offers a way to name the desire for justice without becoming the perpetrator.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *from these mortals... But your treasured ones*: This verse is what scholars call a "genuine textual catastrophe" — the Hebrew is so damaged that nearly every modern translation differs. The twice-repeated *mimmĕtim* ("from mortals/from the dead") creates an almost untranslatable line. Aperto follows Hossfeld/Zenger and NICOT (Jacobson): the second half of v.14 is NOT a continuation of the curse on enemies, but a blessing on "your treasured ones" — those hidden under God's wings (v.8b). The adversative "But" marks the pivot. This reading is structurally coherent: the psalm moves from imprecation (against enemies) to intercession (for the vulnerable community). The enemies have "their portion in this life only"; God's protected ones receive what God stores up.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *in justice... your form*: Two critical terms close the psalm. — *Bĕṣedeq* ("in justice/righteousness") is the same root as v.1's "just cause" (*ṣedeq*). The word has transformed across the psalm: at v.1 it was the speaker's legal standing; at v.15 it is the ground on which the speaker will see God. The legal case opened a door that leads somewhere beyond the courtroom — to the divine face itself. — *Tĕmûnāh* ("your form") is the word from the image-prohibition texts (Exodus 20:4, Deuteronomy 4:12–25, 5:8). In Numbers 12:8, it denotes what Moses uniquely saw — "the form of the LORD" — described as the highest grade of divine revelation, surpassing even prophetic vision. To end a desperate prayer by claiming this encounter is to name the deepest desire: not vindication but God's presence. The cohortative verb (*ʾeśbĕʿāh* — "let me be filled/satisfied") keeps even this as a plea, not a triumphant declaration. — Both Jewish (Rashi, Midrash Tehillim) and Christian (patristic, Augustine) traditions have read "when I wake" as resurrection language. The Bedtime Shema uses v.8 as nighttime protection prayer. The psalm's night/morning frame — "you came upon me by night" (v.3) and "when I wake" (v.15) — gives the whole text a liturgical arc: darkness to dawn.

## Kapitel 19


### The Heavens and the Instruction

\d For the music director. A psalm of David.[^1]

**1** The heavens are telling the weight of God.[^2]
> The sky shows what his hands have made.

**2** Day pours out speech to day.
> Night reveals knowledge to night.

**3** Without a word.
> Without a sound.
> No voice anyone can hear.

**4** Yet through all the earth their voice goes out,[^3]
> their reach — to the edge of the world.

> God pitched a tent there for the sun.
**5** It comes out like a groom from his wedding night,[^4]
> like a warrior who loves to run his course.

**6** It rises at one end of the sky,
> circles to the other.
> Nothing is hidden from its heat.\f + \fr 19:6 \fk TEXT \fq heat \ft The Hebrew *ḥammato* ("its heat") resonates with the root for "wrath" (*ḥemah*). The double-entendre is deliberate (NICOT): nothing escapes the sun's physical reach — the way nothing escapes divine moral attention. This wordplay prepares for the transition to Torah's moral omniscience in v. 7. The sun's omnipresent heat anticipates the question of v. 12: "Who can see their own errors?" — if nothing is hidden from the sun's heat, neither is anything hidden from God's instruction.\f*

**7** The LORD's instruction:[^5][^6] whole.
> It restores life.

> The LORD's testimony: sure.
> It makes the simple wise.

**8** The LORD's precepts: right.
> They bring joy to the heart.

> The LORD's command: bright.
> It gives light to the eyes.

**9** The LORD's awe: pure.
> It endures forever.

> The LORD's judgments: true.
> Altogether just.

**10** More desirable than gold — than heaps of fine gold.[^7]
> Sweeter than honey — honey dripping from the comb.

**11** Your servant, too, is warned by them.
> In keeping them there is great reward.

**12** But who can see their own errors?[^8]
> Clear me of what I cannot know.

**13** Hold your servant back from the arrogant —[^9]
> don't let them rule over me.
> Then I will be whole,
> clean of great rebellion.

**14** May the words of my mouth
> and the murmur of my heart\f + \fr 19:14a \fk KONTEXT \fq Amidah conclusion \ft This verse (*yihyu l'ratson imrei fi wehegyon libbi lefanekha*) became the universal Jewish conclusion to the silent Amidah prayer — recited three times daily by observant Jews worldwide. No other Psalm verse has had this liturgical life. In context, the psalmist offers the entire psalm as a living sacrifice — "words of mouth" (the whole poem just spoken) and "meditation of heart" (the interior engagement) placed before God. The Hebrew *hegyon* ("murmur/meditation") shares a root with *hagah* in Psalm 1:2, creating an intertextual link between the Psalter's two great Torah psalms.\f*
> be acceptable before you,
> LORD — my rock and my redeemer.\f + \fr 19:14b \fk KULTUR \fq my redeemer \ft The Hebrew *go'eli* is the final word of the psalm — and a deliberate surprise. After cosmic grandeur (heavens, sun, sky) and formal Torah praise (six formulaic lines), the poem closes with a kinship-law term. The *go'el* (Leviticus 25:48–49) is the next-of-kin legally obligated to rescue a family member from debt-slavery — to buy them back when they have lost everything. Calling YHWH *go'eli* is not an abstract title ("my Savior"). It invokes God as the relative who shows up when you've been sold, who takes on your debt because you are family. The cosmic psalm ends with the intimacy of kinship rescue.\f*

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *the sun as non-god*: The ancient world worshipped the sun as a god of law and justice — the Babylonian Shamash was cosmic judge, lawgiver, and protector. Psalm 19 borrows the imagery of solar-deity hymns (bridegroom, warrior, omnipresent heat) but deploys it for a thoroughly non-divine sun: one that has been given a tent — an assigned dwelling — by YHWH. The first movement (vv. 1–6) systematically demotes the sun from deity to creature. The second movement (vv. 7–10) transfers all of Shamash's cosmic-juridical functions to YHWH's instruction. The structural move from solar hymn to Torah hymn IS the argument.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *weight of God*: The Hebrew *kavod* (usually translated "glory") derives from the root *k-b-d*, meaning "heavy, weighty." It describes the visible, felt presence of God — what fills the Temple (Exodus 40:34–35), what Moses asks to see (Exodus 33:18). This is not abstract praise but concrete testimony: the heavens carry evidence of divine substance. The chiastic structure of the Hebrew is deliberate — subject-verb-object in the first line, object-verb-subject in the second — creating a perfect poetic balance between "heavens/glory" and "sky/handiwork."
[^3]: **TEXT** — *their voice*: The Hebrew text reads *qawwam* ("their measuring line") — a textual crux. The Greek Septuagint (third century BCE) reads *phthongos* ("their sound/voice"), likely reflecting a Hebrew original *qolam*. Paul follows the Greek reading in Romans 10:18, applying creation's universal speech to the reach of the gospel. Aperto follows the emended reading ("their voice") with LXX and Vulgate support, as it coheres with the psalm's own speech-paradox: creation communicates without human language — yet its voice reaches everywhere.
[^4]: **KULTUR** — *groom / warrior*: The bridegroom and warrior metaphors are not pastoral — they are deliberate borrowings from Akkadian and Sumerian solar-deity hymns, where the sun-god emerges from his "bed chamber" at dawn with youthful radiance, and is called a "hero who goes out" (*gibbor*). Psalm 19 uses precisely this mythological vocabulary — then strips it of divine status. The sun is magnificent, exultant, powerful — and has a tent assigned to it by someone greater. The mythology is kept at full force; the theology is reversed.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *instruction*: The Hebrew *torah* (from *yarah*, "to direct, instruct") means instruction, teaching, guidance — the whole way of life YHWH gives for human flourishing. It does NOT mean "law" in the sense of legal code or obligation. Translating *torah* as "law" imports the Reformation-era antithesis between law and grace — a framework the Hebrew text does not know. The six synonyms that follow (instruction, testimony, precepts, command, awe, judgments) are all terms of delight and gift, not obligation. The psalm knows nothing of a law/grace binary.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *whole*: The Hebrew *temimah* ("whole/perfect") shares the root *t-m-m* with *tam* ("blameless") in v. 13. This is the structural spine of the psalm: Torah is whole/perfect (v. 7); the servant prays to become whole/blameless (v. 13). The aspiration is clear — the wholeness of God's instruction becomes the standard for the servant's own integrity. The same root describes unblemished sacrificial animals (Leviticus 1:3) — Torah is as pure as what is offered to God.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *gold and honey*: Gold and honey are not random luxury items. Gold was the ancient world's ultimate store of economic value; honey dripping from the comb (*nofet tsufim*) was the sweetest substance available — there was no refined sugar. The psalmist claims Torah surpasses both the world's most coveted currency and its most intense sensory pleasure. Robert Alter notes that gold and honey share a visual link — a golden hue — so the comparison works visually as well as economically. This is not pious exaggeration. It is an economic inversion: what everyone chases is less valuable than what Torah freely offers.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *hidden faults*: The Hebrew *nistarot* ("hidden things") does not describe shameful secrets. It describes the structural impossibility of full moral self-knowledge — actions whose harm one did not intend and whose damage one cannot see. The priestly system distinguished between *shegagah* (unwitting sin, Leviticus 4:2; Numbers 15:22–26) and deliberate transgression. This prayer invokes that distinction: "Cleanse me of what I don't even know I'm doing." The question "Who can see their own errors?" is not rhetorical self-deprecation. It is the honest recognition that one's moral field of vision is limited — that we cause harm in ways we cannot perceive.
[^9]: **TEXT** — *the arrogant*: The Hebrew *zedim* (from *zud*, "to act presumptuously") can refer to internal arrogance OR to external people with social power who dominate others. The grammar supports both readings (NICOT: "On the one hand, a request for protection from false accusers... on the other hand, *zedim* may refer to persons who oppose God and therefore oppose the psalmist"). The verb *yimshelu-bi* ("let them not rule over me") uses *mashal* — the same word for political lordship. The liberation reading — real people with real power threatening to dominate the servant — is grammatically valid and must not be collapsed into "arrogant thoughts."

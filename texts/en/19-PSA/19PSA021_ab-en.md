## Kapitel 21


### The King's Thanksgiving

\d For the director of music. A psalm of David.[^1]

**1** LORD —[^2]
> the king rejoices in your strength.
> In your victory — how deeply.

**2** What his heart wanted, you gave.[^3]
> The longing of his lips —
> you did not hold it back.

**3** You came to him first, carrying blessings.
> You set a crown of fine gold on his head.\f + \fr 21:3 \fk TEXT \fq set \ft The Hebrew verb *tashit* (to set, place, make) appears four times in this psalm — at vv. 3, 6, 9, and 12. This is the poem's most subtle structural device: the same verb that describes God setting a crown on the king's head (v. 3) and setting him as a blessing (v. 6) is the same verb that describes God setting enemies ablaze (v. 9) and setting them as targets (v. 12). What God "sets" as gift becomes what God "sets" as judgment. The symmetry is deliberate.\f*

**4** He asked you for life.[^4]
> You gave him more than life —
> length of days, forever and ever.

**5** Through your victory his glory is great.[^5]
> Splendor and majesty you draped across him.

**6** You set him as a blessing — forever.
> You made him glad with the joy of your face.\f + \fr 21:6 \fk TEXT \fq the joy of your face \ft The Hebrew *et-panekha* means literally "your face" or "your presence." The same root (*paneh*) appears again at v. 12 — but with the opposite effect. Here, God's face is the source of gladness and joy for the king. In v. 12, bows are aimed at enemy *faces*. The same divine presence that comforts the faithful terrifies those who resist. This is a deliberate poetic contrast running through the psalm.\f*

**7** The king trusts in the LORD.[^6]
> Through the steadfast love of the Most High,
> he will not be shaken.

**8** Your hand will find every enemy.[^7]
> Your right hand will reach those who hate you.

**9** When you appear —[^8]
> you set them blazing like a furnace.
> The LORD swallows them whole in his anger.
> Fire eats them.

**10** Their offspring you destroy from the earth.[^9]
> Their descendants — gone from among the living.

**11** They aimed evil at you.
> They made their plans.
> They will not succeed.

**12** You set them running —[^10]
> bowstrings drawn at their faces.

**13** Rise up, LORD, in your strength.[^11]
> We will sing —
> we will make music to your might.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *Psalms 20–21 diptych*: Psalm 21 is the community's answer to Psalm 20. In Psalm 20, the people petitioned Yahweh on behalf of the king before military crisis: "May Yahweh answer you in the day of trouble." Psalm 21 celebrates the answer. The two psalms form a liturgical petition-thanksgiving diptych — call and response across two separate compositions. What was asked for (Ps 20:4: "May he give you what your heart desires") is now celebrated as given (Ps 21:2: "You gave him what his heart desired"). Reading Psalm 21 without Psalm 20 is hearing the answer without the question.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *strength*: The Hebrew word *oz* (strength, might) opens and closes this psalm (vv. 1 and 13) — forming an architectural frame called an inclusio. Everything the psalm celebrates — the king's gifts, the enemies' defeat, the community's praise — is bracketed by Yahweh's active, exercised strength. The word is not abstract "power" but dynamic force, the kind that does things in the world. The second line escalates the first: not merely "rejoices" but "how deeply he celebrates" — the Hebrew *ma-yagel meod* is a crescendo, stunned gratitude.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *the longing of his lips*: The Hebrew *areshet* is a hapax legomenon — a word that appears only once in the entire Hebrew Bible. Its cognate in Ugaritic carries overtones of betrothal and deep desire. It is not a bureaucratic "request" or a polite "petition." It is the intimate, barely-spoken longing — what a person dares to voice aloud before God. The first colon uses a common word for desire (*mish'alat*); the second escalates to this rare, warmer word. The two cola say the same fact from two sides: "you gave" / "you did not hold back."
[^4]: **TEXT** — *length of days, forever and ever*: "Length of days forever and ever" is an ancient Near Eastern royal formula for perpetual dynasty — comparable to "Long may she reign" or the Akkadian *ana dariti* (for eternity). It appears in Ugaritic royal poetry and Mesopotamian inscriptions. The request is for an uncurtailed reign and lasting dynasty, not literal immortality — though later Christian interpreters (Augustine, Calvin) read the verse as pointing toward Christ's resurrection. Both registers are live in the reception history: original dynastic prayer and later eschatological reading.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *splendor and majesty*: The Hebrew *hod wehadar* (splendor and majesty) corresponds to the Akkadian concept of *melammu* — the luminous divine radiance that surrounded kings in the ancient Near East, signifying their divine legitimation. In Mesopotamian art, kings were depicted with an aura of light. Psalm 21 takes this concept and rewrites it: the king's radiance is not inherent but explicitly *bestowed* by Yahweh. "You lay them upon him" — the laying is the point. Yahweh loans the radiance; what Yahweh gives, Yahweh can take away. This is Israel rewriting royal ideology from the ground up.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *trusts / steadfast love / not shaken*: This is the theological center of the psalm — the only verse not in direct address, the only verse in the third person. It interrupts the flow of praise to declare the *why* beneath everything: the king's stability rests on trust (*boteah*, a present participle indicating ongoing posture, not a past act) grounded in Yahweh's *hesed* (covenant loyalty, steadfast love — the single most freight-bearing word in the Hebrew Bible). The two cola are not sequential (trust produces stability) but parallel: trust *is* stability because *hesed* is the ground. Hossfeld and Zenger argue this verse may be a later editorial insertion that transformed the psalm from royal ideology into a theological paradigm for every faithful person.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *the addressee in vv. 8–12*: The identity of "you" in vv. 8–12 is the psalm's greatest interpretive crux. Three positions exist: (1) "you" is Yahweh throughout (Goldingay, Dahood, Alter) — fire, presence, and right hand are consistently divine attributes; (2) "you" is the king (Mays, NICOT) — an oracle of assurance; (3) the text shifts mid-verse (Codex Leningrad vs. Codex Aleppo read v. 9 differently). This translation follows the Yahweh-throughout reading: the enemies are fundamentally God's enemies; the victory belongs to God; the closing doxology (v. 13) addresses God, not the king.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *fire*: The furnace and fire imagery here is theophanic — it belongs to the pattern of God's self-disclosure throughout the Hebrew Bible (Exodus 3: burning bush; Exodus 19: Sinai in smoke and fire; Daniel 3: fiery furnace). "The LORD swallows them" is visceral: the Hebrew verb *bala'* means to gulp, to ingest — it is physical, not metaphorical. The pairing of wrath and fire is not vindictive emotion but the incompatibility of divine holiness with unchecked violence.
[^9]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *destroy their offspring*: This is the ethical crux of Psalm 21. The verse uses standard ancient Near Eastern victory rhetoric for decisive generational defeat — compare Pharaoh Merneptah's stele (ca. 1208 BCE): "Israel is laid waste, his seed no longer exists" (Israel clearly survived). The idiom was hyperbolic. But the words mean what they say, and the reader who rushes to the apologetic (it is just convention) is protecting the text from honest encounter. International law scholar Richard Falk argues the verse "nurtures morally problematic impulses" when applied uncritically to contemporary conflicts. Both readings must be held: the ANE convention is real, AND the violence named here is real. The psalm does not apologize for what it celebrates. The question it raises — can trust in divine justice replace the human appetite for revenge? — is the ethical work it demands.
[^10]: **TEXT** — *set them running / bowstrings at their faces*: The Hebrew *teshitem shekhem* means literally "you make them turn their shoulder/back" — the posture of flight. This is the fourth and final use of the verb *tashit* (set/place) in the psalm. The verb's arc across the poem: God sets a crown (v. 3), sets the king as blessing (v. 6), sets enemies ablaze (v. 9), sets enemies fleeing (v. 12). What God "sets" as gift becomes what God "sets" as judgment — the psalm's deepest structural symmetry.
[^11]: **TEXT** — *in your strength / inclusio*: The psalm's final word returns to its first: *oz* (strength). The same phrase — "in your strength" — opens (v. 1) and closes (v. 13) the poem. This is the inclusio: everything between these matching bookends belongs to Yahweh's active power. The king's joy (v. 1), his gifts, the enemies' defeat, and now the community's song (v. 13) are all contained within Yahweh's strength — they do not originate elsewhere. The shift to first-person plural ("we will sing") is the final structural move: the community steps into the poem for the first time, joining what was previously about the king into corporate praise.

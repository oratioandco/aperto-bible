## Kapitel 22

\d For the lead player. To the tune of "The Doe of the Dawn." A psalm of David.[^1]

### My God, My God


**1** My God. My God.
> Why have you forsaken me\add ?\add*\f + \fr 22:1 \fk TEXT \fq forsaken me \ft The Hebrew *ʿāzab* is not a word for mild absence. It is the word for covenantal desertion — the same verb used when Deuteronomy warns Israel that God will never "forsake" them (Deut 31:6). The speaker is accusing God of breaking the fundamental relationship. "My God" and "forsaken" do not fit in one sentence — that contradiction drives the whole psalm. The doubled "My God, My God" is without parallel in the Psalter; it enacts the stutter of shock, the name repeated because the silence is incomprehensible.\f*

> Far from my rescue —
> the words of my roaring.\f + \fr 22:1 \fk KULTUR \fq roaring \ft The Hebrew *šĕʾāgāh* is the word for a lion's roar (Isa 5:29, Amos 3:8). It is applied here to human anguish — the prayer is not polite petition but animal-level sound. The same root reappears in v.14 for the enemies' lion-roar: the speaker's cry of pain uses the same word as the predators' threat. The sufferer has been absorbed into the violence.\f*

**2** I call out by day ��� you do not answer.
> By night — and there is no rest for me.

### The Ancestors Trusted


**3** But you — you are the holy one,
> enthroned on Israel's praise.\f + \fr 22:3 \fk KULTUR \fq enthroned on Israel's praise \ft This is not comfort — it is the knife that makes the abandonment worse. God IS the holy one. God IS enthroned. God's throne is literally constituted by Israel's praise — the one with the power to rescue sits unmoved in majesty. The ancestors who trusted (vv.4–5) were delivered; this speaker is not. The praise that constitutes God's throne is the same praise the speaker cannot yet offer.\f*

**4** Our ancestors trusted you.
> They trusted, and you rescued them.
**5** They cried to you and were delivered.
> They trusted you and were not shamed.

### A Worm


**6** I am a worm. Not a man.[^2]
> Disgraced. Despised.

**7** All who see me sneer.
> They twist their mouths and shake their heads —
**8** "He threw himself on the LORD? Let the LORD save him then.
> Let God pull him out — if God wants him."

### You Drew Me Out


**9** But it was you who pulled me from the womb,[^3]
> who kept me safe at my mother's breast.
**10** I was cast on you before I was born.
> From my mother's belly — you were my God.

**11** Don't be far.
> Trouble is near
> and no one will help.

### Bulls, Lions, Dogs


**12** Many bulls encircle me —
> Bashan's bulls surround me,\f + \fr 22:12 \fk KULTUR \fq Bashan's bulls \ft Bashan was the most fertile cattle territory in the ancient Near East — east of the Jordan, famous for powerful, large livestock (Amos 4:1; Deut 32:14). "Bulls of Bashan" is not a rural metaphor — it names concentrated power. In Ugaritic mythology, the bull was an epithet of El, the high god. Being surrounded by "Bashan's bulls" means being enclosed by forces that crush without effort — the ancient equivalent of facing the full machinery of institutional power with nothing.\f*
**13** mouths torn open,
> lions ravening and roaring.\f + \fr 22:13 \fk KONTEXT \fq roaring \ft The Hebrew uses the same root (*šāʾag*) here as in v.1 — the speaker's own "roaring" cry of anguish and the predators' roaring threat are the same word. The sufferer's sound and the enemy's sound have merged.\f*

**14** Like water I spill out.[^4]
> Every bone pulls apart from its neighbor.
> My heart — wax
> melting in my chest.

**15** My palate dries to a shard of clay.
> My tongue sticks to my jaw.
> You set me down in death's dust.

**16** Dogs surround me —
> a gang of evildoers hems me in.
> They pierce my hands and my feet.\f + \fr 22:16 \fk TEXT \fq pierce my hands and my feet \ft This is the most disputed word in the Hebrew Bible. The received Hebrew text (MT) reads *ka'ari* — "like a lion, my hands and my feet" — which is grammatically incomplete (no main verb). Ancient manuscripts including a Dead Sea area scroll from Nahal Hever (5/6HevPs, dated ca. 50 BCE–68 CE, pre-dating the Jewish-Christian polemic over this verse) read *ka'ru* — "they pierced/dug my hands and my feet." The difference is a single letter: yod vs. waw, frequently confused in ancient script. This translation follows the Nahal Hever reading, which resolves the MT's grammatical problem and is the oldest surviving Hebrew witness to this verse. — This verse has been used extensively in Christian anti-Jewish polemic since the 2nd century (Justin Martyr, Tertullian). The Jewish scholar Naomi Koltun-Fromm documents how this polemic forced Jewish interpreters to abandon their own prior messianic readings of Psalm 22. The textual debate is real and ancient; its weaponization against Jewish communities is also real and must be named.\f*
**17** I can count every bone.

> They look. They stare.
**18** They split my clothing between them,
> throw dice for what I wore.

### Save My Life


**19** But you, LORD — do not be far.
> My strength — come quickly.
**20** Save my life from the sword,
> my only life from the power of these dogs.
**21** Rescue me from the mouth of the lion —

### You Answered Me


> You answered me.\f + \fr 22:21 \fk TEXT \fq You answered me \ft A single Hebrew word — *ʿănîṯānî* — pivots the entire psalm from petition to completed rescue, with no narrative explanation of what happened. After 21 verses of escalating desperation, everything changes in one word. The Masoretic Text preserves this as the harder reading (lectio difficilior) — the abruptness is intentional, not a scribal error. Walter Brueggemann: "Something happened in the unspoken space of the poem between petition and praise." The rescue is not explained, only announced. The ancient Greek translation (LXX) missed this turn entirely, reading "from the horns of lions" instead — a harmonizing reading that eliminated the theological shock.\f*

**22** I will tell your name to my people.
> In the middle of the congregation, I will praise you:

**23** All you who live in awe of God — praise him.
> Every child of Jacob — give him honor.
> Every child of Israel — stand in awe.

**24** Because he did not despise
> the affliction of the afflicted one.
> He did not turn his face away.
> When the poor one cried — he heard.\f + \fr 22:24 \fk KONTEXT \fq he did not despise the affliction \ft This is the theological center of the hymn section — the announcement that reverses everything in the first 21 verses. The Hebrew *ʿanî* (afflicted/poor one) is the Psalter's technical term for the archetypal righteous poor person who relies entirely on God. The announcement is not "God explained the suffering" or "God was right to be silent." It is simply: "He did not despise. He did not hide his face. When the cry came — he heard." The silence is not explained. The relationship is restored.\f*

**25** From you comes my praise in the great assembly.
> I will keep my promises before those who fear you.
**26** The poor will eat until they are full.[^5]
> Those who seek the LORD will praise him —
> may your hearts live forever.

### All the Earth


**27** All the ends of the earth
> will remember and return to the LORD.
> Every family of every nation
> will bow down before you.

**28** For kingship belongs to the LORD.
> He governs the nations.

**29** All who feast on the earth will bow.[^6]
> All who sink into the dust will kneel —
> even those who cannot keep their own souls alive.

**30** Their children will serve him.
> The coming generation will be told of the Lord.
**31** They will come and proclaim his saving work
> to a people not yet born:
> he has done it.\f + \fr 22:31 \fk KONTEXT \fq he has done it \ft The Hebrew *kî ʿāśāh* — "for he has done [it]" — is deliberately unspecified. The deed needs no elaboration because its scope is everything. The psalm that began with one person's scream of abandonment ends with a proclamation reaching unborn generations. The individual sufferer's rescue has become the content of universal testimony. The psalm does not end at "I am well again" but at "a people not yet born will hear what God has done." This is the Psalter's characteristic claim: that the particular becomes paradigmatic, that one person's survival tells the story of everything.\f*

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *The Doe of the Dawn*: The Hebrew phrase *ayeleth hashahar* is almost certainly a musical direction — the name of a known melody or chant style to which this psalm was performed (Ibn Ezra, deClaissé-Walford). The Talmud (B. Yoma 29a) connects it to Esther: "Just as the dawn is the end of the night, so Esther is the end of all miracles." Jewish communities pray this psalm every Purim — both evening and morning services — as their annual cry of existential abandonment and unexpected rescue. This is the psalm's first liturgical home, before it became associated with Good Friday in Christian tradition. A possible wordplay: the Hebrew word for "strength/help" in v.20 (*eyalutî*) may echo the "doe" (*ayeleth*) of the title.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *worm*: The Hebrew *tôlaʿat* names a specific creature: the crimson grub (*coccus ilicis*) used in ancient textile dyeing. When crushed, it produces scarlet dye — a luxury material. The self-abasement is total ("not a human being" — expelled from the category of person), but it may carry a shadow of costly transformation: the very creature whose destruction produces something of value. In an honor-shame culture, being "despised" (v.6b) is not private humiliation but formal exclusion from community — social death.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *pulled me from the womb*: The Hebrew *gōḥî mibbeṭen* uses the language of midwifery — God as the one who draws the newborn into the world. This is one of the clearest female divine images in the entire Psalter. The feminist theologian L. Juliana M. Claassens argues that the psalm reaches for a female image of God precisely at the moment of maximum abandonment: "you were present when I had no words, when I was most vulnerable." In the moment when everything else fails, the speaker reaches for the body's oldest memory of being held. The theological counterweight to v.1: "you forsook me" / "you were there before I could speak."
[^4]: **KONTEXT** — *the somatic cascade*: Verses 14–16 contain the most extreme bodily language in the Psalter. Twenty body-part references appear in this psalm's 31 verses. The three similes cascade in escalating intensity: liquid dissolution (water, v.14a) → structural collapse (bones disjointing, v.14b) → internal melting (heart as wax, v.14c) → desiccation (palate as shard, tongue stuck, v.15) → burial (dust of death, v.15b). The trauma scholar Danilo Verde argues these images function as somatic memory — "not only to heal but also to wound the community," preserving bodily suffering across generations so it would not be forgotten.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *the poor will eat*: This is not metaphor. The *tôdāh* (thanksgiving offering) was a formal Temple ritual: the rescued person appeared before the congregation, offered a sacrifice, and provided a communal meal for the economically poor. The "poor" (*ʿănāwîm*) are those who literally cannot feed themselves. Rescue becomes redistribution — the one person's deliverance feeds the community. Ancient liturgy had economic justice built into its structure.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *vv.29–31*: These closing verses contain some of the most difficult Hebrew in the entire Psalter. Robert Alter calls them "a word salad tossed by a bewildered scribe." The translation follows scholarly consensus on the general sense: the scope of praise expands to include even the dying and the dead ("all who sink into the dust," "those who cannot keep their own souls alive"), and finally reaches across time to "a people not yet born." Any translation of these verses is tentative; the general direction — from one person's rescue to universal, trans-temporal proclamation — is clear even where individual words are opaque.

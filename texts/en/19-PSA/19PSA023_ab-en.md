## Kapitel 23

\d A psalm of David.[^1]

### The Shepherd

**1** The Eternal[^2][^3] is my shepherd,*the king who stands up for me himself.*
> I lack nothing.\f + \fr 23:1 \fk KULTUR \fq the psalm you may have heard at funerals \ft In English-speaking culture Psalm 23 has become a funeral poem. That is a recent development. In its original Jewish home the psalm belongs to the Sabbath table — it is sung or recited at the *seudah shlishit*, the third Sabbath meal, as the holy day ends. The "table prepared before me" (v.5) is the Sabbath table; "the LORD's house" (v.6) is the lingering holiness of the Sabbath. The Christian funeral association began in 19th-century English-speaking Protestantism and solidified in 20th-century mortuary culture through hymn-settings like *Crimond*. If you only know this psalm from graveside, you are meeting a late version of it. Its first life was inside a home, at a meal, with family.\f*\f + \fr 23:1 \fk LEBEN \fq I lack nothing \ft This is one of the hardest lines in the psalm to say honestly. The Hebrew lōʾ ʾeḥsār is three syllables, no qualifiers: I lack nothing. Not I lack less than I used to. Not I will eventually lack nothing. Absolute, present tense. For anyone whose life is organized around the next purchase, the next promotion, the next reassurance from a partner that things are okay — this is a foreign sentence. — Most contemporary readers, asked to be honest, would say the line is not true for them. They lack money, or recognition, or the relationship they hoped for, or simply the feeling of having arrived. The psalm does not argue with them. It does something quieter: it changes who is speaking. The first line is not the boast of a comfortable person. By verse 4 the speaker has walked through the valley of deep shadow. The I lack nothing is said from the far side of real trouble. It is the small, tough thing a person can say only after losing some of what they thought they could not live without — and discovering that they did. — A small practice for the week, if you want one: at the end of each day, name three things you actually had today that you could not have produced for yourself — daylight, a particular person, an unexpected gesture. Not gratitude as performance; just naming. Over a month the muscle starts to work, and the line stops sounding like a sentiment from a card and starts sounding like reportage. — The psalm has been carried across history by people whose surface circumstances made the line look impossible. Enslaved people on plantations sang it. Bonhoeffer prayed it in the Tegel cell. Hospice patients have spoken it in the last hour. The line is not a description of a comfortable life. It is a posture learned inside the valley, by people who walked further into trouble than most of us have and came out saying — somehow, against the visible evidence — that the shepherd was enough.\f*

**2** He lays me down where it's green.
> He leads me to quiet water,\add  the place I'm finally allowed to land.\add*\f + \fr 23:2 \fk KULTUR \fq quiet water \ft The Hebrew phrase is *ʿal-mê mənuḥôt* — "beside waters of rest." *Mənûḥâ* is the theological word for the rest of Sabbath (Exod 20:11) and the rest of the promised land (Deut 12:9). "Still waters," the older English rendering, suggests a picture of motionless ponds. The point is not the water's motion but the sheep's security: in a desert landscape, water is the end of a journey, and the shepherd has brought the flock there safely. Rest, in Hebrew, is a destination.\f*
**3** He pulls me back from the edge, back into my life, and sets my feet on the right road,*because he is who he is.*[^4][^5]

**4** Even through the darkest valley:[^6]
> I'm not afraid.

> you are with me.\f + \fr 23:4 \fk KONTEXT \fq you are with me \ft At exactly this point the psalm's grammar shifts. For three verses the speaker has been describing what God does — "he lays me down, he leads me, he pulls me back from the edge, he sets my feet on the right road." Now, in the valley, the speaker turns and speaks to God directly: "you are with me." This shift is the theological heart of the psalm. The Hebrew-poetry scholars Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger describe the structure as an A-B-C-B'-A' chiasm with this verse at the center. Goldingay sees the same pattern on the grammar axis (third person → second person → second person → third person). In the valley, God is no longer talked about. God is present.\f*\f + \fr 23:4 \fk UNGLAUBLICH \fq you are with me \ft This is the verse where modern readers most often stall: what about when God does not seem to show up? People walk the valley and do not come back from it. Children die. Illness wins. The line can sound, to a reader who has been near real loss, like wishful thinking from the lucky.
The Christian tradition has not heard the verse that way. Across centuries — Augustine in his pastoral letters, the Black church singing through forced migration, Dietrich Bonhoeffer praying the psalm in the Tegel cell weeks before his execution, hospice chaplains kneeling at the dying — the people who carried "you are with me" hardest were not the comfortable. They were the ones the valley took most from. What they testify is not that the valley got removed. It is that someone was inside it with them. The verb in Hebrew is the simple verb of presence, and the grammar shifts at exactly this word from talking *about* God to speaking *to* God — the psalm pivots into the second person *in* the valley, not out of it. That is the believing reading: God's nearness is not proven by outcomes; it is met as company, in the dark, by the one who keeps walking.
For the reader who cannot meet the verse as a metaphysical claim, the line still does something true. It names a real human posture — the moment, in genuine trouble, when the speaker stops describing their situation and starts addressing someone. Even if no one is there, the address itself reorganizes the speaker. Psychotherapists have called this externalization; contemplative teachers have called it presence-practice. Something in the human animal needs to turn outward in the valley.
And for the reader who needs the skeptical scholarly frame: Hebrew Bible critics from Hermann Gunkel onward read Psalm 23 as a psalm of confidence (Vertrauenspsalm) — a genre. It does not promise that the valley will be removed. It models a way of speaking from inside the valley that has carried generations of vulnerable people. The Hebrew Psalter sets Psalm 88 — the unrelieved lament that ends in darkness, not light — directly nearby; the two texts are designed to be read together. Whatever you make of the supernatural, the psalm's literary architecture is unsentimental about how dark the valley actually gets.
What the psalm finally invites — across all three readings — is to risk the address. Not to settle the question first, but to speak the sentence and see what answers. The reader who waits for proof before saying "you are with me" waits forever. The reader who says it inside the trouble is the one this psalm has always belonged to.
> your club keeps the wolves off,\f + \fr 23:4 \fk KULTUR \fq your club keeps the wolves off \ft The Hebrew word for the first object is *šēbeṭ* — the shepherd's weapon. It is not a gentle crook. It is a wooden club, worn at the belt, used to kill predators — the same word that describes the weapon with which God's anointed king breaks nations in Psalm 2:9. David, defending his father's sheep against lions and bears, used one of these (1 Sam 17:34–36). The staff (*mišʿenet*) is the long cane the shepherd leans on and uses to guide the flock. Two distinct objects: one for defense, one for support. The psalm is not describing a sentimental rural scene. The shepherd is armed because the valley is dangerous.\f* your staff keeps me walking.

**5** You spread a table for me, in front of my enemies, right where they have to watch.[^7] *The seat of honor.* Oil in my hair,[^8] the cup filled past its rim.[^9]\f + \fr 23:5 \fk HARM \fq the cup filled past its rim \ft "My cup runs over" has been pulled out of this verse and made into a slogan for the American prosperity gospel — the movement that promises wealth, health, and success to the faithful in return for their faith. The historian Kate Bowler, in *Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel* (Oxford, 2013), traces the weaponization line by line: the phrase is lifted from its setting, detached from the enemies standing at the edge of the table, and recast as a promise of financial abundance.
The line does not mean that. The cup is excessive because ancient Near Eastern hospitality was excessive when a host wanted to honor a guest: the oil on the head is too much, the drink in the cup is more than the guest can finish, the point is public welcome. And the welcome in this verse is *in full view of my enemies*. Read in its original context — the banquet scene documented in the 14th-century-BCE El Amarna letters, where a minor ruler asks Pharaoh to honor him "while our enemies look on" — "the cup filled past its rim" is the psalm's witness that God honors the vulnerable publicly, with the adversaries watching and unable to intervene. The image is closer to a protected-witness meal than to anything on a televangelist's stage. *You call me yours. No enemy lays a hand on me.*

**6** Your goodness and faithful love will hunt me down, all my life.[^10][^11][^12][^13]
> And your house,\add  this is home.\add*\f + \fr 23:6 \fk TEXT \fq your house, this is home \ft The Hebrew word *wəšabtî* is ambiguous: it can be read as "I will return" (from the verb *šûb*) or "I will dwell" (from a different root, *yāšab*, requiring repointed vowels). The traditional Hebrew text preserves "I will return." Scholars have noticed that Psalm 23 sits in the middle of a three-psalm unit: Psalm 22 (abandonment), Psalm 23 (trust in the valley), Psalm 24 (entry into the Temple). Read together, the three psalms form a pilgrim's progression — the one who cried out in abandonment is brought through the valley and returns, again and again, to the house of God. "I'll come back" preserves this pilgrim-motion.\f*

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *a psalm of David*: The superscription reads *mizmôr lə-dāwid* in Hebrew. The *lamed* ("of/to/for") is grammatically open — it can mean "composed by David," "dedicated to David," "in the tradition of David," or "from the David collection." Modern scholarship generally treats Davidic ascriptions as collection-designations rather than authorship claims. The psalm's voice is timeless enough that any person in any century can step inside it.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *The Eternal*: The Hebrew name is *YHWH* — the personal name of God in the Hebrew Bible. By ancient Jewish custom the name is not spoken; English Bibles have traditionally printed it "LORD" in small capitals, but that surrogate reads as a churchly title. Aperto renders it *the Eternal* (Moffatt; the French *l'Éternel*), so it stays a name. In the ancient Near East, "shepherd" was not a pastoral metaphor — it was the standard title for kings. Hammurabi of Babylon called himself "the shepherd" in the opening of his law code (c. 1750 BCE). Egyptian pharaohs bore the shepherd's crook as royal insignia. When this psalm says "the Eternal is my shepherd," it is making a political claim: no human king is my king. God is.
[^3]: **KONTEXT** — *my shepherd*: The traditional English Bible stacks masculine pronouns on God across this psalm — "he leads me, he makes me, he restores me." The Hebrew does not force this. The personal name YHWH carries no grammatical gender, and the verb endings that English renders as "he" are unmarked in Hebrew, not gendered assertions. Wilda C. Gafney, a womanist Hebrew Bible scholar, translates the psalm in *A Women's Lectionary for the Whole Church* (2021) with feminine pronouns — "She makes me lie down in green pastures" — so that women praying the psalm out loud can hear feminine God language. Phyllis Trible earlier noted that the Hebrew verbs for "leading" and "restoring" are the same stems used elsewhere of midwives and nursing mothers. The German feminist translation *Bibel in gerechter Sprache* (2006) reads the shepherd as the one who nurses and carries the lamb. Psalm 23 does not require a male God. It requires a God who is present.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *pulls me back from the edge*: The Hebrew is *napšî yəšôbēb*, "he brings my life back." The verb *yəšôbēb* is the polel of *šûb* — it is resuscitation vocabulary, the word you use for someone who was collapsing. And *nepeš* is not a disembodied "soul"; it is the whole person — breath, throat, body, self. Older English Bibles translate this "he restoreth my soul," but the Hebrew is more urgent and more physical. The psalm is not describing a gentle refreshment. It is describing someone brought back from the brink.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *the right road*: The Hebrew is *maʿgəlê-ṣedeq*, "tracks of rightness." *Maʿgal* means wheel-rut, a physical path worn into the ground by use. *Ṣedeq* is not personal virtue but covenant-justice — the rightness owed to the weak, the integrity of a relationship. Older English Bibles render this "paths of righteousness," which has drained the concreteness out of the image. The psalm pictures God as a shepherd holding the sheep to well-worn tracks that lead somewhere right. — "Because he is who he is" renders the Hebrew idiom *ləmaʿan šəmô*, "for his name's sake": in the ancient world a name was a reputation, a track record. God sets your feet on the right road because that is what faithfulness looks like for him.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *the darkest valley*: The Hebrew word is *ṣalmāwet*, and its meaning is debated. Modern critical scholarship reads it as *ṣalmût*, "deep darkness," from a root meaning "to be dark" (compare Arabic *ẓalām*). An older reading divides the word as *ṣēl-māwet*, "shadow of death" — the reading the ancient Greek translation chose, which became the "valley of the shadow of death" in English Bibles for four centuries. Both readings are defensible. Most modern translators prefer "darkest valley" as closer to the underlying text. The Black church tradition in America read "shadow of death" through lynching and forced migration and found the psalm still held: Howard Thurman wrote of how the spirituals used this verse to walk through real valleys, not symbolic ones.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *right where they have to watch*: The Hebrew *neged ṣōrəray* means directly, confrontationally, "in the face of" my adversaries. The enemies are present and watching. This matches a specific ancient Near Eastern trope: in the El Amarna letters (14th c. BCE), a minor ruler asks Pharaoh to give him gifts "while our enemies look on." Honor in the ancient world required public display: God vindicates the speaker not secretly but publicly, with the adversaries watching and unable to intervene. The enemies are never backgrounded in this psalm. They are at the table's edge the whole time.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *Oil in my hair*: The Hebrew verb is *diššantā*, from *dāšēn*, "to make luxuriant, to make fat." This is not the ritual-anointing verb *māšaḥ* that describes a priest or king being consecrated (the verb from which *māšîaḥ*/Messiah comes). This is banquet-hospitality language. In the ancient Near East, a host welcomed an honored guest by pouring aromatic oil on their head until it ran down. New Kingdom Egyptian tomb paintings show guests at banquets with perfumed cones on their heads. In Luke 7:46, Jesus is publicly shamed for not being anointed with oil as a guest — Psalm 23 pictures God as the host who refuses to stint. The oil is excessive. The welcome is total.
[^9]: **LEBEN** — *right where they have to watch*: The picture is precise and almost shocking when noticed: a table set for the speaker, in the open, with the people who want to harm them standing right there watching. Not a fortress. Not a hideout. A table in plain sight, with the enemies still there. This is not a psalm that smooths over pain. — Kate Bowler, the historian of American prosperity gospel who was diagnosed with stage-IV colon cancer at thirty-five, has written about the way faith traditions sometimes weaponize Psalm 23 to paper over what is actually happening to a suffering person. The chaplain reads it at the bedside to settle people down. The funeral uses it to round off the grief too quickly. The psalm itself does the opposite. It does not erase the enemies — it puts them at the table. It does not pretend the valley was not real — it walks through it. The comfort it offers is not anesthesia. It is company. — A reading practice: read the psalm slowly, twice, and notice each time where the trouble actually goes. The valley is not bypassed; it is walked through. The enemies are not removed; they watch the meal. The oil is not magic; it is the host's attention to the guest. Then ask: what in my life would change if I let comfort come this way — not as removal of the trouble, but as presence inside it? — The communal weight of this verse is large. African-American spirituals carried it through generations of violence in which the enemies were entirely real. Liturgies of the dying carry it through the last hour. Hospice chaplains know that the verse does its work precisely because it does not lie about what is in the room. The God of this psalm sets the table in the open. The trouble is allowed to keep watching. And the host stays anyway. — For the reader who has been told too many times that faith means pretending the pain is fine: this verse is permission to stop pretending.
[^10]: **KULTUR** — *faithful love*: The Hebrew word is *ḥesed*, one of the hardest words in the Bible to translate. It is not mere kindness, and it is not "mercy" (which is a different Hebrew word, *raḥămîm*). *Ḥesed* is the loyalty one party keeps to another after they have entered into covenant — a sustained, unwillingness-to-let-go love that continues even when the other party has failed. William Tyndale coined "lovingkindness" for it in 1535. Contemporary scholarship settled on "steadfast love" as the closest English rendering. In this psalm, *ḥesed* is paired with *ṭôb* (goodness) as a hendiadys — the two words together mean something like "everything good in God's unshakeable character."
[^11]: **TEXT** — *will hunt me down*: The Hebrew verb is *rādap*, which appears about 144 times in the Hebrew Bible. It is a hunt-verb. Enemies pursue with it. Saul hunts David with it (1 Sam 23:25; 26:18). Armies chase the defeated with it. This is the only place in the Hebrew Bible where *rādap* has "goodness and steadfast love" as its subject. The psalm's final move is a theological reversal: the verb that usually describes enemies hunting the speaker is now describing what God's goodness does. Older English versions soften this to "shall follow me," which destroys the force of the image. Goodness does not follow. It hunts, it chases, it refuses to let you go.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *all my life*: The Hebrew is *lə-ʾōrek yāmîm*, literally "for length of days." It is an idiom for an earthly lifespan, not eternity. Hebrew has a different phrase for "forever" (*lə-ʿôlām*); it is not used here. The King James Bible and many English versions after it translate this "forever," which imports a later Christian doctrinal picture of heaven onto a psalm that is about *this* life — the days the speaker actually has, one after another. The psalm's horizon is a full human life in the presence of God. Not heaven. Not later. Now, and all the way through.
[^13]: **LEBEN** — *hunt me down... all my life*: The verb in Hebrew is rādap — to pursue, to chase, the same verb used elsewhere in the Psalter for armies hunting fugitives. Most English translations soften it to follow. The psalm is doing something stranger: it takes the language of being hunted and uses it for goodness and mercy. The reader who has actually been chased — by debt collectors, by an abusive partner who would not stop, by a disease moving through the body, by immigration enforcement — hears this verse differently than the reader who has only had it read at a funeral. — In The Spirituals and the Blues, the theologian James Cone traced how African-American Christian tradition heard the psalm's valley as the real valley of American violence — forced migration, lynching, the long road home. The spirituals did not treat Psalm 23 as chaplaincy-at-the-deathbed. They treated it as a walking song for people being hunted. Hunt me down lands differently when you have been actually chased. The reading is not metaphorical for them; it is literal, and the reversal — that what is chasing is no longer the slave-catcher but the goodness of God — is what makes the song sing. — A quiet experiment: read the verse this week with the strong verb in place. Goodness and mercy will hunt me down all the days of my life. Notice what changes. The grace of the psalm is no longer waiting passively in some future heaven for you to arrive. It is in motion now, behind you, gaining on you, regardless of whether you are running toward it or away. — That reframe matters for anyone who grew up being told that grace was something they had to earn or qualify for. The verb in Hebrew does not give that option. Grace is the pursuer. The person is the pursued. — And the verse closes with the resting place: šaḇtî — I will return, I will sit down. The chase ends at a table that has been waiting the whole time. Not a lullaby. A walking song for people on a long road, with the news that the road has a destination, and someone is already there.

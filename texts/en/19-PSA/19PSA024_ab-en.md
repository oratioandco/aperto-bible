## Kapitel 24

\d Of David. A psalm.

### The Earth Is the LORD's


**1** The earth is the LORD's —[^1]
> and everything in it.
> The world and all who live here.

**2** He planted it on the seas,[^2]
> set it firm over the currents.

### Who Can Stand Here


**3** Who can go up the LORD's mountain?
> Who can stand in his holy place?

**4** Clean hands.[^3]
> Uncorrupted heart.
> Someone who does not give themselves to what is false,
> who has not sworn a lying oath.

**5** They will carry away a blessing from the LORD,[^4]
> vindication from the God who saves them.

**6** This is the company of those who seek him —[^5]
> those who search for your face, Jacob.

### The King of Glory


**7** Lift your heads, you gates.[^6]
> Rise up, you ancient doors.
> The King of Glory is coming in.

**8** Who is this King of Glory?[^7]
> The LORD — fierce and mighty,
> the LORD — the warrior of battles.

**9** Lift your heads, you gates.
> Rise high, you ancient doors.
> The King of Glory is coming in.

**10** Who is he — this King of Glory?[^8]
> The LORD of armies —
> he is the King of Glory.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *The earth is the LORD's*: This opening line is not devotional praise. It is a legal claim — a declaration of cosmic ownership that strips every empire, every corporation, every king of their pretension to absolute ownership. In the ancient world, this was polemic: against Marduk who "owned" Babylon, against Pharaoh who "owned" Egypt, against any local deity's territorial claim. The sentence says: if God owns everything, no human does absolutely. You are tenants, not owners. — This verse has been misused in settler-colonial theology to sanction land seizure — "God's people" as proxies for divine ownership. The psalm says the opposite. It denies any human absolute claim. The earth belongs to God, which means it belongs to no empire. — The psalm is the Sunday psalm of the Jewish Tamid service (Mishnah Tamid 7:4) — the week begins by acknowledging that the world is not ours. It is also chanted during the Torah return in synagogue: the Torah scroll is carried back to the Ark while vv.7-10 are sung, identifying the Torah itself as the King of Glory arriving. No single Christian reading captures the density of this psalm's living Jewish liturgical home.
[^2]: **KONTEXT** — *the seas... the currents*: The "seas" and "currents" here are not geography. They are the primordial chaos forces from ancient Near Eastern cosmology — the same mythological pair (*yam* and *nahar*) that appears in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, where the sea-god Yamm threatens cosmic order. The image is of God establishing the world by subduing these forces — like driving foundation piles into a swamp. The world holds because God planted it there, against the chaos that surrounds it.
[^3]: **KONTEXT** — *Clean hands... uncorrupted heart*: These are ethical conditions, not ritual ones. The Hebrew words for "clean" and "pure" here do not belong to the vocabulary of ritual purification — they are integrity language. The four items form a chiasm: outer conduct (hands) / inner attitude (heart) / inner falsehood (not giving yourself to what is empty) / outer deceit (not swearing falsely). The question is not "have you performed the right rituals?" but "are your relationships honest — with God and with the people around you?" This is anti-corruption language. In a world of false oaths and bloodied hands, the psalm asks: who has kept their integrity intact?
[^4]: **TEXT** — *carry away a blessing... vindication*: The Hebrew word *vindication* here (*tsedaqah*) does not primarily mean moral quality or personal righteousness. It means relational vindication — God declaring in favor of the person whose integrity is intact. It is the saving fidelity of a God who backs up those who kept their hands clean when everyone around them didn't. — The verb "carry away" is the same Hebrew root (*nasa*) as "does not give themselves" in v.4 and "Lift your heads" in vv.7 and 9. This is the poet's structural design: the one who refuses to carry falsehood is the one who carries away blessing; the gates that lift up perform the cosmic version of the same verb. One root word threads the entire psalm.
[^5]: **KONTEXT** — *the company... Jacob*: "Jacob" at the end of this verse identifies the community of seekers as wrestlers — those who struggle with God (Genesis 32). The word *dor* ("company" or "generation") is not just an age-group but a relational community: the people defined by the act of seeking rather than by lineage or ritual status. Scholars identify this verse as a post-exilic addition that democratizes the entrance question — the true community is not the priestly elite but the poor who seek because they have nowhere else to turn.
[^6]: **KONTEXT** — *Lift your heads, you gates... ancient doors*: The gates are personified — cosmic infrastructure being commanded to acknowledge sovereignty. The image is borrowed from the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, where gods in the divine council lift their heads in acknowledgment when a victorious king arrives. The command is the entire universe being ordered to recognize who its ruler is. — "Ancient doors" translates Hebrew *pitchei olam* — literally "doors of *olam*." The word *olam* means both "age-old" (ancient in time) and "eternal" (permanent in quality). Both senses are intentional. These are doors that have stood since the beginning and will stand forever. — "The King of Glory" (*melek hakavod*) is a title that appears nowhere else in the entire Hebrew Bible — a hapax, a unique throne name. The root of *kavod* (glory) is *kaved* — heaviness, weight, substance. The King of Glory is the King whose weight nothing can resist. — In Jewish liturgical practice, these verses are chanted as the Torah scroll is returned to the Ark. The Ark doors open; the congregation sings "Lift your heads, you gates"; the Torah enters as the King of Glory. This practice continues unbroken in synagogues worldwide.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *the warrior of battles*: The Hebrew *gibbor milchamah* means a physical combatant — a battlefield warrior, not a metaphorical "mighty one." The psalm identifies God specifically as someone who fights. David Clines argued that this language writes "a blank check for war." John Goldingay countered: "It would be at least as plausible to suggest that we are making war God's business rather than ours and writing a blank check for pacifism." The tension is real and unresolved. The psalm does not tell you which reading is correct.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *Who is he... the LORD of armies*: The second round of the gate drama is not redundant — it is more insistent. Three escalations: (1) "Who is he" adds an emphatic pronoun absent from v.8's question; (2) "the LORD of armies" (*YHWH tsevaot*) surpasses v.8's "warrior of battles" — this is not one fighter but the commander of the entire heavenly host; (3) the final "He is the King of Glory" adds the emphatic pronoun again — a clinching identification. The universe has its answer. The title *YHWH tsevaot* is a throne name — the name God bears as the royal resident of Zion, the one whose power makes the city of God invulnerable.

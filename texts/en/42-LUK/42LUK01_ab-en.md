## Kapitel 1


### To Theophilus


**1** Many before me have already taken up the task of setting down an orderly account*, the way our historians frame a reliable record,* of the things that have come to their fullness among us,*brought to completion, not merely happened to,*[^1]
**2** just as they were handed to us*, passed down through a trusted chain,* by those who were there from the beginning, who saw it with their own eyes*and could vouch for it firsthand* and went on to serve the word they witnessed.[^2]
**3** And so I too, having traced the whole course of it carefully from the start*, investigating the sources myself,* resolved to write it out for you in order, most honored Theophilus,*whose patronage stands behind this work,*[^3]
**4** so that you may know how firm the ground is beneath the things you were taught.*Not to settle every question by force, but to give you something solid to stand on.*[^4]

### An Old Priest and an Impossible Promise


**5** It happened back when Herod was king over Judea,*an Idumean propped up by Rome, paranoid and quick to kill,* and in those days there was a priest named Zechariah, from the division of Abijah,*the eighth of the twenty four rotations that took their turns serving in the Temple,* and his wife was a descendant of Aaron herself; her name was Elizabeth.*Two priestly bloodlines under one roof, the kind of house a village reads as marked by God's favor.*[^5]
**6** Both of them lived right before God, walking faithfully in everything the Lord commanded, blameless.[^6]
**7** But they had no child, because Elizabeth could not have children, and by now both of them were old.*In their world that emptiness was read as a curse, and the blame fell on the woman.*[^7]
**8** One day Zechariah's division was on duty, and he was serving as priest before God.
**9** As the priests did it, the lot fell to him to go into the sanctuary of the Lord and burn the incense.*The one task chosen by lot, the holiest a common priest could ever touch.* Out of a whole lifetime, this was his one turn.[^8]
**10** And the whole gathered crowd was praying outside, at the hour of incense.*He goes in alone; the people stay beyond the threshold while the smoke rises.*[^9]
**11** Then an angel of the Lord appeared to him, standing at the right side of the altar of incense.*The right side, the side of favor and power.*[^10]
**12** When Zechariah saw him he was shaken, and fear came over him.*Not mild surprise, the kind of dread that hits the body.*[^11]
**13** But the angel said to him, "Don't be afraid, Zechariah. Your prayer has been heard.*God himself has heard it.* Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you are to name him John.[^12]
**14** He will be your joy and delight, and many will rejoice at his birth,
**15** because he will be great in the sight of the Lord. He must never drink wine or strong drink,*set apart for God like a Nazirite from the womb,* and he will be filled with the Holy Spirit while he is still in his mother's womb.[^13]
**16** He will turn many of the people of Israel back to the Lord their God.
**17** He will go out ahead of the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah,*the prophet whose return would signal the day of the Lord,* to turn the hearts of parents back to their children and the disobedient back to the wisdom of those who live right, to make a people ready for the Lord."[^14]
**18** Zechariah said to the angel, "How can I be sure of this? I'm an old man, and my wife is well on in years."*The same question Abraham once asked, and from a priest who should know the old stories best.*[^15]
**19** The angel answered, "I am Gabriel. I stand in the presence of God, and I was sent to speak to you and bring you this good news.*One of the few who stand in God's own court, sent with the good news himself.*[^16]
**20** And now, listen: you will be silent, unable to say a word, until the day this happens, because you did not believe my words, which will come true at their appointed time."*The priest who doubted the promise must now carry it in silence.*[^17]
**21** Meanwhile the people were waiting for Zechariah, wondering why he was taking so long in the sanctuary.*Far longer than the brief offering should take.*[^18]
**22** When he finally came out, he could not speak to them, and they realized he had seen a vision in the sanctuary. He kept making signs to them but stayed mute.*He could not give the blessing the people were waiting for.*[^19]
**23** When his days of service were over, he went home.*Back to the hill country, where priests kept land and trade between rotations.*[^20]
**24** Soon afterward his wife Elizabeth became pregnant, and for five months she kept herself out of sight. She said,
**25** "This is what the Lord has done for me. In these days he has looked on me and taken away the shame I carried in front of everyone."*The disgrace a barren woman bore before the whole village, lifted at last.*[^21]

### Mary Says Yes


**26** When Elizabeth was six months along, God sent the angel Gabriel to a town in Galilee called Nazareth, a place no one important had ever heard of,*a peasant village Rome had never bothered to learn the name of, which is exactly where the Most High chose to begin.*[^22]
**27** to a young woman pledged in marriage to a man named Joseph, a descendant of David. The pledge was binding, as good as marriage,*and Joseph's line is no incidental detail: it carries the throne of David straight into this child's legal name.* though she had never slept with a man. For a girl in her position, a pregnancy now would look like only one thing, betrayal, and a betrothed girl found pregnant could be denounced, cast off, even stoned. Her name was Mary.[^23]
**28** The angel came to her and said, "Rejoice! God has poured out his favor on you, and it rests on you still.*You are already the graced one, and graced you remain.* The Lord is with you."[^24]
**29** The words shook her. She turned them over and over, trying to make sense of a greeting like that.[^25]
**30** "Don't be afraid, Mary," the angel said. "God has chosen to show you his favor.
**31** Listen: you are going to conceive and give birth to a son, and you are to name him Jesus.
**32** He will be great. He will be called the Son of the Most High, and the Lord God will give him the throne of his ancestor David.
**33** He will reign over the people of Jacob forever, and his kingdom will never end."*This is the promise sworn to David coming due: the throne re-established, now without end.*[^26]
**34** "How can this happen," Mary asked, "when I have never slept with a man?"*Not whether God could be trusted, but how this would actually come to be.* She knew what such a pregnancy would cost her, and still she stayed in the conversation.[^27]
**35** The angel answered, "The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will settle over you, the way God's presence once filled the tent of meeting.*The same glory-cloud that overshadowed the tabernacle now overshadows you, and you become the place where God dwells.* So the child will be holy, and he will be called the Son of God.[^28]
**36** And here is something more: your relative Elizabeth, the one everyone called barren, is six months pregnant in her old age,*the proof is already breathing, a sign you can go and see for yourself.*[^29]
**37** because no word that comes from God will ever fail."
**38** "I am the Lord's servant," Mary said.*His to command, by my own choosing and no one else's.* "I give myself to this. Let everything happen to me just as you have said." And the angel left her.[^30]

### Two Women, Two Miracles


**39** Soon after, Mary set out and hurried into the hill country of Judea, to a town there.*It was days of hard travel, the better part of a hundred miles from Nazareth, and she went like someone racing to see for herself whether the sign was true.*[^31]
**40** She went straight to Zechariah's house and greeted Elizabeth.*Two women alone, far from the men and the priests, where no one was watching. This is where the new thing would first be spoken aloud.*[^32]
**41** The moment Elizabeth heard Mary's greeting, the baby leaped inside her,*the very leap the angel had promised, a child filled with the Spirit before he ever drew breath,* and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit.[^33]
**42** She cried out at the top of her voice:*no demure whisper but a prophet's shout,* "Of all women, you are the one God has blessed,*blessed as Jael was blessed, and Judith, the women through whom God delivered Israel,* and blessed is the child you carry![^34]
**43** But who am I, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?*That she, an old priest's wife, should be the one honored by a visit from the young woman carrying her Master.*[^35]
**44** The instant your greeting reached my ears, the baby inside me leaped for joy.
**45** How happy is the woman who believed!*She trusted where another, my own husband, had doubted and been silenced.* Every word the Lord spoke to her will be brought to its full completion."[^36]

### His Name Is John


**57** When Elizabeth's time came, she gave birth to a son.
**58** Her neighbors and relatives heard how great a mercy the Lord had shown her,*for in their eyes a barren old woman had carried the shame of God's withheld favor, and now that reproach was lifted,* and they celebrated with her.[^37]
**59** On the eighth day they came to circumcise the child,*the covenant sign that marked his formal entry into Israel, exactly as the Law required,* and they were ready to name him Zechariah, after his father.[^38]
**60** But his mother cut in. "No," she said. "He is to be called John."*The community had a name ready and a custom on its side; she had a word from God, and she would not be talked out of it.*[^39]
**61** "But no one in your family has that name," they objected.
**62** So they turned to his father, motioning to ask what he wanted to call him.*They appealed to the man of the house, certain he would set the matter right and overrule his wife.*[^40]
**63** He asked for a writing tablet, and to everyone's astonishment he wrote, "His name is John."*Not "let him be called," but a settled fact: the name is already his, given from heaven before it was ever spoken on earth.*[^41]
**64** At that very moment his mouth was opened and his tongue set free, and he began to speak, praising God.*His first words after the long silence were not complaint but blessing, the speech of a man whose faith had been remade in the waiting.*[^42]
**65** Fear settled over all the neighbors,*the awe that falls on people when they sense God himself has drawn near,* and through the whole hill country of Judea people kept talking about everything that had happened.[^43]
**66** And all who heard it turned it over in their hearts and asked, "What is this child going to be?" For the hand of the Lord was with him.*The hand of the LORD: the very power and favor that had carried Israel out of Egypt now rested on this child.*[^44]

### Growing Up in the Wilderness


**80** The child kept growing, and grew strong in spirit,*the Spirit shaping him from within, not the body alone but the prophet taking form.*[^45] He lived out in the wilderness country, far from the towns,*the ground where prophets are made: Moses, Elijah, Israel itself were each forged in the wastes, away from Temple and city, and came back carrying a word no one in power had given them.*[^46] until the day he stepped into the open before Israel,*the day he was presented, formally shown to the nation as the one they had been waiting for.*[^47]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *orderly account ... fullness*: Luke opens with the conventions of a formal historiographical prologue, the kind Greek historians like Herodotus and Thucydides used to establish credibility. The verb behind "come to their fullness" (peplerophoremenon, perfect passive) signals events brought to completion by God, not mere historical occurrence. The "many" predecessors are honored, not dismissed: Luke joins a tradition rather than displacing it.
[^2]: **KONTEXT** — *handed to us ... own eyes*: "Handed to us" renders paredosan, the formal transmission of tradition (paradosis) prized in both Jewish and Greco-Roman teaching, where reliable accounts moved through known chains of teachers. "Saw it with their own eyes" renders autoptai, a technical word for eyewitnesses carrying legal and historiographical weight, the strongest credential an ancient writer could claim.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *most honored Theophilus*: "Most honored" (kratiste) is an honorific marking high social rank. A dedication like this was a social transaction: Theophilus, whose name means "lover of God," was likely the patron who funded the work's production and copying, and naming him honored both giver and recipient. The verb "traced carefully" points to active research, not passive reception of what Luke was handed.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *firm the ground ... taught*: "How firm the ground is" renders asphaleia, grounded reliability you can stand on, not aggressive certainty that silences questions. "The things you were taught" renders katechethes, the root of "catechesis," formal religious instruction: Theophilus has already been taught and now seeks confirmation, so the aim is reasoned confidence, not blind faith.
[^5]: **KONTEXT** — *Herod ... division of Abijah ... descendant of Aaron*: "In the days of Herod" anchors the story in hard political reality: Herod the Great (reigned 37 to 4 BCE) was an Idumean client king installed by Rome, distrusted by many Jews and notorious for violent paranoia. The "division of Abijah" was the eighth of twenty four priestly courses (1 Chr 24:10); each served one week twice a year plus the major festivals, so priestly duty was intermittent. Both Zechariah (a priest) and Elizabeth (of Aaron's line) carry priestly descent, an unusually blessed pairing in the eyes of their community, which sharpens the shock of their childlessness.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *right before God ... blameless*: "Righteous" (dikaioi) here is covenant faithfulness, not moral perfection, and "blameless" (amemptoi) means without reproach in keeping the Torah. Luke piles up the language of exemplary piety precisely so the reader will not read their childlessness in the next verse as a hidden judgment from God.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *could not have children*: The word translated "could not have children" (steira, barren) carries deep biblical resonance, recalling Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, and Hannah, women whose barrenness God reversed to bring forth a child of promise. Socially, childlessness was devastating and was typically blamed on the wife, threatening the family line and her standing. The text vindicates Elizabeth and never frames her infertility as moral failure.
[^8]: **KONTEXT** — *the lot fell to him ... burn the incense*: Incense was offered on the small golden altar inside the Holy Place, a space only priests could enter, morning and evening. With so many priests and so few openings, the duty was assigned by lot, and a given priest might be chosen once in a lifetime or never. For Zechariah this was the pinnacle of his priestly life, a privilege he could have waited decades for.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *praying outside, at the hour of incense*: The priest entered the Holy Place alone while the assembled worshipers prayed in the courts outside (cf. v.10). The rising incense was understood to carry the people's prayers upward, and the priest acted as their mediator before God, which is why this private moment is also a public, communal one.
[^10]: **KONTEXT** — *the right side of the altar*: In the dim Holy Place, lit only by the seven branched lampstand, the angel stands at the right of the altar. The right side traditionally signals favor and strength, so the placement itself, before a word is spoken, marks this as a message of grace.
[^11]: **TEXT** — *he was shaken, and fear came over him*: The Greek pairs etarachthe ("was troubled," deeply agitated) with phobos ("fear"), a visceral, physical terror, not gentle astonishment. This bodily fear is the standard human response in the Old Testament pattern of angelic announcements (cf. Gideon, the parents of Samson, Abraham).
[^12]: **TEXT** — *Your prayer has been heard*: "Has been heard" is a divine passive (eisekousthe): the unspoken actor is God himself. The prayer in view can be read at once as Zechariah's longing for a child and as Israel's prayer for redemption, and Gabriel's answer in vv.13 to 17 covers both.
[^13]: **KONTEXT** — *He must never drink wine or strong drink*: Lifelong abstinence from wine and strong drink echoes the Nazirite consecration (Num 6) and the dedication of figures like Samson and Samuel, marking John as set apart for God from the very start. That the Spirit fills him before birth signals the new era Luke is announcing, where the Spirit's work begins even in the womb.
[^14]: **KONTEXT** — *the spirit and power of Elijah*: This draws directly on Malachi 3:23 to 24 (4:5 to 6), where God promises to send Elijah before the great day of the Lord to turn parents' hearts to their children. To a first century Jewish ear, "the spirit and power of Elijah" announces nothing less than the long awaited dawn of God's decisive intervention. The body renders Malachi's gendered "fathers" inclusively as "parents," following the verse's own scope.
[^15]: **HARM** — *How can I be sure of this?*: Zechariah's request for proof echoes Abraham's "How am I to know?" (Gen 15:8). His doubt is presented honestly: even the righteous can struggle to believe. The silencing that follows is formational discipline within faith, not condemnation, and the narrative restores him by the end of the chapter.
[^16]: **NAME** — *I am Gabriel*: Gabriel is one of the archangels who "stand in God's presence" (cf. Dan 8 to 9); his name means roughly "God's strong one." The verb behind "bring good news" (euangelisasthai) is gospel vocabulary, surfacing this early in Luke. To name himself and his standing is to answer Zechariah's doubt with the full weight of heaven's authority.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *you will be silent ... until the day this happens*: The silence is both sign and discipline, and it is embodied, not merely symbolic: Zechariah will live wordless for some nine months until the child is born and named (resolved at v.64). The irony lands hard, the very priest whose role is to speak blessing over the people leaves the sanctuary unable to speak at all.
[^18]: **KULTUR** — *wondering why he was taking so long*: The incense offering itself was brief, and priests did not linger in the Holy Place. The crowd's unease grows because the delay is abnormal, a quiet sign that something extraordinary has happened inside.
[^19]: **KONTEXT** — *he could not speak to them*: After offering incense the priest would normally come out to pronounce the blessing over the waiting people. Zechariah's inability to speak both confirms that he has encountered something divine and withholds the very blessing his office exists to deliver, so the crowd reads the silence correctly: he has seen a vision.
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *he went home*: Because priests served only two weeks a year plus festivals, they lived ordinary working lives the rest of the time, often on land or in trade in the Judean hill country. Zechariah simply returns to that everyday life, now carrying the promise in silence.
[^21]: **KULTUR** — *taken away the shame I carried in front of everyone*: Elizabeth's "disgrace among people" reflects the real social stigma attached to childlessness, not any divine judgment. Her words are vindication: God has reversed her standing in the community's eyes. The seclusion may signal modesty, gratitude, or protection of the pregnancy, and the text leaves the reason open.
[^22]: **KONTEXT** — *a place no one important had ever heard of*: Nazareth was a small agricultural village beneath the notice of Rome and largely of Jerusalem. Luke's reversal theme is already running: the supreme God selects a nobody from nowhere, not a royal house in a capital.
[^23]: **KONTEXT** — *a descendant of David*: Betrothal was legally binding, broken only by divorce; the family's honor was at stake. Legal paternity through Joseph conveyed inheritance and lineage rights regardless of biological generation, which is how the child receives the Davidic claim (2 Sam 7).
[^24]: **TEXT** — *it rests on you still*: "Graced one" renders the perfect passive participle kecharitomene: a completed action with an ongoing result. Mary has been favored and remains so. Origen noted this exact greeting is found nowhere else in Scripture, addressed to Mary alone.
[^25]: **TEXT** — *The words shook her*: The verb dietarachthe denotes deep inner turmoil, not mild surprise, and dielogizeto pictures her reasoning the greeting through, an interior weighing rather than passive confusion.
[^26]: **KONTEXT** — *his kingdom will never end*: The piled titles, Son of the Most High, throne of David, an everlasting reign, announce the fulfillment of God's covenant promise to David (2 Sam 7). To a first hearer these were not generic honorifics but the long-awaited Messianic claim.
[^27]: **TEXT** — *How can this happen*: Mary's question is inquiry, not doubt. She asks "how?" not "how can I be sure?" In contrast to Zechariah, her question is met with explanation rather than silencing.
[^28]: **KONTEXT** — *the way God's presence once filled the tent of meeting*: "Will settle over you" renders episkiasei, "overshadow," echoing the cloud that covered the tent of meeting so that the glory of the Lord filled it (Exod 40:35). Mary becomes the new locus of divine presence, and the Spirit, not human agency, is the agent of conception.
[^29]: **KONTEXT** — *six months pregnant in her old age*: Elizabeth's pregnancy is offered as a confirming sign. Faith here is not blind: the impossible has already begun in someone Mary knows, answering the unspoken question of how anyone could believe such news.
[^30]: **TEXT** — *I am the Lord's servant*: Doule is a female bondservant, but here it is Mary's own self-designation, a freely chosen radical availability to God, not subservience imposed by men. "Let everything happen to me" renders an optative of willing consent: a powerful active yes, her agency, not passive compliance.
[^31]: **KULTUR** — *hurried into the hill country*: The journey from Nazareth to the Judean hill country was roughly 80 to 100 miles, several days on foot through difficult terrain. Mary travels "with haste" (meta spoudes, urgency), which the commentators read as eagerness to confirm the sign Gabriel had given her: that the aged Elizabeth was herself pregnant.
[^32]: **KONTEXT** — *greeted Elizabeth*: Women's gatherings were private, domestic space, outside official religious authority. Yet Luke sets the first prophetic utterances of the new era here, among two women, not in the temple. Zechariah, the priest, is still struck mute and cannot speak; it is Elizabeth who will prophesy.
[^33]: **KONTEXT** — *the baby leaped inside her*: The verb (eskirtesen) is strong, the leaping of lambs, not a gentle stir. This fulfills Gabriel's word that John would be filled with the Spirit even from his mother's womb (1:15). The unborn forerunner recognizes the unborn Lord: prophetic action before birth.
[^34]: **TEXT** — *Of all women, you are the one God has blessed*: Elizabeth's cry (krauge megale, a loud shout) is prophetic intensity, not refined speech. Her blessing deliberately echoes the words spoken over Jael (Judges 5:24) and Judith (Judith 13:18), placing Mary among Israel's heroic women through whom God brought deliverance.
[^35]: **KULTUR** — *the mother of my Lord*: By every social expectation the younger woman should defer to the elder, and a peasant girl to the wife of a priest. Elizabeth reverses it: she counts the visit an honor done to her. "My Lord" (kyrios) carries messianic and divine weight, an astonishing title acknowledging the unborn Jesus' superior status.
[^36]: **TEXT** — *How happy is the woman who believed*: A formal beatitude (makaria, blessed/fortunate). The blessing is for believing, not for biological privilege, and stands in pointed contrast to Zechariah, who doubted Gabriel and was struck mute (1:18-20). "Full completion" renders teleiosis: not merely that the promise will happen, but that it will be brought to perfect fulfillment.
[^37]: **KULTUR** — *how great a mercy the Lord had shown her*: In this culture childlessness was widely read as a sign of divine disfavor, a public reproach borne by the woman. Elizabeth's late pregnancy is therefore not merely happy news but the visible reversal of a presumed curse, "great mercy" (megalyno to eleos) the Lord had shown her. The neighbors gather to share in the honor restored to the household.
[^38]: **KONTEXT** — *On the eighth day they came to circumcise the child*: Circumcision on the eighth day was the non-negotiable covenant requirement (Leviticus 12:3, Genesis 17:12), John's formal entry into the people of Israel. The ceremony took place in the home as a public, communal celebration, and it customarily included the naming. Naming a son after his father or grandfather preserved family identity, so the community fully expected a "Zechariah the younger."
[^39]: **KULTUR** — *his mother cut in*: Elizabeth's intervention is doubly surprising: she breaks the family-naming convention, and she does so as a woman, when control of naming was not hers to exercise. That she already insists on "John" signals that she knows the angelic message given to Zechariah, whether through his written communication or prophetic insight. The name itself, Yohanan, means "the LORD has shown grace," a one-word summary of the whole story.
[^40]: **KULTUR** — *So they turned to his father*: The community appeals to Zechariah as the family's male head, expecting him to override Elizabeth and restore the customary name. They gesture rather than speak to him, which suggests he was understood to be deaf as well as mute. The expectation makes what follows a second surprise: he sides with his wife.
[^41]: **TEXT** — *His name is John*: The Greek uses the present tense ("John is his name"), a declaration of established fact rather than a proposal up for debate. The writing tablet (pinakidion) was a small wood-and-wax board. By choosing the heaven-given name over the family name, Zechariah confirms in writing the obedience his earlier doubt had withheld.
[^42]: **TEXT** — *his mouth was opened ... praising God*: "His mouth was opened" is a divine passive: God restores his speech the instant he obeys. "Immediately" (parachrema) is Luke's signature for sudden divine action. That his very first utterance is blessing (eulogon), not the complaint of a man freed from punishment, marks how the silence had formed rather than merely disciplined him.
[^43]: **KONTEXT** — *Fear settled over all the neighbors*: The "fear" (phobos) is reverent awe at a manifest act of God, not terror. Such wonder spreads: the report travels through the Judean hill country, preparing the wider community for the role this child will one day play.
[^44]: **TEXT** — *the hand of the Lord was with him*: "The hand of the LORD" (cheir kyriou) is an Old Testament idiom for God's active power and favor (for example Exodus 13:3, Ezra 7:9). The phrase signals divine commissioning: this child is set apart for God's purposes, and the question "What then will this child become?" hangs over the rest of the story.
[^45]: **TEXT** — *grew strong in spirit*: The Greek ekrataiouto is passive: the child was being strengthened. Paired with "in spirit," the phrase points past physical growth to spiritual maturation, the hidden formational work that prepares him for what is coming.
[^46]: **KONTEXT** — *the wilderness country*: The plural eremois means uninhabited regions, not a single lifeless desert; he moved among the sparsely settled Judean wilderness. Scripture reads the wilderness as the place of prophetic formation (Moses, Elijah, Israel). John's shaping happens outside institutional religion, away from the Temple though he is a priest's son, and from there he will emerge to confront Jerusalem's leadership. Some have speculated contact with the Essene community at Qumran, but the text gives no warrant for it.
[^47]: **TEXT** — *stepped into the open before Israel*: The noun anadeixis denotes a public showing or formal commissioning, the official beginning of his ministry. The verse holds the question raised earlier, "What then will this child become?" (1:66), unanswered, leaving the reader waiting until he appears in chapter 3.

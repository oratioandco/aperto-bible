## Kapitel 1


### Why I'm Writing This


**1** Many have undertaken to set down an account of the events that came to their completion among us[^1]—as they were handed down to us by those who were eyewitnesses from the beginning and became servants of the word.
**2**  **3** And so I too, having followed the whole story carefully from the beginning, have decided to write it out for you in order, Most Excellent Theophilus[^2], so that you may have solid ground[^3] beneath the things you have been taught.
**4** [^4]

### An Old Priest and an Impossible Promise


**5** In the days of Herod, king of Judea[^5], there was a priest named Zechariah, from the division of Abijah[^6]. His wife was of the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elizabeth. *A priestly couple, both from ancient priestly lineage.*
**6** They were both righteous before God, blameless in all that the law required[^7] — *a couple no one could fault.*
**7** But Elizabeth was childless.[^8] *Childlessness, in their world, was not a private sorrow. It was shame — the question no one asked out loud: What did they do wrong? They had done nothing wrong.* And now both of them were old.

**8** When the time came for Zechariah's division to serve in the Temple, he went up to Jerusalem.
**9** The priests cast lots to see who would enter the sanctuary and burn incense before the Lord[^9] — *the honor that came, perhaps, once in a priest's whole life, if it came at all.* The lot fell to Zechariah.
**10** He went in. The congregation stood outside, praying, while he burned the incense.
**11** *The Holy Place: the seven-branched lampstand, the altar of gold, the smoke rising. He was alone in there — as the priest always was, with God.*
Then he saw someone standing at the right side of the altar. *The right side — the side of blessing.[^10]* Before the figure spoke a word, every nerve in Zechariah's body knew: something is happening here. And fear fell on him.[^11]
\v 12
**13** "Don't be afraid, Zechariah," the angel said. "Your prayer has been heard.[^12] Your wife Elizabeth will have a son — name him John. He will be a joy to you, and many will be glad because of him.
**14**  **15** He will be great before God, drinking no wine or strong drink[^13], filled with the Holy Spirit from his mother's womb.
**16** He will bring many of the children of Israel back to the Lord their God,
**17** going before him in the spirit and power of Elijah[^14] — turning the hearts of parents to their children, the disobedient to the wisdom of the righteous — making a people ready."

**18** Zechariah looked at him. "How will I know this is true? I am an old man — and my wife is well on in years."[^15]

**19** The angel answered: "I am Gabriel. I stand in the presence of God[^16]. I was sent to speak to you and to tell you this good news.
**20** And now — because you did not believe my words, which will be fulfilled at the right time — you will be silent. Unable to speak. Until the day when these things happen."[^17]

**21** Outside, the congregation was waiting. Zechariah was taking too long.
**22** When he finally came out and could not speak to them, they understood: he had seen something in there. He was making signs to them, and he stayed silent.
**23** When his days of service were over, he went home.
**24** And Elizabeth his wife conceived. She stayed home for five months.
**25** "The Lord has done this for me," she said. "He has looked at me — and taken away my disgrace."

### Mary Says Yes


**26** Six months after Elizabeth had conceived, God sent Gabriel again. This time, not to the Temple in Jerusalem, not to a priest at the altar — but north, to Galilee, to a village called Nazareth. *Not the kind of place you'd expect anything to happen.*
**27** A young woman named Mary lived there. She was engaged to a man named Joseph, who was of the house of David. And she had not yet been with a man.[^18]

**28** Gabriel came to her and said: "Greetings — you whom God has favored.[^19] The Lord is with you."
**29** The words shook her.[^20] She stood there, trying to work out what this greeting could mean.
**30** "Don't be afraid, Mary," the angel said. "You have found favor with God.
**31** Listen — you will conceive and give birth to a son. Name him Jesus.
**32** He will be great — Son of the Most High. God will give him the throne of David, and he will reign over Jacob's house forever.
**33** His kingdom won't end."

**34** Mary said: "How can this be? I'm not married yet."

**35** The angel answered: "The Holy Spirit will come over you. The power of the Most High will overshadow you — *the way the cloud once covered the holy tent in the wilderness[^21]*. And for this reason the holy one who is born will be called the Son of God.[^22]
**36** Even your relative Elizabeth — who everyone said could not have children — is six months pregnant.
**37** Because nothing is impossible with God."[^23]

**38** Mary said: "I am the Lord's servant.[^24] Let it be to me as you have said."[^25]
And the angel left her.
*She knew what this could cost. Or perhaps she didn't, not fully. Either way, she said yes.*[^26]

### Two Women, Two Miracles


**39** Mary set out right away — *traveling south through the hill country, several days' journey,* to a town in Judea.
**40** She went to Zechariah's house and greeted Elizabeth.
**41** The moment Elizabeth heard Mary's greeting, the baby leaped inside her.[^27][^28] And Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit.
**42** She cried out — with a loud voice, *the way you do when something is bigger than you*:

"Of all women, you are the most blessed. And the child you carry — blessed.
**43** Who am I, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?[^29]
**44** Because listen — the moment I heard your voice, the child in my womb leaped for joy.
**45** Blessed is she who believed that what the Lord told her would come true."

### Mary's Song


**46** Mary said:
> My soul magnifies the Lord.
**47** My spirit leaps with joy —
> God is my rescuer.\f + \fr 1:47 \fk TEXT \fq rescuer (σωτήρ) \ft Often translated "savior" — but "rescuer" captures the concrete, physical force of the word. In the Magnificat's context of reversed power structures, God is not an abstract savior but an active rescuer who intervenes in real situations of oppression.\f*
**48** He saw me —
> his servant, \add nobody, from nowhere\add* —\f + \fr 1:48 \fk TEXT \fq nobody, from nowhere (ταπείνωσιν) \ft Often translated "humility" — but ταπείνωσις in the Greek Old Testament primarily means low social status, not the virtue of humbleness. Mary is describing her social position: young, poor, female, from an insignificant village. She is not claiming to be humble. She is naming what everyone already knew — she was nobody. And God noticed her anyway.\f*
> and from now on
> every generation will call me blessed.
**49** The Mighty One did great things for me.
> His name stands alone.\f + \fr 1:49 \fk TEXT \fq His name stands alone (ἅγιον τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ) \ft Literally "holy is his name." The Hebrew concept of holiness (קָדוֹשׁ, qadosh) means set apart, unlike anything else, in a category of its own. "His name stands alone" renders this: there is nothing to compare it to.\f*
**50** His mercy reaches
> from one generation to the next —
> for everyone who lives in awe of him.
**51** He acted with the power of his arm.
> He scattered the proud —
> caught in their own plans.\f + \fr 1:51-53 \fk KONTEXT \fq scattered... brought down... filled... sent away \ft The four reversals use Greek aorist tenses that grammarians call "gnomic aorists" — past tense forms describing what God characteristically does. Not just "God did this once" but "this is what God is always doing." The literary model is Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1-10), which makes the same move: personal gratitude becomes cosmic vision. The Magnificat has been sung daily at Vespers (evening prayer) for over 1,500 years — and banned by military dictatorships in Latin America who recognized its revolutionary implications.\f*
**52** He brought the powerful down from their thrones.
> He raised up the forgotten ones.
**53** He filled the hungry with good things.
> He sent the rich away empty.\f + \fr 1:52-53 \fk LEBEN \fq brought down... raised up... filled... sent away \ft The Magnificat cuts two ways. For those who feel small, overlooked, or powerless: God sees you and is already working on your behalf. For those who have power, security, or plenty: this text is uncomfortable — and meant to be. Mary's song is not a comfortable prayer. It asks every reader: where do I stand in God's great reversal? The honest answer is usually: both places at once.\f*
**54** He reached out to Israel, his servant —
> remembering mercy —
**55** just as he promised our ancestors:
> Abraham and his children,
> forever.

**56** Mary stayed with Elizabeth for about three months, and then returned home.

### His Name Is John


**57** The time came. Elizabeth gave birth to a son.
**58** Her neighbors and relatives heard that the Lord had shown her great mercy, and they came to celebrate with her.

**59** On the eighth day, they came for the circumcision — *the day a child receives his name, the sign of the covenant.[^30]* They were going to name him Zechariah, after his father. *That was the right thing, the expected thing.*
**60** His mother spoke up. "No. He is to be called John."[^31]
**61** They said, "No one in your family has that name." And they gestured to his father — *surely he would have a say in this.*
**62**  **63** Zechariah asked for a writing tablet. He wrote: ***His name is John.***
They were all surprised.
**64** And immediately his mouth opened, his tongue was set loose — and he began to praise God.[^32]
**65** Word spread through all the hill country of Judea, and everyone who heard about it was deeply unsettled. They kept turning it over in their minds: ***What will this child become?***
**66** For the hand of the Lord was with him.

### Zechariah's Prophecy


**67** His father Zechariah was filled with the Holy Spirit, and he prophesied:[^33]
**68** Blessed is the Lord, the God of Israel —
> he came. He ransomed his people.
**69** He raised up a horn of salvation for us[^34]
> in the house of David, his servant —
**70** as he spoke through his holy prophets
> from the beginning:
**71** rescue from our enemies,
> freedom from all who hate us.
**72** He has shown mercy to our ancestors.
> He has kept his holy covenant,
**73** the oath he swore to our father Abraham[^35]:
**74** to pull us free from enemy hands —
> to let us serve him without fear,
**75** upright and clean before him
> all our days.
**76** And you —[^36]
> little one —
> you will be called prophet of the Most High.
> You will go before the Lord and clear his way,
**77** to give his people knowledge of rescue —
> through the forgiveness of their sins —
**78** because of the gut-deep mercy of our God.[^37]
> By it, the rising sun will visit us from heaven\f + \fr 1:78 \fk TEXT \fq rising sun (ἀνατολή) \ft A word with deliberate double meaning: "rising" (of the sun, cf. Mal 4:2 "sun of righteousness") AND "the Branch" (a messianic title, cf. Zech 3:8; 6:12 LXX). Luke's language is intentionally layered: the messianic Branch rises like the dawn. Light imagery runs through the entire closing section.\f*
**79** to give light to those who sit in darkness,[^38]
> death's shadow over them,
> and guide our feet into the way of peace.\f + \fr 1:79 \fk LEBEN \fq way of peace (εἰρήνη) \ft The final word of Zechariah's prophecy is "peace" — Hebrew shalom, meaning not just absence of conflict but comprehensive well-being. The entire Benedictus moves from political liberation (enemies, hatred, rescue) to this: a way of peace. The journey of faith, Zechariah suggests, does not end in victory over enemies. It ends in peace — the kind that comes from knowing you are held in gut-deep mercy.\f*

### Growing Up in the Wilderness


**80** The child grew strong in spirit. He lived in the wilderness until the day he stepped into the open — *into the public life of* Israel.[^39]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *completion (πεπληροφορημένων)*: Perfect passive participle — emphasizes divine completion, not mere occurrence. These events didn't just "happen"; they reached their God-intended fulfillment. The tense carries ongoing significance: what was completed still matters now.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *Theophilus (Θεόφιλος)*: The Greek title κράτιστε ("Most Excellent") was used for senior Roman officials. The dedication was a social transaction: a patron supported the work, the author honored them publicly. Theophilus had already received instruction (v.4); Luke writes to deepen what he knows.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *solid ground (ἀσφάλεια)*: Literally "security, stability" — something you can stand on. Not aggressive certainty that silences inquiry, but grounded confidence that invites it. Luke's goal is reliability, not proof.
[^4]: **APOLOGETICS** — *carefully from the beginning*: Luke's prologue follows the conventions of Greco-Roman historiography (cf. Herodotus, Thucydides). He claims eyewitness tradition, personal investigation, and orderly presentation — the standard credibility markers of ancient historians. The 5,800+ Greek New Testament manuscripts far exceed any other ancient work (Homer: ~1,900; Thucydides: ~8). Whatever one makes of the content, the transmission is remarkably well-attested.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *Herod*: Herod the Great (reigned 37–4 BCE), an Idumean appointed king of Judea by Rome. Deeply distrusted by many Jews; notorious for executing potential rivals, including his own sons. "In the days of Herod" signaled to ancient readers: dark, dangerous times.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *division of Abijah (ἐφημερία)*: One of 24 priestly divisions that rotated service in the Temple (cf. 1 Chr 24:10). Each served two weeks per year plus the three major festivals. Priests lived ordinary lives the rest of the year — farming, trading — and traveled to Jerusalem for their rotation.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *righteous... blameless (δίκαιοι... ἄμεμπτοι)*: Two distinct terms: δίκαιοι means covenantally faithful — living in right relationship with God and neighbor. ἄμεμπτοι means "without reproach" — no one could bring a charge against them. This is not moral perfection but consistent faithfulness. Luke establishes their innocence before revealing their suffering.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *childless*: Cultural anthropologists (Bruce Malina) describe the first-century Mediterranean as an honor-shame culture. Childlessness was not private grief — it was public disgrace. The unspoken assumption: God is punishing them. Elizabeth bore decades of social stigma despite her blamelessness. The text deliberately juxtaposes her righteousness (v.6) with her shame (v.7) to expose the injustice of the assumption.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *burn incense*: The incense offering occurred twice daily in the Holy Place. Being chosen by lot was the honor of a priest's entire career — most never drew it. Archaeological evidence confirms the priestly lottery system from clay lots discovered at Jerusalem sites. Zechariah had likely waited decades for this moment.
[^10]: **KULTUR** — *right side*: In ancient Jewish understanding, the right side of the altar signaled favor and good news. Ephrem the Syrian writes: "John, herald of the Lord of the right, was announced from the right of the altar." The position itself was a message before any words were spoken.
[^11]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *angel*: Angel appearances are among the most challenging aspects of these stories for modern readers. The text assumes a world where God communicates through messengers — not as literary decoration, but as real encounter. Interpretive spectrum: some read angelic appearances as symbolic narrative (literary convention expressing divine communication), others as visionary experience (genuine encounter in altered consciousness), others as literal (real spiritual beings). Jesus himself spoke of angels as real (Matt 18:10). Luke presents Gabriel as a concrete messenger with a concrete message — and Zechariah's terror as genuine.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *has been heard (εἰσηκούσθη)*: Aorist passive — a "divine passive" where God is the implied agent. Not "someone heard you" but "God heard you." The prayer may have been for a child, or for Israel's redemption, or both. Either way: decades of silence did not mean God wasn't listening.
[^13]: **KONTEXT** — *no wine or strong drink*: A Nazirite vow (cf. Num 6:3), indicating a life set apart for God from birth. Samson and Samuel were similarly consecrated (Judg 13:4-5; 1 Sam 1:11). The pattern — consecrated before birth, filled with the Spirit, set apart for a mission — connects John to Israel's greatest prophets.
[^14]: **KONTEXT** — *spirit and power of Elijah*: Cf. Malachi 4:5-6: "See, I will send the prophet Elijah before that great and dreadful day of the Lord comes." John fulfills this expectation — not as Elijah reincarnated, but carrying Elijah's prophetic spirit. The "turning hearts" language comes directly from Malachi's closing words.
[^15]: **LEBEN** — *How will I know?*: Zechariah's doubt does not disqualify him from God's plan — the promise is fulfilled regardless. His question echoes Abraham's in Genesis 15:8 ("How can I know?"). Faith and doubt live closer together than we often assume. The silence that follows is discipline, not rejection — and when it ends (v.64), his first words are praise.
[^16]: **KONTEXT** — *Gabriel (Γαβριήλ)*: One of the archangels who "stand before God" (cf. Dan 8:16; 9:21). The name means "God's valiant one" or "strength of God." The same Gabriel who appeared to Daniel now appears to Zechariah — a 500-year gap bridged by the same messenger. This is the highest possible angelic authority.
[^17]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *you will be silent*: Zechariah is struck mute — not permanently, but until the promise is fulfilled (v.64). Modern readers may find this punitive. The text frames it differently: silence becomes a space where something new can grow. Nine months without speaking transforms him. His first words after the silence are not complaint but blessing (v.67-79). Some interpreters read this as discipline; others as a sign that confirms the angel's authority. Either way, the silence is formative.
[^18]: **KULTUR** — *engaged (μεμνηστευμένην)*: Betrothal in first-century Judaism was legally binding — more like marriage than modern engagement. Girls were typically betrothed between ages 12 and 14; the betrothal period lasted about a year before the husband brought the bride to his home. Sexual unfaithfulness during betrothal was treated as adultery. Mary's pregnancy before the marriage was finalized put her — and her entire family — at serious social and legal risk.
[^19]: **TEXT** — *favored (κεχαριτωμένη)*: Perfect passive participle of χαριτόω — "one who has been graced." The tense matters: it describes a completed action with ongoing effect. God's favor rests on Mary already, before any response. Catholic tradition translates "full of grace" (connecting to the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception). Protestant tradition prefers "favored one" — emphasizing God's initiative rather than Mary's state. Both readings honor the Greek: Mary is the recipient of extraordinary divine grace.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *shook her (διεταράχθη)*: A strong verb — intensified form of ταράσσω (to disturb). Not mild unease. The prefix δια- amplifies the force: thoroughly shaken, deeply disturbed. This is not gentle wonder. Gabriel's greeting disrupts everything Mary thought she knew about her own life.
[^21]: **TEXT** — *overshadow (ἐπισκιάσει)*: Echoes Exodus 40:35, where the cloud of God's presence "overshadowed" the tabernacle — the dwelling-place of God among the people. Mary becomes the new locus of divine presence. The language is deliberately veiled: it describes divine creative power, not a physical act. Luke's restraint here is remarkable.
[^22]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *the Holy Spirit will come over you*: The virgin conception is one of the most debated claims in the Gospels. The text presents it without apology or explanation — simply as what happened. Interpretive spectrum: historical-faith (literal divine conception, affirmed in the Nicene Creed across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions), theological-symbolic (a statement about Jesus' unique identity and divine origin), critical-historical (a theological construction drawing on OT motifs). Common ground across most readings: Jesus' origin is understood as uniquely connected to divine initiative. The question depends in part on one's assumptions about God and the natural order.
[^23]: **KONTEXT** — *nothing is impossible with God*: An echo of Genesis 18:14, where God asks Sarah: "Is anything too hard for the Lord?" — just after promising her a son in old age. Luke deliberately connects Mary's situation to Sarah's. The pattern is clear: God specializes in impossible pregnancies that change history (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, Elizabeth, Mary).
[^24]: **TEXT** — *servant (δούλη)*: Literally "female slave." Mary's self-designation is radical availability to God — chosen identity, not imposed servitude. We translate "servant" rather than "handmaid" (which carries Atwood/Handmaid's Tale resonance for contemporary readers) or "slave" (technically closer to the Greek but wrong register for this literary project).
[^25]: **TEXT** — *let it be to me (γένοιτό μοι)*: Optative mood — the rarest Greek mood, expressing a deliberate wish or prayer. Not passive resignation ("whatever happens") but active consent ("I choose this"). Mary's fiat is one of the most consequential acts of will in the Bible.
[^26]: **LEBEN** — *she said yes*: Mary's response is a model of active receptivity. She doesn't understand everything (who could?), but she says yes to what she does understand. Her courage is concrete: this pregnancy could mean social shame, broken engagement, even danger. Faith here is not certainty — it's trust that says yes before all the consequences are clear.
[^27]: **TEXT** — *leaped (ἐσκίρτησεν)*: A vigorous physical verb — used of lambs leaping. Not a gentle stirring but an explosive movement. The same word describes David dancing before the Ark (2 Sam 6:16 LXX). The unborn John's response to the unborn Jesus mirrors David's response to God's presence.
[^28]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *the baby leaped... filled with the Holy Spirit*: Two supernatural claims in a single verse: an unborn child recognizes the presence of another unborn child, and a woman is filled with prophetic Spirit upon hearing a greeting. The text presents this as Spirit-enabled recognition — not mere coincidence. For skeptical readers: the passage at minimum describes something extraordinary happening in the encounter between these two women. For faith readers: the Spirit is already at work before birth, before preaching, before anything public.
[^29]: **KONTEXT** — *mother of my Lord*: Elizabeth calls Mary "mother of my Lord" — κύριος here carries both messianic and potentially divine significance. An elderly priest's wife defers to a young peasant girl. The social reversal is deliberate: status, age, and religious credentials give way to the new thing God is doing.
[^30]: **KULTUR** — *eighth day*: Circumcision on the eighth day (Gen 17:12) was the moment a son formally entered the covenant community of Israel and received his name. It was both religious rite and social event — the community witnessed and affirmed the child's identity.
[^31]: **NAME** — *John (Ἰωάννης)*: Hebrew Yohanan — "YHWH has shown grace" or "God is gracious." The name is the theological summary of the entire birth narrative: after decades of childlessness and months of silence, God's grace arrives. Elizabeth's insistence on a name no one in the family bears signals: this child belongs to God's story, not just the family's.
[^32]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *immediately his mouth opened*: παραχρῆμα — "at once," Luke's characteristic word for sudden divine action. The instant Zechariah obeys (writing "John" rather than "Zechariah"), his speech returns. The connection is unmistakable: obedience releases what doubt had locked. His first words after nine months of enforced silence are not complaint or explanation — they are praise. The silence has done its work.
[^33]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *filled with the Holy Spirit, and he prophesied*: The man who could not speak for nine months now speaks the longest prophecy in Luke's infancy narrative. Luke presents this as Spirit-empowered utterance — not Zechariah's own composition but words given by God through him. The Benedictus has been prayed daily at Lauds (Morning Prayer) across Christian traditions for centuries. Whether one reads "filled with the Holy Spirit" as divine inspiration, prophetic ecstasy, or literary convention, the content of the prophecy — with its blend of political hope and tender intimacy — is remarkable.
[^34]: **TEXT** — *horn of salvation (κέρας σωτηρίας)*: A biblical image for strength and power (cf. 2 Sam 22:3; Ps 18:2). A bull's horn represented fighting power — "horn of salvation" means: God has raised up a powerful deliverer. The image is deliberately strong, even militaristic in its original frame. Under Roman occupation, these words carried explosive political charge.
[^35]: **KONTEXT** — *oath to Abraham*: Cf. Genesis 22:16-18. The Abrahamic covenant promised descendants, land, and blessing to all nations. Zechariah sees John's birth and the coming Messiah as the fulfillment of that ancient oath — the covenant arc stretching from Abraham to this moment in this room.
[^36]: **TEXT** — *little one (παιδίον)*: The tenderness of this shift is extraordinary. Zechariah has been speaking cosmic theology — covenants, enemies, salvation history. Then suddenly he turns to the infant in his arms: "And you, little one." The Greek παιδίον is diminutive, affectionate. A father naming his son's destiny.
[^37]: **TEXT** — *gut-deep mercy (σπλάγχνα ἐλέους)*: Literally "bowels of mercy" — σπλάγχνα refers to the gut, the viscera, the deepest physical seat of emotion in ancient understanding. God's compassion is not abstract or detached. It is gut-level, visceral, as involuntary as a parent's response to a child's cry.
[^38]: **KONTEXT** — *darkness... shadow of death*: Echoes Isaiah 9:2 ("the people walking in darkness have seen a great light") and Psalm 23:4 ("though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death"). Zechariah's prophecy ends not with military victory (despite vv.71-74) but with light, guidance, and peace — reframing the political hope in spiritual terms.
[^39]: **KULTUR** — *wilderness*: In the prophetic tradition, the wilderness is where God forms people: Moses spent 40 years there, Elijah fled there, Israel wandered there for a generation. The wilderness is not empty — it is the place where everything unnecessary is stripped away. John's formation happens outside institutional religion, though he is a priest's son. The prophet is shaped not by the Temple but by the desert.

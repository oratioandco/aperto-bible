## Kapitel 1


### To Theophilus


**1** Dear Theophilus,[^1] many have already undertaken to set in order an account of the events that have been brought to completion among us,[^2]
**2** They drew on what was handed down to us by those who were eyewitnesses from the beginning, who then entered the service of this message.
**3** So I too, after following the whole matter carefully from its source, decided to write it out for you in order,
**4** so that you may know: what you have been taught stands on solid ground.[^3]

### An Old Priest and an Impossible Promise


**5** In the days of Herod, king of Judea,[^4] a priest named Zechariah served in the priestly division of Abijah.[^5] His wife came from the daughters of Aaron. Her name was Elizabeth.
**6** Both of them lived rightly before God, blameless in everything the law required.[^6]
**7** And yet they had no children. Elizabeth was childless.[^7] *In their world, that wasn't a sorrow kept private. It was shame. The question no one asked aloud: what did they do wrong?* And by now they were both old.

**8** One day his division was on duty, and Zechariah was serving as priest before God.
**9** By the priesthood's old custom, the lot fell to him. He was to enter the sanctuary of the Lord and burn the incense.[^8] *The honor that might come once in a priest's life, or never come at all.*
**10** Outside, the whole gathering of the people was praying. It was the hour of the incense.
**11** *Inside, the Holy Place: the seven-branched lampstand, the veil, the smoke rising. He was alone in there, the way the priest always was.*
Then an angel of the Lord appeared to him, standing at the right side of the altar of incense.[^9][^10]
**12** Zechariah saw him, and fear fell over him.
**13** But the angel said to him:

**14** He will be your joy and delight, and many will rejoice at his birth.
**15** He will be great in the Lord's eyes. He will drink no wine, nothing fermented.[^11] And he will be filled with the Holy Spirit before he is born.
**16** He will turn many of the children of Israel back to the Lord their God.
**17** He will go ahead of him in Elijah's spirit and strength,[^12] turning the hearts of parents back to their children, turning the disobedient back to a life that walks in God's way. So he readies a people for the Lord."

**18** Zechariah said to the angel: "How am I to know this? I'm an old man, and my wife is well on in years."[^13]

**19** The angel answered him: "I am Gabriel.[^14] I stand before God. I was sent to bring you this good news.
**20** And now: because you did not believe my words, which will be fulfilled in their time, you will be silent. You will not be able to speak until the day these things happen."[^15]

**21** Outside, the people were waiting for Zechariah, wondering why he was taking so long in the sanctuary.
**22** When he came out, he couldn't speak to them. They could see he'd had a vision in the Holy Place. He kept making signs, and the words wouldn't come.
**23** When his days of service were over, he went home.
**24** Some time later his wife Elizabeth conceived, and for five months she kept to herself.
**25** "This is what the Lord has done for me," she said, "the day he looked on me and took away my disgrace among the people."[^16]

### Mary Says Yes


**26** Six months had passed since Elizabeth had conceived. God sent Gabriel again. *Not to the Temple in Jerusalem this time. Not to a priest at the altar. North, into Galilee, to a village named Nazareth.*
**27** He came to a young woman engaged to a man named Joseph, a descendant of King David. Her name was Mary, and she had not yet been with a man.[^17]

**28** The angel came to her and said: "Greetings, Mary. God has set his kindness on you;[^18] the Lord is with you."
**29** The words shook her.[^19] She stood there, turning the greeting over, trying to make out what it could mean.

**30** The angel said to her: "Don't be afraid, Mary. God's kindness is on you.
**31** Listen: you will conceive and bear a son, and you will name him Jesus.[^20]
**32** He will be great. He will be called Son of the Most High. The Lord God will give him the throne of his ancestor David,
**33** and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever. His kingdom will never end."[^21]

**34** But Mary said to the angel: "How can this be? I'm not married yet."

**35** The angel answered her: "The Holy Spirit will come over you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you,*the way the cloud once covered the holy tent in the wilderness*.[^22] And so the holy one who is to be born will be called the Son of God.[^23]
**36** And listen: your own relative Elizabeth has conceived a son in her old age. She is already in her sixth month. The woman everyone said was childless.
**37** For nothing said by God will be impossible."[^24]

**38** Mary said: "I am the Lord's servant.[^25] Let it happen to me, just as you have said."[^26]
And the angel left her.
*She could not yet know the cost.*[^27]

### Two Women, Two Miracles


**39** In those days Mary set out, hurrying into the hill country, to a town in Judea. *Several days' journey south, alone.*
**40** She let herself into Zechariah's house and greeted Elizabeth.
**41** The moment Elizabeth heard Mary's greeting, the child leaped in her womb,[^28][^29] and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit.
**42** Then she cried out, *like a prophetess:*

**43** Who am I, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?[^30]
**44** For listen: the moment the sound of your greeting reached my ears, the child in my womb leaped for joy.
**45** How happy is the woman who trusted that what the Lord had told her would come true."[^31]

### Mary's Song


And Mary said:

**46** Everything in me celebrates God. Everything.[^32]
**47** My whole spirit, awake. God is my rescuer.[^33]
**48** He saw me. Me. His servant, nobody, from nowhere.[^34]
> And now: every generation will call me happy.
**49** The Mighty One did this. For me.
> His name: holy.
**50** His mercy runs
> from one generation to the next,
> for everyone who stands in awe of him.

**51** He acted in strength.
> He scattered the proud, caught in their own plans.\f + \fr 1:51-53 \fk TEXT \fq scattered… brought down… filled… sent away \ft The four verbs of reversal are Greek aorists — a tense that grammarians call here the "gnomic aorist": past-tense forms used to describe what God characteristically does. Not "God did this once" but "this is what God is always doing." The German Aperto renders this in the present tense; the English keeps the past tense, but the gnomic force is the same. The reversals are not nostalgia for an old story. They are an ongoing description.\f*
**52** He brought the powerful down from their thrones.
> He raised up the forgotten ones.
**53** He filled the hungry with good things.
> He sent the rich away empty.\f + \fr 1:52-53 \fk LEBEN \fq brought down… lifted up… filled… sent away \ft The Magnificat cuts two ways at once. To anyone who feels small, overlooked, powerless: God sees you, and is already at work on your behalf. To anyone who has power, security, or plenty: this song is uncomfortable, and it is meant to be. Mary's prayer is not the soothing hymn the church habit can make it. It asks every reader — where do I stand in the reversal? The honest answer is usually both places at once: most readers are powerful in some rooms and powerless in others, and the song addresses both. A question without any prior religious framework: in which areas of your life are you the one being lifted, and in which are you the one whose seat may be at risk? The senior in the office is the junior in their own family; the financially comfortable adult is the one whose body is failing; the privileged in one society are the precarious in another. The song does not flatten these registers — it names them. For readers inside a faith: the Magnificat sits at the heart of the church's daily prayer. It is sung at Vespers in the Catholic, Anglican, and Orthodox traditions, every evening, for centuries. The repetition is not nostalgia; it is formation. To say these words daily is to be slowly rearranged by them, until God's reversal sounds like home rather than threat. Civically, the Magnificat has been read in every era of social struggle: Latin American base communities under military regimes recited it as resistance; the song was banned in Argentina, Guatemala, El Salvador for being politically subversive. Liberation theologians have read it as the Christological foundation for an option for the poor. Conservative readings have insisted the dethroning is interior — the proud heart inside each believer. Both readings have textual basis; the text refuses to choose between them. Vocationally, the song raises a structural question for anyone whose work touches power: where in my professional life am I positioned to lift the lowly or to keep them down? Hiring decisions, promotions, the architecture of meetings, who gets the floor. This week: pray or read the Magnificat aloud once. Notice which lines comfort you and which ones make you flinch.\f*

**54** He has come to Israel, his servant,
> he has not forgotten his mercy,
**55** just as he promised our fathers,
> Abraham and his children,
> forever.\f + \fr 1:55 \fk KONTEXT \fq Abraham and to his children forever \ft The Magnificat closes by naming the Abrahamic covenant — the promise God made to Abraham in Genesis 17:7 and confirmed by oath in Genesis 22:17–18: "I will be your God and the God of your descendants after you… your offspring will bless all the nations of the earth." Mary's song is not announcing a new beginning. It is celebrating covenant continuity: the same God, the same promise, the same mercy, now arriving in her body. The Magnificat is one of the most deeply Jewish prayers in the New Testament.\f*

**56** Mary stayed with her about three months, and then she went home.

### His Name Is John


**57** The time came. Elizabeth gave birth to a son.
**58** Her neighbors and her relatives heard how greatly the Lord had shown mercy to her, and they rejoiced with her.

**59** On the eighth day they came to circumcise the child. *That was the day a son received his name, the sign of the covenant.*[^35] They were going to call him Zechariah, after his father.

**60** But his mother spoke up. "No. He's to be called John."[^36]

**61** They said to her: "But no one in your family has that name."
**62** So they made signs to his father, asking what he wanted the child called.
**63** He asked for a writing tablet and wrote: *His name is John.* And they were all amazed.
**64** At once his mouth was opened, his tongue freed,*after nine months of silence,* and he began to speak, praising God.[^37]
**65** A kind of awe settled on everyone living nearby, and across the whole hill country of Judea people talked these things over.
**66** And everyone who heard kept it in their heart, asking: "What is this child going to become?" For the Lord's hand was on him.[^38]

### Zechariah's Prophecy


**67** His father Zechariah was filled with the Holy Spirit, and he prophesied, saying:[^39]

**68** Praise to the Lord, the God of Israel.[^40]
> he has come to his people. He has set them free.
**69** He has raised up for us a mighty rescuer[^41]
> from the line of David, his servant,
**70** just as he said long ago,
> through the mouths of his holy prophets:
**71** a rescue from our enemies,
> a freedom from the hand of those who hate us.

**72** He has shown mercy to our fathers,
> he has remembered his holy covenant,
**73** the oath he swore to Abraham our father:[^42]
**74** to take us out of the hand of our enemies,
> to give us back to him without fear,
**75** to live for him, clean and upright,
> every day of our lives.

**76** And you, little one:[^43]
> you will be called prophet of the Most High.
> You will go ahead of the Lord
> to clear the road for him,
**77** to give his people the knowledge of rescue,
> the rescue that comes through the forgiveness of their wrongs,

**78** because of the gut-deep mercy of our God,[^44]
> by which the dawn from on high
> is breaking over us,\f + \fr 1:78 \fk TEXT \fq dawn from on high (ἀνατολή) \ft A word that holds two images at once. \fq Anatolḗ\fq* primarily names the rising of the sun — the dawn breaking over the horizon — and so the verse evokes Malachi 4:2's "sun of righteousness rising with healing in its wings." But the same Greek word, in the Greek Old Testament, was also the word for the messianic Branch — the shoot from David's line in Jeremiah 23:5 ("a righteous Branch from David's line") and Zechariah 3:8; 6:12. Both readings are simultaneously available, and Zechariah is almost certainly holding both at once: the messianic Branch will rise on the world the way the dawn rises on the dark.\f*
**79** to shine on those who sit in darkness,[^45]
> with death's shadow over them,
> to lead our feet onto the road of peace.\f + \fr 1:79 \fk LEBEN \fq the road of peace \ft The last word of Zechariah's prophecy is "peace" — Hebrew \fq shalom\fq*, which means not the absence of conflict but comprehensive well-being: wholeness, rightness, life as it was meant to be. The whole Benedictus moves toward this. The song begins with enemies and ends with peace. That trajectory is what the text is asking the reader to inhabit. A question, before any religious framing: what would peace concretely mean in your life right now? Not abstractly, concretely. Not the end of a geopolitical conflict, but the end of the small interior war you have been carrying for months — with a family member, with yourself, with a memory that will not stop talking. Shalom rarely begins where we expect it; it usually starts a layer beneath the categories we have used to sort our lives. For readers inside a faith, this verse sits at the center of multiple contemplative traditions. The Eastern practice of hesychia — stillness of heart — understands peace not as absence but as the presence of an Other. The Jesus Prayer, carried in the breath, trains exactly this. Quaker silent meeting, Taizé chants, Ignatian silence between exercises — all stand in the same line. Peace is received, not manufactured; one makes oneself available to it. Politically, shalom carries an edge that the English word "peace" tends to lose. In the prophetic Hebrew tradition, shalom is always also justice — \fq mishpat\fq* and shalom belong together. There is no peace negotiated over the heads of the wounded. The Christian peace ethic — from Bonhoeffer through Martin Luther King to the Mennonite, Quaker, and Catholic Pax Christi traditions — has worked from this distinction: peace that papers over the truth is not shalom; shalom costs truth. Communally, the verse has shaped the church's liturgy of peace — the passing of the peace before communion, the kiss of peace in early Christian gatherings, the sign of reconciliation that precedes the sacrament. The order is theological: you cannot share the bread until you have looked the person beside you in the face. Try one short stillness at the start of the day this week, holding the single word "peace" — and then listen for what answers.\f*

> Peace.

### Growing Up in the Wilderness


**80** The child grew, and grew strong in spirit. He lived in the wilderness until the day he stepped into the open life of Israel.[^46]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *Dear Theophilus (κράτιστε)*: The Greek title \fq κράτιστε\fq* (\fq kratiste\fq*) was the formal honorific for senior Roman officials and educated patrons — Paul uses it later when addressing Felix and Festus (Acts 23:26; 24:3; 26:25). Theophilus is socially located: a man of rank, a library, a circle in which this book will be read aloud. The name itself ("friend of God") plays alongside, but the work is addressed to a real recipient of the educated upper class. In Greek the address sits in v.3; we move it to the front because a modern dedication names its addressee first.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *Luke's prologue*: Luke opens his work in the form that ancient historians from Thucydides to Josephus had used: a single periodic sentence in which method, source-work, and addressee are named in one breath. We render the opening not as a Greek period-piece but as what it would be today — a dedication to the patron. To consult eyewitnesses, sift earlier reports, and order the whole — that is the self-discipline of historical prose, not the modesty of a devotional author.
[^3]: **UEBERSETZUNG** — *solid ground (ἀσφάλεια)*: Older translations reach for "certainty" — but the Greek word does not name the kind of proof that closes a question. \fq ἀσφάλεια\fq* is related to \fq σφάλλομαι\fq* — to stumble, to lose footing; the word literally means: that on which one does not stumble. Footing. A place to stand. It is the security someone seeks who wants to keep going, not the certainty that ends asking.
[^4]: **KULTUR** — *Herod*: Herod the Great (reigned 37–4 BCE) — an Idumean placed on the throne of Judea by Rome. Builder of the second Temple on a colossal scale; ruthless to anyone he saw as a rival, including his own sons. "In the days of Herod" was, to a first-century Jewish ear, the name of dark and dangerous times.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *division of Abijah (ἐφημερία)*: One of the twenty-four priestly divisions that took turns at Temple service (1 Chronicles 24). Each division served two weeks of the year, plus the three pilgrimage festivals. Abijah was the eighth in the rotation. The rest of the year the priests lived at home in their villages — farmers, craftsmen, teachers — and traveled to Jerusalem for their week.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *rightly… blameless (δίκαιοι… ἄμεμπτοι)*: Two distinct words. \fq δίκαιοι\fq* names covenant faithfulness — living in right relationship with God and with one another. \fq ἄμεμπτοι\fq* means literally "no one could bring a charge." The pairing is not a claim of moral perfection. It is the narrator's quiet insistence: no fault could be found in them. Whatever was about to be assumed about them by their neighbors was not the truth about who they were.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *childless*: In the honor-shame world of first-century Galilee, childlessness was not a private grief. It was public disgrace. The unspoken assumption — that God had withheld blessing for some unconfessed fault — followed a couple everywhere they went. The anthropologists Bruce Malina and Jerome Neyrey describe how thoroughly this assumption shaped first-century social standing. Elizabeth had carried this stigma for decades, although the previous verse has just established she had done nothing to deserve it.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *chosen by lot*: Lots were drawn each morning to decide which priests would carry out which acts of Temple service. The incense offering — entering the Holy Place and burning incense at the inner altar — was the rarest and most honored of these duties. With several thousand priests in active service, most never drew this lot. Many waited their whole career. Archaeologists have found priestly lots at excavated sites from the late Second Temple period; the system was concrete and well attested.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *right side*: In ancient Jewish understanding, the right side of the altar signaled favor, blessing, good news. Ephrem the Syrian, writing in the fourth century, put it this way: "John, herald of the Lord of the right, was announced from the right of the altar." The position was a message before any words were spoken.
[^10]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *an angel of the Lord*: Angel appearances are one of the places where contemporary readers most often hesitate. The Christian tradition has taken Gabriel seriously as a real being for two thousand years: Daniel meets him in his visions (Daniel 8; 9), Luke names him here and again to Mary, and the living prayer of the Eastern and Catholic traditions still names him by name today. Jesus himself spoke of angels as real (Luke 4:10; Matthew 18:10). To read it this way is to read a real encounter: something becomes visible in the sanctuary that is normally unseen, and it does not leave the priest unchanged. — For the reader who cannot hold the visitation as a literal encounter, the text still has work to do as image. The angel can be read as the inherited form in which Luke's tradition tells what cannot otherwise be said: that what arrived in the sanctuary that day did not originate inside Zechariah's own thinking, that the message had a sender. The figure of Gabriel is then the language an ancient narrative uses for divine address — not literary ornament, but the only register the writer has for the kind of moment in which everything turns. — Skeptical scholarship has its own description of the same scene. Visionary experience in priestly settings is broadly documented in the history of religions — Jewish, Christian, Muslim mystical literatures all carry similar accounts; clinical psychology recognizes altered states under fasting, isolation, and the burden of prolonged hope. An old priest, alone in the Holy Place at the most charged moment of his career, in the smoke of the incense — the conditions are themselves not ordinary. — Wherever a reader stands in this, the text means a definite thing: a message was delivered, and the one who received it was undone by it. The invitation here is small and concrete — to walk with Zechariah as far as the next scene, and see what the message turns out to do.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *drink no wine*: The abstinence is the language of the Nazirite vow (Numbers 6:3) — a consecration to God lived out in the body. Samson and Samuel are the great Nazirites of the Hebrew Bible; like John, both were given to mothers who had been called childless. The point is not individual asceticism. It is the sign that this child belongs to God from the beginning, in the way of the great prophets who were marked for their work before they could choose it.
[^12]: **KONTEXT** — *the spirit and power of Elijah*: The closing lines of the Hebrew Bible (Malachi 3:23–24, or 4:5–6 in Christian numbering) promised that Elijah would return before the day of the Lord, to turn the hearts of parents to their children and the children to their parents. John fulfills that promise — not as Elijah reincarnated, but as Elijah's successor, carrying the same prophetic Spirit. Jesus himself confirms this reading later in Luke (7:27) and Matthew (11:14). The "turning hearts" language of v.17 comes word-for-word from Malachi's closing sentence.
[^13]: **LEBEN** — *how am I to know this?*: Zechariah is a priest. He has spent his life inside the institutional language of God's promises, and when grace shows up to fulfill one of those promises in his own house, he asks for evidence. The angel's answer is silence — not as punishment but as the form of formation the moment requires. A question worth sitting with regardless of where you stand on religion: where in your own life are you asking for proof of something that has already arrived? The job that came through, the friend who is still there, the recovery that should have ended you — at what point does the request for confirmation become a way of refusing to receive what is in your hands? For readers inside a faith tradition, the silence imposed on Zechariah is a recognizably contemplative discipline. The Quaker silent meeting, the Trappist Grand Silence, the Eastern Orthodox practice of hesychia, the Yom Kippur fast from speech — every old tradition knows there are seasons when the only theology adequate to the moment is the closed mouth. And the verse has a vocational edge: pastors, therapists, teachers, doctors who handle the language of meaning every day know the gap between the language and the lived thing. The text suggests that gap is not a disqualification. It is, sometimes, the exact place where God arrives. This week: try one fifteen-minute stretch in which you offer no explanation, to anyone, including yourself.
[^14]: **KONTEXT** — *Gabriel*: One of the archangels who, in the Jewish tradition, "stand before God." The name in Hebrew means something like "God's strong one" or "the strength of God." This is the same Gabriel who appeared to the prophet Daniel five centuries earlier (Daniel 8:16; 9:21) — a five-hundred-year gap bridged by the same messenger. Luke is signaling that this annunciation belongs to the same prophetic arc Daniel began.
[^15]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *you will be silent*: Gabriel's announcement reads to modern ears like arbitrary punishment for an honest doubt — exactly the kind of place where readers expect understanding and instead hear sentence. Three observations move the picture. First: the muteness is bounded — "until the day these things happen." Second: it opens a nine-month space in which Zechariah can take in what his mind has just refused. Third: when the silence breaks (v.64), his first words are not complaint but praise. The Christian contemplative tradition recognizes the shape of what is happening to him — the silent retreat, the day in the monastery, the week without speech. What contemplatives elect, Zechariah here receives; both work the same movement: language is stilled so that something else can rise. The older mystical writers read this as the cell in which something new can be born. — For the reader who cannot take the angelic mandate as a real intervention, the verse still works as image: the experience of being suddenly without language, after asking the wrong kind of question of the wrong moment, is something most readers have lived through under other names. Skeptical scholarship would note that functional speech-loss following severe emotional shock is well documented in the clinical literature, and that the social fact of priestly silence following an unrelatable vision is unsurprising in itself. — Wherever a reader stands in this verse, the text invites the same posture: hear the silence not as loss but as preparation, and wait with Zechariah until v.64 shows what has ripened inside it.
[^16]: **LEBEN** — *he took away my disgrace*: Elizabeth does not say God has eased her private feelings. She says he has lifted the disgrace from her social standing — the public shame she had carried for decades was never hers to begin with. The verb she uses names a public movement, not an interior one: whatever judgment had attached itself to her name, God has detached. — A question that lands before any theological framework: which form of "disgrace" am I carrying that is not actually mine but a social verdict — a statistic, a position not reached, a life-shape that did not arrive, a label inherited from a parent or an industry or an ex-partner that I have quietly taken on as the truth about myself? — For readers inside a faith, the verse opens a movement of liberation that the Jewish tradition of \fq teshuvah\fq* and the Christian practice of \fq confessio\fq* both train: learning to tell apart what is actually weighing on a person and what the world has placed there. A contemplative form: the Ignatian examen, asked at the end of the day — where was I made small today by a verdict that did not come from God? — Relationally, infertility remains one of the most difficult experiences to speak about — couples in IVF, single women past forty, men with undiscussed diagnoses. The verse asks an honest question of one's own circle: where are people in our community left alone with their quiet form of "something like disgrace"? And structurally, the unemployed, the recently released, the displaced carry a similar social mark that no one names aloud. — Call one person you know who is currently carrying a form of "not-having-reached" — and simply be there, without offering an explanation.
[^17]: **KULTUR** — *engaged*: Betrothal in first-century Judaism was not modern engagement. It was the first stage of the marriage itself — legally binding, and broken only by formal divorce or by death. Girls were typically betrothed between the ages of twelve and fourteen; the betrothal period lasted about a year, after which the husband brought the bride home. Sexual unfaithfulness during betrothal was treated, legally, as adultery. Mary's pregnancy before her marriage was finalized put her — and her entire family — at real social and legal risk. The angel's announcement is not happening in safety.
[^18]: **UEBERSETZUNG** — *God has set his kindness on you (κεχαριτωμένη)*: The Greek word is a single perfect-passive participle — an action already done and still going on: she has been graced, and is being graced. The Latin Vulgate rendered it \fq gratia plena\fq*, "full of grace," and across fifteen centuries that translation became the cornerstone of an entire Marian theology — Mary as the singularly graced one, addressed by an honorific. The evangelical alternative, "highly favored," runs the opposite risk: it slides toward prosperity-language, as though grace were a prize. Aperto translates the verb as an act: God has set his kindness on you, and that kindness is still resting. The kindness is real and personal, and it does not depend on what Mary does next. Different Christian traditions have read this verse differently for centuries — Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant readings each have textual basis. Aperto holds the verse itself open, and lets the long discussion stay where it belongs: between traditions, not inside the verse line.
[^19]: **TEXT** — *shook her (διεταράχθη)*: An intensified verb — the prefix \fq δια-\fq* sharpens an already strong word for being disturbed. Not gentle wonder. Mary is thoroughly shaken. Notice too that what shakes her is not the angel's appearance but the words — at v.12 Zechariah is shaken by the sight; Mary is shaken by what is said. The narrator's quiet point: she is listening more closely than the priest was.
[^20]: **NAME** — *Jesus*: Hebrew Yehoshua, shortened to Yeshua: "YHWH saves." The name is the mission. Modern readers, hearing "Jesus" as a proper name, often miss what an ancient ear heard at once — that the child's name was already the announcement of what he had come to do. Joseph receives the same instruction in Matthew's account, with the meaning made explicit: "you shall name him Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21).
[^21]: **KONTEXT** — *the throne of David… of his kingdom there will be no end*: The angel is quoting the Davidic covenant — God's promise to David through the prophet Nathan in 2 Samuel 7:12–16: "I will raise up your offspring after you… and I will establish his kingdom… your throne shall be established forever." The messianic claim is meaningless without this background. Gabriel is naming this child as the long-awaited heir to that promise — the king from David's line whose reign will not end.
[^22]: **UEBERSETZUNG** — *will overshadow (ἐπισκιάσει)*: The same Greek verb that the Septuagint uses at Exodus 40:35 for the cloud of God's presence "overshadowing" the tabernacle — the dwelling-place of God among the people. Luke chooses the word deliberately: Mary, in her body, becomes the new locus of divine presence. The language is veiled on purpose. It describes a divine creative act, not a physical event. Luke's restraint here is one of the most disciplined choices in the New Testament.
[^23]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *the Holy Spirit will come over you*: The virgin conception is one of the hardest aspects of the Christmas story for modern readers. Luke tells what is happening here in the language of the temple: \fq ἐπισκιάσει\fq* — "overshadow" — is the verb the Septuagint uses for the cloud of God's presence, the Shekinah, filling the tent of meeting at Exodus 40:35, so that even Moses could not enter. The angel is setting Mary in parallel with the tabernacle: where the cloud was then, Mary is now — the place where God will dwell. Taken seriously, this is not a biological claim being smuggled into history; it is the opening act of a God who does not lift his son out of the world but gives him into it. — For the reader who cannot hold the conception as a literal event, the text still works as theological statement: the language marks not Mary's biology but Jesus' identity. "Son of God" is then read as title-claim rather than birth-description; the verse places the divine origin of Jesus in the foreground, and what falls into the background is the mechanism. Read this way, v.35 is above all a coronation in advance. — Skeptical scholarship would point out that Hellenistic and Jewish literature both knew the type-scene of the extraordinary conception as a marker of an extraordinary destiny — Isaac to Sarah, Samuel to Hannah, John to Elizabeth, then Jesus to Mary. Some historians read Luke 1:35 as a chosen theological composition inside that older pattern, where the unusual origin tells the reader what the child means. — What all readings share is the restraint of Luke's own language. There is no speculation about Mary's body, no shame-vocabulary, no judgment of sexuality; the veiling of the language is itself part of what the text is saying. Wherever a reader stands on the question, the text invites the same step: stay with what it says — that something has begun here which does not come from inside the existing world.
[^24]: **KONTEXT** — *nothing said by God will be impossible*: A direct echo of Genesis 18:14, where the Lord asks Sarah, who has just laughed at the announcement of her own pregnancy in old age: "Is anything too hard for the Lord?" Luke is placing Mary deliberately inside the matriarchal pattern — Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, Elizabeth, Mary. God's promises arrive again and again through women whose situations are humanly closed.
[^25]: **UEBERSETZUNG** — *the Lord's servant (δούλη)*: The Greek word \fq δούλη\fq* means, strictly, a female slave. The older English versions reached for "handmaid"; Aperto translates "servant." The reason is a specific cultural fact that the verse can no longer be heard without. Margaret Atwood's \fq The Handmaid's Tale\fq* (1985) — and the streamed adaptation that has been in the cultural foreground since 2017 — has permanently recolored the older English word; "handmaid" now arrives in a contemporary reader's ear carrying the whole machinery of coerced reproductive servitude. That is the opposite of what Mary is saying. Her self-designation is a chosen identity, not an imposed one; her belonging to God is her own — voiced in her own mouth, in the absence of any external pressure, before any consequence has yet arrived. "Servant" clears Atwood's frame from the verse line so that Mary's act of choosing can land. The harder word stays visible here, in the footnote, for the reader who wants to see how thoroughly the verse refuses what Atwood diagnosed.
[^26]: **TEXT** — *let it be (γένοιτό μοι)*: The Greek mood here is the optative — the rarest mood in the language, used for a deliberate wish or a prayer. Not the passive "so be it" of resignation, and not the imperative "let it happen" of a command. Mary is voicing a chosen, active willing: she wants this. The optative is one of the most consequential acts of will in the Bible — and grammatically, it is consent in its most considered form.
[^27]: **LEBEN** — *her yes*: Mary's answer is a model of active receptivity. She does not understand the whole picture — who could? — but she says yes to what is in front of her. Her courage is concrete: the pregnancy could mean broken engagement, social shame, even physical danger. Faith here is not certainty; it is the kind of trust that says yes before all the consequences are clear. The Greek optative she uses is not endurance — it is a resolute, active willing. A question worth sitting with, regardless of any prior religious commitment: where in your own life are you waiting for a guarantee before you say yes? For the right timing, the complete picture, the certainty that it will end well? Most of the decisions that turn out to matter — a vocation, a relationship, a leaving or a staying — were made without that guarantee. For readers inside a faith, the verse opens onto a recognizably Ignatian movement: the \fq Spiritual Exercises\fq* treat Mary's fiat as a daily disposition, something practiced in ordinary life rather than staged as a single heroic act. The Jewish \fq hineni\fq* — "here I am" — stands in the same line: Abraham, Moses, Samuel say it before they know what is coming. Relationally, Mary's yes also has a social weight. She speaks it alone — no advisor, no mother, no fiancé in the room. Anyone who knows someone facing an isolated decision can be the angel who does not speak but does not leave. Sometimes the angel is the one who stays after the message lands. Say one yes this week without first securing the guarantee that it will work.
[^28]: **TEXT** — *leaped (ἐσκίρτησεν)*: A strong physical verb: used in Greek for lambs leaping, for joyful dancing. The same word the Septuagint uses for David's wild dancing before the Ark (2 Samuel 6:16). Not a stirring — a leap. The unborn John responds to the unborn Jesus the way David responded to God's presence: with the body, with movement, with joy.
[^29]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *the child leaped… filled with the Holy Spirit*: Two supernatural claims layered into a single verse — an unborn child recognizes the presence of another unborn child, and a grown woman is filled with prophetic Spirit at the sound of a greeting. The text does not soften either claim, and that is the first thing for a modern reader to register. — The Christian tradition has read Spirit-filling not as metaphor for an interior state but as real encounter — God arriving in a person and speaking through them. What Joel 3:1-2 promises for the end-times — "your sons and your daughters will prophesy" — Luke shows already breaking in here, ahead of schedule. The sequence is precise: Mary overshadowed by the Spirit (1:35), John filled with the Spirit while still in the womb (1:15), Zechariah becoming a prophet (1:67), the disciples at Pentecost (Acts 2). Elizabeth is not an anomaly; she is the start of a sequence in which the Spirit speaks through specific people what they could not yet have known on their own — that the girl at her door is carrying the Lord. The old Church read this verse with care: it is the first prophetic acclamation of Christ in the Gospel, and it is spoken by a woman, and it is given by the Spirit. — For the reader who cannot hold this as Spirit-directed speech, the text still says something the reader will recognize: Elizabeth knows more than she can know — perhaps because the child in her own body has already responded (v.41a), and her words are her way of catching up with what has already moved through her. Read this way, the prophetic language is the form in which the body's recognition becomes audible. — A comparative-religion approach would note that the encounter of two pregnant women, each carrying something improbable, is charged with a weight that traditional language calls "Spirit" — both mystical and clinical literature record the kind of charged recognition that arises between people in such convergences. — What all readings share: Luke writes this moment as the trigger of a speech that knows more than the people speaking. The text invites the reader to listen seriously to the older woman — she is the first to call the coming one by name.
[^30]: **KONTEXT** — *the mother of my Lord*: This is the first place in Luke's Gospel where the word \fq κύριος\fq* — "Lord" — points not to the God of Israel but to Jesus himself. An elderly priest's wife defers, by title, to a young pregnant peasant girl: the social reversal is deliberate. There is also a quiet echo at work: 2 Samuel 6:9, where David exclaims, "How can the ark of the Lord come to me?" Elizabeth is recognizing Mary as the new bearer of God's presence — the Ark of the Covenant in a young woman's body. From this verse forward, "Lord" in Luke begins to carry the Christological weight the rest of the Gospel and Acts will build on.
[^31]: **TEXT** — *how happy (μακαρία)*: Two different Greek words underlie what English translations often flatten to one. At v.42 Elizabeth uses \fq eulogēménē\fq* — the covenant blessing-word, the language of being marked by God's favor in a public, lineage-bearing sense ("blessed are you among women"). Here at v.45 the word changes: \fq makaría\fq* — the beatitude word, which Jesus will use to open the Sermon on the Plain in Luke 6 ("blessed are the poor… blessed are the hungry"). This is the first beatitude in Luke's Gospel — proto-Sermon. Aperto holds the distinction: "blessed" at v.42 (covenant-blessing), "happy" at v.45 (the beatitude form Jesus himself will use). What is being celebrated here is not Mary's social standing but Mary's act of trust — and there is a quiet contrast with Zechariah just visible in the corner of the scene: he asked the angel for evidence and was given silence; she said yes, and is now being called happy in the way the Sermon will later call the poor happy. Faith, the text suggests, is not the absence of fear or question; it is the act of trusting what has been said when the situation does not yet warrant it.
[^32]: **KONTEXT** — *Everything in me lifts up the Lord*: Mary's song — the Magnificat, named for its opening Latin word — has been prayed daily at Vespers, the evening prayer of the Church, for over fifteen hundred years. Its structural model is the song Hannah sings in 1 Samuel 2:1–10 when her impossible pregnancy ends in the birth of the prophet Samuel; the parallels run line for line. What is less often remembered is that the Magnificat was banned by 20th-century military dictatorships in Argentina, Guatemala, and El Salvador — the regimes recognized at once what the song says about what God does to thrones. Latin American liberation theologians (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Leonardo Boff) have read it for half a century as the foundational text of God's option for the poor. The verb that opens it — \fq megalýnei\fq*, "magnifies, makes great" — is doing the heavy work: she is not describing herself; she is naming what is happening through her.
[^33]: **TEXT** — *rescuer (σωτήρ)*: Usually translated "savior" — but "savior" in contemporary religious English has narrowed to a churchly abstraction. \fq σωτήρ\fq* in its original register is concrete: the one who pulls you out, the one who comes for you, the one who arrives when you cannot get yourself free. In the Magnificat's vocabulary of overturned power, "rescuer" carries the right weight.
[^34]: **TEXT** — *the girl from nowhere (ταπείνωσιν)*: The Greek word does not name the virtue of humility. In the Septuagint and in everyday usage, it names social location: low standing, the bottom of the order. Mary is describing where she stood — young, female, peasant, from a village no one expected anything from. She is not claiming to be humble; she is naming what everyone else already knew about her. And God noticed her anyway. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions have built foundational Marian devotion on this verse — "every generation will call me blessed"; the Protestant traditions have read Mary as the exemplary believer named by God. Both readings have textual basis. Aperto holds the verse open and lets both stand.
[^35]: **KULTUR** — *the eighth day*: Circumcision on the eighth day (Genesis 17:12) was the moment a son was formally received into the covenant community of Israel and given his name. It was at once religious rite and social event — the gathered community witnessed the boy's entry into the people and confirmed the name his parents had chosen.
[^36]: **NAME** — *John (Ἰωάννης)*: Hebrew \fq Yohanan\fq* — "YHWH has shown grace," or simply "God is gracious." The name is the theological summary of everything that has happened so far in this chapter. After decades of childlessness, after the public shame, after nine months of silence, grace has arrived; the name says so. Elizabeth's insistence on a name no one in the family carries is not stubbornness. It is the recognition that this child belongs to God's story, not the family's.
[^37]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *at once*: A medically unexplained muteness that ends in the instant a name is written on a tablet — modern readers are right to stop here. — The Christian tradition reads the scene as a sharpened story about obedience and divine action. Luke chooses the word \fq παραχρῆμα\fq* — "at once," "in that very instant" — his signature term for unmediated divine intervention; it appears more often in Luke-Acts than in all the rest of the New Testament combined. The order of the verse is the theological point: first the act of obedience — the father writes the name the angel gave, against the resistance of the relatives — then the mouth opens. The silence breaks not because nine months have run out, but because Zechariah now does what at v.18 he could not. And the first word out of him after nine months is not complaint, not explanation, not anything about himself. It is praise. The early Church read this sequence — trust comes before release — as a spiritual pattern, not as a magical mechanism. — For the reader who cannot hold this as divine intervention, the scene still works as image: the experience anyone knows of having gone silent for a long time because there was nothing to say, and then finding the way back into language through a single act of consent. Writing the name is the gesture by which Zechariah ratifies what has overtaken him; what releases afterward is not primarily the tongue — it is the resistance. — Clinical literature documents functional speech-loss that begins with a shock and resolves after a decisive act; a reader who works in that frame can hear in Zechariah's healing a psychosomatic movement described in the language of faith. — Wherever a reader stands on this, the verse puts trust before resolution and lets the order do its work. The invitation is to listen to the first word after the silence. It is praise.
[^38]: **TEXT** — *the hand of the Lord*: "Hand of the Lord" is an old Hebrew idiom for God's active power — both the power that delivers and the power that judges. In 1 Samuel 5:6 the hand of the Lord is heavy on the Philistines; in Ezra 7:9 the good hand of the Lord is on Ezra. The narrator's brief sentence at the end of v.66 marks the child: whatever else this becomes, God's hand is on it.
[^39]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *filled with the Holy Spirit, and he prophesied*: Zechariah speaks, after nine months of imposed silence, a prophecy in a form he cannot simply have rehearsed — and that raises a question worth taking seriously: where do these words come from? — The Christian tradition reads the Benedictus as the work of the Holy Spirit, as v.67 says plainly: not the product of priestly polish or human composition, but the speech of a man who has been carried through the silence and now knows more than he knew before. Luke is building the same line he traced with Elizabeth (1:41) and will trace again with Simeon (2:25-32): in pivotal moments the Spirit speaks through specific people, and the words they say carry the people of God forward. The Benedictus has been prayed daily at Lauds — the morning office — across the Catholic, Anglican, Orthodox, and Lutheran traditions since the early centuries; the Church has handled it as Spirit-given Scripture, not as private composition, for a millennium and a half. — For the reader who cannot hold the prophecy as Spirit-directed speech, the verse still describes something the reader will recognize. Zechariah is voicing what the tradition of his people has carried him on since childhood — the psalms, the prophets, the language of temple worship — and the nine months of silence have compressed those inherited images into a density that now has to come out. Read this way, the Benedictus is the speech of a man who has finally listened long enough to hear what his own tradition had been saying. — Comparative religious literature documents what happens in prolonged enforced silence: in monastic, contemplative, and Ignatian practice alike, periods of imposed quiet are known to compress interior knowledge until, when language returns, it returns as vision-speech, dense and prophetic in form. — What all readings share is what the Benedictus itself shows: a depth that needs more than time alone to account for. Wherever the reader locates that depth, the invitation is the same — listen to the song before sorting it.
[^40]: **KONTEXT** — *Praise to the Lord*: The Benedictus opens in the Berakah form — the classical Jewish prayer of blessing — that begins every Jewish blessing, daily prayer, and meal-blessing: \fq Barukh attah Adonai\fq*, "Blessed are you, Lord." Zechariah, the priest who has not spoken for nine months, returns to speech inside the deepest formal tradition of his people: he says the prayer his ancestors had said. The Benedictus is prayed daily at Lauds — the morning office — across the Catholic, Anglican, Orthodox, and Lutheran traditions. A note on the verb: \fq epeskhépsato\fq*, "has visited" — the active visitation word: God has come to his people, not merely "looked upon" them at a distance. The same verb returns in v.78. And \fq lýtrōsin\fq*, "redemption," carries the concrete force of ransoming, paying off, freeing; in the Septuagint it is the Exodus word — the language of political liberation, not abstract spiritual rescue.
[^41]: **TEXT** — *a mighty rescuer*: The Greek phrase reads literally "a horn of salvation" — an old Hebrew Bible idiom for strength: the image is a bull's horn, the seat of fighting power (cf. 2 Samuel 22:3; Psalm 18:2). It means a powerful rescuer, a deliverer with real force behind him. Under Roman occupation the phrase had explosive political charge: the people who first heard the Benedictus heard a song about a king who would break the empire's hold. The Benedictus does not flinch from that meaning — but it will not end there either. By v.79 the song has moved from "rescue from our enemies" to "the way of peace."
[^42]: **KONTEXT** — *the oath he swore to Abraham*: Genesis 22:16–18 — the oath God swore to Abraham after the binding of Isaac: "By myself I have sworn, says the Lord… I will surely bless you, and I will surely multiply your offspring as the stars of heaven… and by your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed." Zechariah is reading his own moment inside that ancient promise. The covenant arc stretches from Abraham, through the priest's son just born in this room, to the one for whom that son is the herald.
[^43]: **TEXT** — *And you, little one*: The tonal shift at this verse is the chapter's most intimate moment. Zechariah has been speaking cosmic theology — covenants, enemies, salvation history, the long arc of the Hebrew Bible. And then, suddenly, he turns. He is holding his newborn son in his arms. The Greek \fq paidíon\fq* is a diminutive — "little child," "little one." A father, after nine months of silence, naming his own son's life. The poem turns from God's "we" to the father's "you," from the cosmic horizon to the small face in front of him. Everything else in the Benedictus has been building toward this.
[^44]: **TEXT** — *gut-deep mercy (σπλάγχνα ἐλέους)*: Literally, "the bowels of mercy." The Greek \fq splánchna\fq* refers to the gut — the viscera, the deep inward organs that, in ancient understanding, were the physical seat of emotion. God's compassion is not abstract or detached. It is gut-level, involuntary, the way a parent's body responds before they have decided how to respond. Older English versions render this "tender mercy" — but the older phrase has softened into something almost decorative. Aperto keeps the gut visible: this mercy is not deliberation, it is reflex. It is the response that has already happened before any decision is made.
[^45]: **KONTEXT** — *darkness… shadow of death*: Two great Hebrew Bible echoes. Isaiah 9:2: "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light; those who lived in a land of deep darkness — on them light has shined." And Psalm 23:4: "even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for you are with me." The Benedictus reaches back into both at once. The song that began with horns of salvation and rescue from enemies — language of political and military deliverance — does not end with a victory. It ends with light, with guidance, and with peace. The hope is being reframed inside the song itself.
[^46]: **KULTUR** — *the wilderness*: In the prophetic tradition, the wilderness is the place where God forms people. Moses spent forty years there before Sinai. Elijah fled there when his life was threatened. Israel wandered there for a generation between Egypt and the land. The wilderness is not empty; it is the place where everything unnecessary is stripped away. John's formation happens outside institutional religion, although he is a priest's son. The prophet is shaped not by the Temple but by the desert.

## Kapitel 2


### The Birth of Jesus


**1** In those days, a decree went out from Caesar Augustus[^1] — a registration of the whole Roman world[^2] was to be taken.
**2** This was the first registration, while Quirinius governed Syria.[^3]
**3** And everyone went to register, each to his own town.

**4** Joseph also went up from Galilee, from the town of Nazareth, into Judea — to the city of David, called Bethlehem,[^4] because he was of the house and lineage of David.
**5** He went to register with Mary, his betrothed, who was pregnant. *The journey was several days, mostly uphill.*
**6** While they were there, the time came.
**7** She gave birth to her firstborn son, wrapped him in cloths, and laid him in a feeding trough.[^5] There was no room for them in the guest room.

### The Shepherds and the Angels


**8** There were shepherds out in the fields that night, keeping watch over their flocks.[^6] *Men who slept under the sky and were counted for little — their testimony not trusted in court, their lives lived at the edge of what respectable society considered reliable.*
**9** *An ordinary night.* And then the angel of the Lord stood before them — and the glory of the Lord blazed around them.[^7] *That light was not meant to be outdoors. They knew it.* Great fear seized them.

**10** “Don't be afraid.” *The angel said it quickly. But the voice shifted — dropped into something older, heavier.* “I bring you news of great joy for all the people:[^8]
**11** today, in the city of David, a Savior[^9] has been born. He is the Messiah, the Lord.
**12** And this is your sign — you will find him wrapped in cloths, lying in a feeding trough.”

**13** And suddenly, with the angel, a vast army of heaven,[^10] praising God:

**15** When the angels had gone back into heaven, the shepherds turned to one another. “Let's go to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened — that the Lord has made known to us.”
**16** They went with haste and found Mary and Joseph, and the baby lying in the feeding trough.
**17** When they had seen it, they went and told what they had heard about this child.
**18** Everyone who heard it was amazed.

**19** Mary kept all these things, turning them over in her heart.[^11]

**20** The shepherds returned, praising and glorifying God for everything they had heard and seen — exactly as they had been told.

**21** On the eighth day *— as the Torah prescribed —* the child was circumcised *and enrolled in the covenant of his people.* His name was given: Jesus[^12]. The name the angel had spoken before he was even conceived.

### The Presentation in the Temple


**22** When the days of their purification were completed *— forty days —*[^13] as the law of Moses required, Joseph and Mary brought the child up to Jerusalem to present him to the Lord —
**23** as it is written in the law of the Lord: every firstborn male is to be consecrated to the Lord —
**24** and to offer *— the sacrifice permitted for those who could not afford a lamb:*[^14] a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons.

**25** In Jerusalem there was a man named Simeon[^15] — righteous and devout, a man the Holy Spirit rested on. *He had organized his whole life around a single hope:* the consolation of Israel, *the comfort God had promised through the prophets*[^16].
**26** He had been told by the Holy Spirit that he would not die before he had seen the Lord's Messiah.[^17]
**27** When the Spirit brought him to the Temple courts that day, he went.

**28** Joseph and Mary brought Jesus in. And Simeon took the child in his arms. *He had waited for this. He had waited for decades. And now he held it — and spoke:*

**33** The parents stood there, amazed at what was being said about their son.

**34** Simeon blessed them. *He stood with them for a moment — this young couple, this child. Then he turned to Mary. Something in his face changed.*

**35** and you, Mary — a sword will pierce through your own soul[^18] — so that the hidden thoughts of many hearts may be uncovered.”

**36** There was also a woman named Anna[^19] — a prophetess, daughter of Phanuel, from the tribe of Asher. She was well into her years: she had been widowed young,
**37** and had spent her life at the Temple since then, fasting and praying, day and night.
**38** She came up at that very hour, gave thanks to God, and spoke about the child to everyone in Jerusalem who was waiting for the redemption of the city.[^20]

**39** When they had done everything the law required, they returned to Galilee, to their own town of Nazareth.
**40** The child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom. And the grace of God was on him.

### The Boy Jesus at the Temple


**41** Every year, Joseph and Mary went to Jerusalem for the Passover festival.[^21]
**42** When Jesus was twelve, they went as usual.
**43** *The journey was made in company — neighbors and relatives from Nazareth, the whole village moving together as they always did.* When the festival ended, the group started home. Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem, and his parents did not know.
**44** *They assumed he was somewhere in the caravan — with relatives, with friends, with someone.* It was a full day's journey before they looked for him among relatives and acquaintances, and he was not found.
**45** They turned back to Jerusalem, searching for him.
**46** After three days they found him *— one day traveling without him, one day returning, and a day of searching the city.* He was in the Temple, sitting in the middle of the teachers, listening to them and asking questions.
**47** Everyone who heard him was astonished at his understanding and his answers.

**48** When his parents saw him, they were astonished. His mother said: “Child, why have you done this to us? Your father and I have been frantic — searching for you in anguish.”

**49** He said: “Why were you searching for me? Didn't you know I had to[^22] be in my Father's house?”[^23]
**50** They did not understand what he meant.

**51** He went back down with them to Nazareth and was obedient to them. His mother kept all of it in her heart.[^24]
**52** And Jesus grew — in wisdom and in stature, and in favor with God and with people.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **NAME** — *Augustus*: The title “Augustus” (Latin) or Sebastos (\fq Σεβαστός\fq*) in Greek means “the Exalted One” or “the Revered.” Official Roman inscriptions hailed him as “savior of the world” and “lord.” Luke frames Jesus's birth inside the machinery of the world's most powerful empire — which will unknowingly be used to fulfill a prophecy about the city of David.
[^2]: **KONTEXT** — *whole Roman world (οἰκουμένη)*: The Greek oikoumenē referred to the Roman-ruled inhabited world. Luke deliberately places the birth announcement within the scope of Caesar's power — while the truly world-changing event happens in a village no census planner would notice.
[^3]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Quirinius*: The chronology here is the most discussed historical difficulty in Luke's Gospel. According to the historian Josephus, Quirinius became governor of Syria in 6 CE, while Herod — under whom Matthew places the birth — died in 4 BCE. This creates a gap of at least ten years. Proposed solutions include an earlier command of Quirinius in the region, a translation of \fq πρώτη\fq* as “before” rather than “first,” and the possibility that Luke compresses or connects two separate registrations. The historical tensions are real, and honest scholarship acknowledges them. For a fuller treatment, see the sidebar “The Quirinius Problem.” Luke's primary claim is theological: God acts in real, datable history — not myth or legend — even when we cannot resolve every chronological detail.
[^4]: **NAME** — *Bethlehem*: The Hebrew \fq בֵּית לֶחֶם\fq* (Beit Lehem) means “house of bread.” The prophet Micah had said the ruler of Israel would come from Bethlehem (Micah 5:2). It was the city associated with David — and now the one born “Son of David” is born there. Ancient readers would have heard “house of bread” as the place where the Bread of Life enters the world.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *guest room (κατάλυμα)*: The Greek katalyma refers to a guest room in a private home — not a commercial inn. Luke uses this same word for the “upper room” of the Last Supper (22:11). The word for a commercial inn is pandocheion (\fq πανδοχεῖον\fq*), which Luke uses in 10:34. First-century Palestinian homes typically had a main room, a guest space, and a lower-level area where animals sheltered at night — with a stone feeding trough (\fq φάτνη\fq*) built into the floor. There is no innkeeper in this story; the family guest room was simply already full.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *shepherds*: First-century shepherds occupied a low social position in Palestinian society. The Mishnah classified them as generally unreliable witnesses in court (m. Sanhedrin 3:3). Their work — constant movement, night shifts in the fields, direct contact with animals — kept them from observing Sabbath and purity regulations. Cultural anthropologists (Bruce Malina, Jerome Neyrey) describe this honor-shame society: shepherds lived at the margins of what “respectable” people considered reliable. They were economically necessary but socially suspect. That God chose them as the first recipients of the most important announcement in history follows Luke's consistent pattern: the economically poor, the socially marginal, and the ritually excluded are the ones God approaches first.
[^7]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *the glory of the Lord blazed*: Modern readers often find supernatural accounts like this difficult to engage. The text assumes a worldview in which God communicates through messengers and in which divine presence takes visible form. The Hebrew concept kabod (\fq כָּבוֹד\fq*, “glory,” “weight”) referred to the manifest presence of God — the same blazing brightness that filled the tabernacle in the wilderness (Exodus 40:34-35). That light appearing outdoors, in a field, at night, to shepherds: the ancient listener would have recognized immediately that the normal order was being interrupted. Interpretations of such accounts vary: some read them as visionary or symbolic experiences; the Christian tradition takes them as real events involving genuine divine presence. How readers interpret this depends on their prior commitments about God's relationship to the world.
[^8]: **KONTEXT** — *good news (εὐαγγελίζομαι)*: The Greek euangelizomai was standard vocabulary in Roman imperial propaganda — used to announce the emperor's birth, military victories, and decrees. Inscriptions across the empire called such announcements “good news.” The angel borrows the empire's own PR vocabulary and redirects it: the truly world-changing announcement is not from the palace in Rome but from a field outside a village in occupied Judea.
[^9]: **KONTEXT** — *Savior, the Messiah, the Lord (σωτήρ, χριστὸς κύριος)*: All three titles carried counter-imperial force. “Savior” (sōtēr, \fq σωτήρ\fq*) was one of Augustus's official epithets, inscribed on monuments across the empire. “Lord” (kyrios, \fq κύριος\fq*) was used both for the emperor and for the God of Israel. The combination “the Messiah, the Lord” (christos kyrios, \fq χριστὸς κύριος\fq*) is unique in the New Testament — this precise phrase appears nowhere else. The angel is not simply announcing a birth; the announcement is a rival imperial claim, made to shepherds, about a baby in a feeding trough.
[^10]: **TEXT** — *vast army of heaven (στρατιά)*: The Greek stratia is a military term — an army, not a choir. “Heavenly host” preserves older English usage in which “host” once meant “army,” but modern readers hear “host” as “multitude” or “gathering.” The military connotation is deliberate: the Pax Romana was enforced by Rome's legions. Luke answers with God's army — not armed, but praising.
[^11]: **LEBEN** — *kept... turning them over in her heart*: Luke uses two active verbs: syneterein (συνετήρει, "she kept/guarded") and symballein (συμβάλλουσα, "pondering/turning over"). Mary does not simply remember—she guards and processes. Christian traditions have found in this response a model for engaging experiences that outpace understanding. A practice, not a posture. Things can be kept before they are explained. — A question that does not require any prior religious frame: what experience in your life are you currently trying to force into an explanation that doesn't quite fit? A relationship that ended in a way you never named. A grief that has refused to be metabolised on schedule. A piece of news that arrived too large to process. The cultural pressure to convert every experience into a coherent story is recent and not particularly healthy. Mary's response is older, slower, and stranger: keep it. Don't decide what it means yet. — For readers inside a faith, this verse anchors a recognizably contemplative practice. Teresa of Ávila described what she called recollection—holding difficult or holy things before God without forcing premature resolution. Ignatian discernment works the same way: notice the movement before you interpret it. Lectio divina assumes that the meaning of a text or an experience does not arrive in the first reading; it surfaces over time, in repeated returning. The Jesus Prayer, the breath prayer, the practice of journaling without conclusion—all stand in this lineage. — Vocationally the verse has weight for anyone whose work involves witnessing things they cannot immediately resolve—therapists, chaplains, teachers, parents, social workers, nurses. The professional pressure is to produce an interpretation, a plan, a next step. Mary suggests another posture: there are seasons in which the most faithful work is to keep, to hold, to carry. The carrying is itself the work. — Relationally, the verse names what good friendship and good marriage know: there are conversations that should not be summarised the next morning. Some things only become clear after long sitting in the company of one who will not demand they be made tidy. — This week: one thing this month you have not yet decided what to think about—let it stay undecided for one more week. Notice what shifts.
[^12]: **NAME** — *Jesus (Yeshua)*: The Aramaic/Hebrew name Yeshua (\fq ישׁוּעַ\fq*) means “YHWH saves.” The Greek form Iesous (\fq Ἰησοῦς\fq*) is a transliteration of Yeshua. The name had been given by the angel before his conception (1:31). Naming and circumcision on the eighth day — the child receiving both his identity in the covenant and his personal name in a single act. His name is his mission statement.
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *their purification*: Leviticus 12 prescribed a forty-day purification period for the mother after the birth of a male child, ending with a sacrifice at the Temple. Luke's “their” (\fq αὐτῶν\fq*) extends the obligation to the family group. The firstborn son's dedication was a separate requirement (Exod 13:2, 12–13): every firstborn male “belongs to YHWH” — a law rooted in the story of the Exodus, when the firstborn of Israel was spared.
[^14]: **KULTUR** — *turtledoves (poverty offering)*: Leviticus 12:8 specified a lamb as the primary offering after childbirth, with two turtledoves or two young pigeons as the alternative “if she cannot afford a lamb.” The text does not editorialize; it simply records what Mary and Joseph brought. The family presenting the Savior of the world at the Temple brought the offering of the poor.
[^15]: **NAME** — *Simeon (שִׁמְעוֹן)*: The Hebrew Shim'on (\fq שִׁמְעוֹן\fq*) means “the one who has been heard” or “God has heard.” His name is his biography: a man whose prayer God heard, who had been promised he would not die before the answer came.
[^16]: **KONTEXT** — *consolation of Israel (παράκλησιν)*: The Greek paraklesis (“consolation, comfort”) echoes Isaiah 40:1 — “Comfort, comfort my people” — and the prophetic tradition of God's promised restoration after exile. The consolation of Israel referred to the messianic hope: God would return to his people, end the exile of spirit that continued even after physical return from Babylon, and restore the kingdom. Simeon was among those still actively waiting for this. The same Greek root (parakletos) would later be used for the Holy Spirit as Comforter/Advocate.
[^17]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *told by the Holy Spirit*: The text presents Simeon as having received a direct communication from the Spirit — a form of prophetic revelation. For secular readers this raises questions: how does a person “receive” a message from the Spirit? What does that actually mean or feel like? The text does not explain; it simply reports what Simeon had been told. Christianity has historically believed in the activity of the Holy Spirit in communicating with people through Scripture, inner conviction, and prophetic experience. Simeon's knowing is not based on a vision or a voice in the air — it reads as settled conviction. How one categorizes that depends on prior commitments about God's engagement with the world.
[^18]: **KONTEXT** — *a sword (ῥομφαία)*: The \fq ῥομφαία\fq* was a large, heavy sword — heavier than the standard short sword (machaira). The image is deliberately violent and personal. Interpretations have included: Mary's anguish watching her son die at the cross; the division Jesus will cause that will cut through Mary's own loyalties and certainties; and the ongoing pain of a mother who does not fully understand her son (cf. v. 50). Many readers find all three dimensions present. Simeon does not explain the sword; he simply announces it. The shadow falls across the scene without resolving.
[^19]: **NAME** — *Anna (Phanuel)*: The Hebrew \fq חַנָּה\fq* (Hannah) means “grace” or “favor.” The same name as the Hannah of 1 Samuel — another woman who came to the Temple with a need God would answer. Carol Meyers (*Women in Scripture*) notes that Anna functions as a professional prophet with public religious authority, not merely a pious elderly woman. Her father's name Phanuel (\fq פְּנוּאֵל\fq*) means “face of God” — the same name as the place where Jacob wrestled with God (Gen 32:30). His daughter now sees God face to face in the child.
[^20]: **KONTEXT** — *redemption of Jerusalem (λύτρωσιν)*: The Greek lytrōsin (“redemption,” “release”) carried both political and theological weight: freedom from captivity, release from bondage, the reversal of exile and domination. Anna's audience — people waiting for Jerusalem's redemption — understood this in terms of liberation from Roman occupation and the restoration of God's people. Luke does not resolve the tension between political and spiritual redemption; both dimensions are present throughout his Gospel.
[^21]: **KULTUR** — *Passover festival (Passover pilgrimage)*: Adult Jewish males were required by Torah to attend three annual pilgrimage festivals in Jerusalem: Passover, Pentecost, and Tabernacles (Exod 23:14-17). Families often traveled together in large extended-village groups for safety, mutual support, and the social dimension of the journey. The pilgrimage was not merely a religious duty — it was a collective identity event, a yearly reconfirmation of belonging to the people of the Exodus.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *I had to (δεῖ)*: The Greek dei (\fq δεῖ\fq*, “it is necessary,” “I must”) is Jesus's first use of his mission word in Luke's Gospel. The same word marks key moments throughout Luke-Acts: “the Son of Man must suffer” (9:22), “I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God” (4:43), and his final words before the crucifixion. It is the language of divine necessity, not personal preference. This is where it begins — at age twelve, in the Temple.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *my Father's house (ἐν τοῖς τοῦ πατρός μου)*: The Greek phrase is intentionally ambiguous: “in my Father's house” (spatial — the Temple as God's dwelling) and “about my Father's affairs/business” (purposive — occupied with God's concerns) are both grammatically valid. The ambiguity may be intentional: Jesus identifies God as his Father for the first time in the Gospel, without fully clarifying what that means. Either reading places God — not Joseph — as the one to whom Jesus's primary accountability belongs. The parents do not understand (v. 50), and Luke does not resolve this for them.
[^24]: **LEBEN** — *kept all of it in her heart*: This is the second time Luke uses this phrase (cf. v. 19). The repetition is significant: Mary's posture throughout the chapter is one of guarding and pondering rather than explaining. She cannot explain the shepherds' message, cannot explain the sword Simeon announced, and now cannot explain what her twelve-year-old meant when he said he had to be in his Father's house. She keeps it. Spiritual traditions across Christianity—contemplative, charismatic, Reformed—have found in this repeated refrain a model for faith that holds unresolved mystery without forcing premature closure. — A question that lands without any religious framework: what is something a person you love has said or done that you still don't fully understand—and have you given yourself permission to leave it that way? Modern adult life trains us to demand interpretation: closure, narrative, the moral of the story. Mary models another way of being with the people closest to us. Some things they say will only make sense in twenty years. Some things may never make sense. Keeping is not failure. — For readers inside a faith, the verse opens what the contemplative tradition calls the practice of pondering—not problem-solving, not anxious rehearsing, but a slow holding before God. The Carmelite tradition (Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross) developed an entire spirituality from this kind of attention. The Eastern church names it vigilance of heart. Quaker silent meeting practices it corporately. None of these traditions treat ambiguity as failure of faith; they treat it as the condition under which faith does most of its work. — Relationally and vocationally, this verse rewrites how we might live with people in our lives whom we cannot yet understand—the adolescent child, the aging parent whose memory is changing, the colleague going through something they will not name, the friend whose decisions baffle us. The temptation is to demand explanation. The text suggests another path: keep it. Stay close. Let understanding catch up later, if it catches up at all. — Communally, the church has always known that the deepest truths of the faith are not fully explained—Trinity, incarnation, eucharist. The liturgical year is a long pondering, returning to the same mysteries every year, slowly being formed by them rather than mastering them. — This week: one conversation you have replayed in your head trying to understand—stop replaying it for seven days. See what surfaces in the silence.

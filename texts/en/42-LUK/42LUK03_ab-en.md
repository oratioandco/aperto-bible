## Kapitel 3


### A Voice in the Wilderness


**1** \x + \xo 3:1-2 \xt See Sidebar: The Political Synchronism — Why Luke Names the Powers The fifteenth year of the reign of Emperor Tiberius.[^1][^2] Pontius Pilate governing Judea. Herod tetrarch of Galilee —*a subordinate king,* ruling at Rome's pleasure. His brother Philip ruling Ituraea and Trachonitis. Lysanias tetrarch of Abilene.
**2** High priests Annas and Caiaphas.[^3] *Seven names. Seven layers of empire and occupation and compromised religion.*

And then: the word of God came\x + \xo 3:2 \xt See Sidebar: Wilderness Theology — The Desert as Sacred Space[^4] to John son of Zechariah. In the wilderness*— that emptied, listening place*.

*After generations of silence, God spoke again.*

**3** John traveled through the entire region around the Jordan, preaching a baptism of repentance[^5][^6] —*a turning —* for the forgiveness of sins.  **4** As Isaiah[^7] had written:
> A voice cries out in the wilderness:
> Prepare the way of the Lord,
> make his paths straight.
**5** \q1 Every valley shall be filled in,
> every mountain and hill brought low.
> The crooked will become straight,
> the rough ways smooth.
**6** \q1 And all flesh shall see God's salvation.

### Brood of Vipers


**7** \x + \xo 3:3-16 \xt See Sidebar: John's Baptism — How It Differed from Temple Practices Crowds streamed out to the Jordan to be baptized.*John didn't ease them in.*

“You brood of vipers![^8] *The words landed before they'd gathered themselves.* Who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?
**8** Bear fruit that shows you mean it. And don't start thinking, 'We have Abraham as our father'[^9] — God can raise up children for Abraham from these stones.
**9** The axe is already at the root of the trees. Every tree without good fruit gets cut down and thrown in the fire.”

**10** “What should we do?” the crowds asked.

**11** “If you have two coats, give one to someone who has none. If you have food, do the same.”[^10]

**12** Tax collectors came too.*These were Jews who collected revenue for Rome, presumed cheats, shut out of decent religious company. That they were here at all said something.*[^11] “Teacher, what should we do?”

**13** “Collect only what's required.*Not a penny more.*”

**14** Soldiers also asked —*Herod's troops, armed and accustomed to taking what they wanted.* “And us — what should we do?”

“Don't extort anyone. Don't bring false charges. Be content with your wages.”[^12]

### One Mightier Than I


**15** The people had begun to wonder.*Something was moving through the crowd — a question held carefully, the kind of hope that doesn't say its name out loud.* Could John be the Messiah?[^13][^14]

**16** John answered them all: “I baptize you with water. But someone mightier than I is coming. I am not worthy to untie the strap of his sandals[^15] —*a task reserved for non-Jewish slaves, beneath even what a disciple would do for his teacher.* He will baptize you with Holy Spirit and fire.[^16]
**17** His winnowing fork is*already* in his hand.*Think of the threshing floor: grain tossed into the air, the wind taking the chaff, the wheat falling back. That separation, final and complete.* He will gather the wheat into his barn. And the chaff he will burn with unquenchable fire.”

**18** With many other words, John proclaimed good news[^17] to the people.

### Herod Silences the Prophet


**19** But*Herod Antipas,* the tetrarch, had been*publicly* rebuked by John. For Herodias, his brother's wife[^18] —*a marriage that violated the law in more than one direction.* And for every other evil thing he'd done.
**20** So Herod added*one more to the list of his evils:* he locked John in prison.[^19]

*The voice was silenced.*

### Jesus Is Baptized


**21** When all the people were being baptized, Jesus was baptized too.*Not before them. Not apart from them. He stood in line with everyone else.*

Then he prayed.[^20]

Heaven opened.[^21]

**22** The Holy Spirit descended on him in bodily form, like a dove*settling*.[^22] *In bodily form — Luke alone says this, and the phrase is deliberate. Something happened that could be seen.*

A voice came from heaven: “You are my beloved Son.[^23][^24] In you I am well pleased.”[^25]\f + \fr 3:22 \fk UNGLAUBLICH \fq Jesus was baptized \ft A sinless figure submits to a baptism of repentance, the heavens open, and a voice names him Son. Modern readers find the scene strange from both sides: why does Jesus need this rite, and what kind of event is the voice from heaven?
On the believing reading the heavens really open. The Trinitarian moment Christians have always heard here — the Father speaking, the Son standing in the water, the Spirit descending — is taken on its own terms: a single, irreducible event in which God names a beloved Son. Luke is doing careful theological architecture: Jesus stands in the line with everyone else — the tax collectors, the soldiers, the crowds of chapter 3 — and the heavens open over the one who refused to stand apart. The dove echoes the Spirit hovering over the waters of Genesis 1; the voice quotes Psalm 2 and Isaiah 42. From the second century onward, in a long arc that runs through the Cappadocian fathers to twentieth-century baptismal theology gathered at Lima, Christians have read this as the disclosure of who Jesus had always been — and as the pattern of every baptism since: water, Spirit, voice naming a beloved child. The scene is not Jesus needing repentance; it is the Son taking his place inside the human story so completely that the heavens cannot help but answer.
For the reader who can't engage the open heaven as event, the scene still works as Luke's most precise theological signature. The man who refused to stand apart is the one over whom the sky opens — solidarity precedes glory. As image, the passage describes a vocation that begins in being named before it does anything, and that pattern of identity-before-task is something a reader can hear without committing to its metaphysics.
And for the reader who can't take even that, critical historians — E. P. Sanders, John Meier, the early Bart Ehrman — regard the baptism itself as among the most plausibly historical episodes in the Gospel tradition: early Christians had no reason to invent a scene that placed their Lord under John's authority and inside a rite for sinners. The voice and the dove are a different question; comparative religion catalogues many divine-call narratives, and Mark's terse \it „he saw" (Mk 1,10) suggests an inward vision later narrated outward. You don't have to take the heavens literally to keep reading.
The text stages an opening — sky, voice, dove — and then closes, leaving a man dripping on the bank. How far you are willing to follow into what was named there is what the scene asks of you next.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *Tiberius*: Tiberius Claudius Nero (r. AD 14–37). In many provinces of the empire he was worshipped as a divine figure — the emperor the Roman world called a god. Luke names him first before pivoting to the God who actually speaks.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *fifteenth year*: Luke's synchronism — six officials named, seven time markers — is ancient historiographical practice for anchoring events precisely. Pontius Pilate as governor is confirmed by the Pilate Stone discovered at Caesarea Maritima (1961). Lysanias as tetrarch of Abilene, once thought anachronistic, is now attested by a Greek inscription from the region. The fifteen-year marker places John's ministry in 28/29 CE — consistent with Josephus (Antiquities 18.5.2) and archaeological evidence. Luke is doing journalism, not mythology.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *Annas and Caiaphas*: Jewish law prescribed one high priest. Annas served AD 6–15 but was deposed by Rome; his son-in-law Caiaphas held the official title (AD 18–36). Both wielded real power — a portrait of compromised religious authority under occupation.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *word of God came (ῥῆμα θεοῦ)*: The exact LXX prophetic commissioning formula — used at the opening of Jeremiah (1:1), Ezekiel (1:3), Hosea, Joel, and others. After 400 years of prophetic silence, God speaks again. Not in Rome. Not in Jerusalem. In the wilderness.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *repentance (μετάνοια)*: Not guilt, but orientation — a complete turning of direction. John wasn't preaching emotional remorse. He was calling people to change the direction of their lives, which in first-century Palestine carried concrete social and economic implications.
[^6]: **LEBEN** — *repentance*: Christian traditions have understood this turning differently, and each offers something. For Catholics and Orthodox, repentance is sacramental—externalised in confession, absolution, acts of restitution. For Protestants, it is interior—a change of mind that produces changed behaviour. For Anabaptist and liberation traditions, repentance is structural—it requires changing one's relationship to power, money, and the marginalised. John's audience in this passage includes crowds (lifestyle), tax collectors (money), and soldiers (power). The text seems to say: all three. Metanoia is a movement word in Greek, not a feeling word. — A question that works without any theological framework: where in your life are you currently driving into a dead end you have already recognised, without moving the wheel? A relationship that has consisted of repetition for months. A habit that drains you without your quite knowing why. A professional track you have stopped believing in. Repentance in Luke's sense doesn't begin with regret. It begins with the turn. — For readers inside a faith, the verse opens an old practice. The Ignatian examen—a five-hundred-year-old exercise that takes ten minutes—follows exactly this double movement: a stocktaking (where did I turn off?) and a concrete reorientation (what is the next step?). The Eastern Christian metanoia-bow—physical prostration as the visible confession of the turn—understands repentance as a movement of the whole person, not only the head. Lent in the Western liturgical year, Yom Kippur in the Jewish calendar, the practice of fasting before Easter in the Orthodox church—all stand in the same line: turning needs form, or it evaporates. — Relationally, repentance has an edge that theological language tends to soften. Leaving a dead end sometimes means leaving people who live in it. Ending a harmful relationship, dissolving a professional complicity, exiting a circle in which everyone keeps each other small—this is not always greeted with applause. Luke does not hide this: John's preaching of metanoia eventually costs him his head. — Civically, structural repentance—as a society, a church, an institution—is the heaviest form. The German churches after 1945, the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, the contemporary reckoning with clerical abuse, the post-colonial work of churches that funded slavery: each of these shows how much longer institutional turning takes than personal, and how uncomfortable it is. — This week: one small concrete movement—not the grand resolution, but the first step out of a dead end you have been seeing for months.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *Isaiah*: Luke quotes Isaiah 40:3–5 more fully than Matthew or Mark, including the universal climax: “all flesh shall see God's salvation” (v.6). This is Luke's theological thesis. The chapter opens naming seven rulers in a specific corner of empire; it closes with every human being everywhere.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *brood of vipers (γεννήματα ἐχιδνῶν)*: Literally “offspring of vipers.” Not merely an insult — an accusation of origin. Children of the ancient serpent (Genesis 3), children of deception rather than of Abraham. Standard prophetic invective, designed to shatter complacency before it offers hope.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *Abraham as our father*: Many in first-century Judaism believed Abrahamic lineage provided a kind of divine safety net — ethnic privilege as spiritual insurance. John's counter — “God can raise up children for Abraham from these stones” — demolishes that assumption. The covenant continues through transformed lives, not inherited bloodlines.
[^10]: **LEBEN** — *two coats*: This is the simplest ethic in the New Testament—and one of the hardest. John doesn't call for revolution or legislation. He points to the specific surplus in hand: two coats when someone nearby has none. Christian traditions across centuries have applied this differently—Catholic social teaching with its solidarity and preferential option for the poor, Quaker simplicity (live at the level of genuine need, not comfort), Protestant stewardship that holds material goods loosely, the Anabaptist tradition of community of goods, monastic poverty rules from Benedict and Francis. All share a conviction: property is not absolute; it stands under the claim of the neighbour. — A question, before any theology: what is your second coat today? The answer is rarely dramatic. It is the third jacket that has not been worn for two winters. It is the money in the account that simply sits there. It is the hour on Sunday afternoon you regularly spend on something that helps no one, including yourself. John doesn't have a revolutionary economics. He runs an inventory of the surplus each of us carries. — For readers inside a faith, the verse is one of the most direct ethical instructions in the New Testament. John Chrysostom wrote in a sermon that has been quoted ever since: the coat hanging in your closet belongs to the naked. The Israeli prophetic tradition knew no separation between piety and social justice—repentance shows itself in the concrete distribution of resources. The Wesleyan covenant, the Quaker testimony of simplicity, the Catholic Worker movement under Dorothy Day, modern church food banks and clothing drives—all read this verse the same way. — Structurally the verse has weight. Today's global inequality—a third of humanity lives in conditions that make the second coat a distant concept—is the background of any honest reading. Movements like the Sojourners community, Catholic Relief Services, Christian Aid, and ecumenical movements like Bread for the World have read this verse structurally: not only my own second coat, but the architecture of a society in which some have none. — Vocationally the verse asks about our work. Professions that organize distribution—accountants, policy makers, NGO workers, social workers, teachers in under-resourced schools—are the inheritors of John's question inside our own structures. — This week: one specific item—one, not the donation drive—out of your closet this week, into the hands of someone who can use it.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *tax collectors (τελῶναι)*: Local toll collectors — Jews who subcontracted Roman revenue collection, despised as collaborators and exploiters of their own people. In honor-shame culture (Bruce Malina), they were social and religious outcasts. Their presence among those seeking baptism was genuinely surprising.
[^12]: **LEBEN** — *Be content with your wages*: John's answer to those with coercive power is not "give it up" but "don't abuse it." Three prohibitions—extortion, false accusation, general shakedown—describe the specific temptations of institutional power in any era. The application transfers readily: authority in any form (management, law enforcement, bureaucracy, medicine, parenting) faces the same temptations: leveraging power for personal gain, misusing information to harm, bending rules for preference. John doesn't moralise. He names the specific distortions and demands they stop. — A question that does not require any prior commitment: where in your life do you have power you don't usually think of as power? The vendor relationship in which your contract decision can make or break someone's quarter. The team you manage. The classroom you teach. The patient or client whose wellbeing depends on your attention that day. Most adults underestimate the weight they carry in other people's lives. John's three negatives are not abstract; they describe small daily distortions that nobody else might notice. — For readers inside a faith, the verse opens what Christian ethics has called the discipline of position. Catholic social teaching speaks of the social mortgage on power—every authority is held in trust, not as personal possession. The Reformed tradition's insistence on vocational integrity, the Anabaptist suspicion of institutional power, the Catholic worker movement's insistence on dignity in labour relations—all are downstream of John's instruction. The Ignatian examen, applied to a working day, asks the same questions John asks: where today did I use my position to lift, and where to lean? — Vocationally the verse is precise. Burnout in helping professions—teachers, nurses, social workers, clergy—often comes not from the work itself but from being squeezed between the right thing and an institutional incentive that pulls the other way. John's framing offers some relief: faithfulness in this passage is not heroic transformation; it is the daily refusal of the small distortions. — Communally, the church has had to learn this slowly. The recent reckonings with clergy abuse, with financial misconduct, with covered-up institutional violence—all have been forms of public confession that the three sins John names had not been taken seriously inside the institution that preaches them. — Civically, the verse names what every healthy democracy depends on: people in power who decline the small abuses long before they ever face a large one. — This week: one specific situation in which you hold quiet power—notice it, name it, and ask whether the smallest distortion is creeping in.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *Messiah (χριστός)*: “The Anointed One” — the king God had promised, who would liberate Israel and bring justice. Under Roman occupation, longing for the Messiah was especially intense, and John was a figure who matched no previous category.
[^14]: **LEBEN** — *hope that doesn't say its name out loud*: The crowd's unspoken question—"is this the one?"—is more universal than its Jewish context suggests. Longing for justice to come, for things to be set right, for someone to break through—this is human in any era. Political disappointment, institutional failure, personal suffering all feed the same silent question. What the text adds: even genuine longing can misidentify its object. The crowd is right to hope; they have the wrong candidate. Hope must learn discernment, not merely passion. — A question that works without any theological frame: what are you currently hoping for that you have not said out loud—maybe not even to yourself? The promotion. The reconciliation. The diagnosis to be wrong. The grief to lift. The country to right itself. There is a particular kind of hope that lives below the surface because saying it out loud would make it too vulnerable. The crowd around John carries that kind. The verse honours it without rushing to solve it. — For readers inside a faith, this is the precise location where discernment is learned. Ignatian discernment of spirits, the Carmelite tradition of dark night, the Jewish practice of waiting through the seasons of dryness, the contemplative discipline of staying in the question—all are responses to the situation in which we know we are hoping for something but cannot quite name what. The Daily Office, prayed through dry seasons, is a structural response: keep showing up, even when the hope cannot articulate itself. — Vocationally the verse cuts close. Many of us are working in fields that promised something they have not delivered—academia, journalism, medicine, the church itself. The unspoken question becomes: am I in the right place? Often the right answer is not to leave but to learn discernment about what one was actually hoping for, and to grieve the version that will not arrive. — Communally, the church carries this question structurally. Most generations of Christians have hoped for renewal, return, awakening. Some of those hopes have been answered in unexpected forms; many have been answered with patience instead of arrival. The desert fathers, the early Methodist class meetings, the Iona community in our era have all known: hope that survives needs companions. — Politically, the verse warns against political messianism. Every era's misplaced hopes—on a leader, a movement, a single election—belong to the same family as the question the crowd asks John. Discernment is not cynicism; it is hope that has learned to ask. — This week: one hope you have been carrying in silence—write it down, just for yourself. Not to solve it. Just to name it.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *sandal strap*: Removing sandals was the work of non-Jewish household slaves — even disciples wouldn't do it for their teacher (Craig Keener). John's self-placement below this threshold is extreme: he isn't even the lowest servant in the household of the Coming One.
[^16]: **KONTEXT** — *Holy Spirit and fire*: Different traditions emphasize different aspects: Pentecostal — empowering fire (Acts 2:3). Catholic and Orthodox — purifying fire of sanctification. Protestant — judging fire of divine wrath on sin. Luke likely intends purification and judgment together, with Pentecost as partial fulfillment and final judgment as completion.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *good news (εὐηγγελίζετο)*: Luke calls John's harsh preaching “gospel” — the same word used for Jesus' ministry. Warning and hope are inseparable: even talk of axes and unquenchable fire serves the goal of rescue. God speaks in judgment because God desires to save.
[^18]: **KULTUR** — *Herodias*: This marriage violated Torah on multiple counts: Herodias had divorced her first husband Philip (a woman initiating divorce was nearly unprecedented), married his half-brother while Philip still lived (adultery), and entered a union the law classified as incestuous (Leviticus 18:16). John's rebuke was not moralizing — it was legal indictment of a reigning ruler.
[^19]: **LEBEN** — *locked John in prison*: Luke records John's imprisonment without drama or explanation—just a sentence, then silence. The Greek word for witness (μάρτυς) eventually became "martyr" because those who spoke truth to power so often paid for it. Church history across every tradition is populated with figures who named what shouldn't be named and were silenced—Bonhoeffer, Oscar Romero, the martyrs of early Christianity and modern persecution alike. The text doesn't say faithfulness guarantees safety. It says faithfulness is worth it anyway. — A question that does not require any theological commitment: where in your life are you currently being asked to say something true that will cost something? Not abstract truth. Concrete: the workplace conversation you keep deferring. The family dynamic everyone knows is broken but no one will name. The community in which one specific honest sentence would change the temperature of every subsequent meeting. John's imprisonment is the extreme version of an experience most adults know in smaller forms: speaking the true thing has a price tag. — For readers inside a faith, the verse opens what Christian formation has always called the discipline of witness. The Quaker tradition's testimony of integrity, the Anabaptist refusal of oaths to power, the Catholic and Orthodox practice of patron saints whose stories are remembered precisely as costly truth-telling, the Protestant tradition of the prophet pastor—all are forms of the same intuition. Witness is not a temperament; it is a practice. — Vocationally the verse is heavy for journalists, lawyers, doctors, whistleblowers, scientists working at the edge of inconvenient truth, anyone whose professional integrity is currently in tension with institutional pressure. The history of medical ethics whistleblowing, of investigative journalism, of scientific integrity in the face of corporate or state pressure, is a long inheritance from the same impulse John acted on. — Communally and civically, the church has not always been on the right side of this verse. Sometimes the institutional church has been the prison rather than the prisoner. The recent histories of churches under apartheid, under fascist regimes, under colonial powers, in the cover-up of abuse, are histories in which the church needed its own John the Baptists from inside its own walls. The verse is sobering for any institution that finds itself comfortable with power. — Forward motion: the text does not promise vindication on a schedule. It does promise that what is spoken truly is not lost. The river of witness that begins with John flows on—uncomfortably, costly, alive. — This week: one true sentence you have been deferring—write it down for yourself first. Speak it out loud only when you are ready. Notice what shifts.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *while he was praying (προσευχομένου)*: Only Luke notes that Jesus was praying at his baptism. This is characteristic of Luke's Gospel: Jesus prays before choosing the Twelve (6:12), before Peter's confession (9:18), at the Transfiguration (9:28–29), in Gethsemane (22:41–44). In Luke's telling, heaven opens and God acts in the context of prayer.
[^21]: **KONTEXT** — *heaven opened*: An apocalyptic signal from Jewish literature (Ezekiel 1:1). Isaiah 64:1 had pleaded: “Oh, that you would tear open the heavens and come down!” Luke presents Jesus' baptism as God's answer to that ancient prayer.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *in bodily form (σωματικῷ εἴδει)*: Luke's unique phrase. Matthew and Mark simply say “like a dove.” Luke insists: this was visible, physical — not merely inner experience or symbolic vision. Something happened that could be seen.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *beloved Son (ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός)*: The heavenly voice simultaneously echoes three Old Testament passages: Psalm 2:7 (royal Messiah — “You are my Son”), Genesis 22:2 (the beloved Isaac, offered up), and Isaiah 42:1 (the suffering Servant). Jesus is at once King, sacrifice, and servant. His identity and his vocation arrive together in this single word: beloved.
[^24]: **LEBEN** — *You are my beloved Son*: This voice comes before Jesus has done anything. No miracles yet. No sermon. No disciples. Before any achievement, a declaration: beloved. The sequence matters: identity first, then vocation. Christian traditions have drawn different things from this—the mystical tradition (Meister Eckhart, Thomas Merton) understands it as a disclosure of what is already true of humanity restored in Christ; the contemplative tradition reads it as a pattern for spiritual life—receiving before striving; the Reformation tradition emphasises that this declaration comes by divine gift, not human merit. All agree on the direction: from God to person, not the reverse. — A question that works without any prior religious frame: how much of your sense of being okay in the world is tied to what you have produced this month? The deal closed. The article published. The good parenting moment. The grade. The compliment. There is a way of living adult life in which one is only worth what one has most recently delivered—and most of us know this exhaustion. The voice at Jesus' baptism describes the inverse architecture: belovedness comes first, before output. Even for those who do not believe in any specific theology, the question is worth sitting with: what would it mean to start the day from being held rather than from performing? — For readers inside a faith, this verse is the centre of an entire spiritual practice. Henri Nouwen wrote almost obsessively about it: the temptation to define oneself by what one does, and the slow daily relearning that one is beloved before one is competent. Centring prayer, the Jesus prayer, the Quaker silent meeting, the Ignatian colloquy with the Father—all are forms in which the practitioner trains to receive this voice rather than to earn it. — Vocationally the verse is medicine. The professions most prone to identity-fusion with output—academics, clergy, doctors, parents, founders—are the ones for which this verse most needs to be daily bread. Burnout is rarely about workload alone; it is about identity collapse when the output dries up. The text suggests another foundation: identity is given, not constructed. — Communally, the church has always known that baptism is the sacrament of this verse. In Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, and most Reformed traditions, baptism is the moment in which the same declaration is spoken over each Christian: you are my beloved. Many traditions return liturgically to this regularly, especially at Easter and on the feast of the Baptism of the Lord, because the voice needs to be heard again. — This week: one morning, before reaching for the phone, sit for two minutes with the sentence "I am beloved before I have produced anything today"—and notice what tries to argue with it.
[^25]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *voice from heaven*: Modern readers often struggle with divine voices. The text assumes God communicates directly with humans — not as metaphor but as historical event. The range of interpretations: inner spiritual experience made vivid through narrative; literary device to convey theological truth about Jesus' identity; objective divine speech (the Jewish bat qol was an accepted mode of post-prophetic divine communication). The text presents it as witnessed event. How one receives this depends on prior assumptions about whether God acts in history.
[^26]: **KULTUR** — *thirty years old*: Thirty was the traditional age of entry into public service: David became king at thirty (2 Samuel 5:4), Joseph entered Pharaoh's service at thirty (Genesis 41:46), Levites began temple ministry at thirty (Numbers 4:3). Jesus begins at the properly mature age.
[^27]: **TEXT** — *known as (ὡς ἐνομίζετο)*: The Greek verb means “was legally reckoned” — not “everyone guessed” but “was legally recognized as.” Joseph was Jesus' legal father. Luke quietly holds the tension between legal paternity and divine conception without resolving it here.
[^28]: **TEXT** — *Zerubbabel*: Led the return from Babylonian exile (Ezra 3:2). A Davidic heir, seen by prophets as a potential messianic figure (Haggai 1:1; Zechariah 4). His inclusion links Jesus to Israel's post-exilic restoration hope.
[^29]: **KONTEXT** — *Nathan*: Luke traces the Davidic line through Nathan, David's son, while Matthew follows the royal line through Solomon. Different ancient traditions preserved different genealogical records. Both Gospel writers affirm Jesus' Davidic descent — essential for messianic identity — arriving there through different routes.
[^30]: **KONTEXT** — *Abraham*: Matthew's genealogy ends here — Jesus as fulfillment of Jewish promises. Luke continues past Abraham to Adam, universalizing the scope. Matthew presents Jesus the Jewish Messiah; Luke presents Jesus the Savior of all humanity. Both emphases are essential.
[^31]: **TEXT** — *Noah*: The line reaches past the flood — back before nations were divided, before the judgment of Babel. Noah was the ancestor of all surviving humanity.
[^32]: **KONTEXT** — *Adam*: The genealogy culminates in Adam — not Abraham, not Noah, but the first human being. Luke's Jesus belongs to the entire human family. What was broken at the beginning (Genesis 3) finds in Jesus the one through whom restoration comes.
[^33]: **TEXT** — *son of God (τοῦ θεοῦ)*: Adam was created directly by God (Genesis 2:7) and is thus “son of God” by creation. Luke creates an inclusion: the heavenly voice just declared Jesus “my beloved Son” (v.22), and now the genealogy traces back to Adam “son of God.” Both are sons of God — but Jesus is Son in the deepest and final sense.
[^34]: **LEBEN** — *son of Adam, son of God*: The genealogy ends here—not with a title, not with a doxology, just two short phrases: son of Adam, son of God. The theological weight is available to anyone willing to stand in it: every human being is descended from the one Luke just connected to God. Human dignity in this reading is not earned, not conferred by merit or achievement or belonging to the right group. It is structural. The Imago Dei—the image of God—belongs to the species. This is why Christian traditions across every era have found in this text a basis for care for the poor, for human rights, for opposition to slavery and exploitation: not sentiment, but genealogy. — A question that does not require any prior religious commitment: which categories of person do you, in practice, treat as if they were less human than you? The honest inventory is rarely flattering. The political opponent. The criminal. The addict. The far-rich and the far-poor. The person whose worldview makes them feel alien. Most of us carry quiet hierarchies of dignity that we would deny if asked. Luke's genealogy puts a stop to the entire architecture: if everyone descends from Adam, and Adam from God, the hierarchy has nothing to stand on. — For readers inside a faith, this verse is the foundation of one of the church's longest and most uneven witnesses. Bartolomé de las Casas argued from this same theological line that indigenous peoples were fully human under God—against the colonial church of his own day. The abolitionist movement on both sides of the Atlantic, from Quaker and Methodist roots, drew on the same conviction. The civil rights movement in the United States, with Martin Luther King Jr. and the Black church tradition, made it explicit. So have the Catholic and Protestant theologians of liberation from Latin America (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Leonardo Boff). — Vocationally the verse cuts at the everyday architecture of work. Hiring, pay equity, who gets credit, whose voice fills the room, whose suffering is allowed to interrupt the meeting—each of these is a small enactment of either the genealogy Luke offers or its denial. — Communally and civically, the church has not always been on the right side of this verse. Some of the institutional church's worst chapters—participation in slavery, in colonial expropriation, in residential schools, in silence during genocide—were chapters in which this verse was apparently forgotten. Reading it now is also a form of recommitment. — This week: one category of person you have been quietly writing off—someone in the news, in your neighbourhood, in your workplace. Spend five minutes considering them as a descendant of the same first parents Luke just named.

## Kapitel 3


### A Voice in the Wilderness


**1** \x + \xo 3:1-2 \xt See Sidebar: The Political Synchronism — Why Luke Names the Powers The fifteenth year of the reign of Emperor Tiberius.[^1][^2] Pontius Pilate governing Judea. Herod tetrarch of Galilee —*a subordinate king,* ruling at Rome's pleasure. His brother Philip ruling Ituraea and Trachonitis. Lysanias tetrarch of Abilene.
**2** High priests Annas and Caiaphas.[^3] *Seven names. Seven layers of empire and occupation and compromised religion.*

And then: the word of God came\x + \xo 3:2 \xt See Sidebar: Wilderness Theology — The Desert as Sacred Space[^4] to John son of Zechariah. In the wilderness*— that emptied, listening place*.

*After generations of silence, God spoke again.*

**3** John traveled through the entire region around the Jordan, preaching a baptism of repentance[^5][^6] —*a turning —* for the forgiveness of sins.  **4** As Isaiah[^7] had written:
> A voice cries out in the wilderness:
> Prepare the way of the Lord,
> make his paths straight.
**5** \q1 Every valley shall be filled in,
> every mountain and hill brought low.
> The crooked will become straight,
> the rough ways smooth.
**6** \q1 And all flesh shall see God's salvation.

### Brood of Vipers


**7** \x + \xo 3:3-16 \xt See Sidebar: John's Baptism — How It Differed from Temple Practices Crowds streamed out to the Jordan to be baptized.*John didn't ease them in.*

“You brood of vipers![^8] *The words landed before they'd gathered themselves.* Who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?
**8** Bear fruit that shows you mean it. And don't start thinking, 'We have Abraham as our father'[^9] — God can raise up children for Abraham from these stones.
**9** The axe is already at the root of the trees. Every tree without good fruit gets cut down and thrown in the fire.”

**10** “What should we do?” the crowds asked.

**11** “If you have two coats, give one to someone who has none. If you have food, do the same.”[^10]

**12** Tax collectors came too.*These were Jews who collected revenue for Rome, presumed cheats, shut out of decent religious company. That they were here at all said something.*[^11] “Teacher, what should we do?”

**13** “Collect only what's required.*Not a penny more.*”

**14** Soldiers also asked —*Herod's troops, armed and accustomed to taking what they wanted.* “And us — what should we do?”

“Don't extort anyone. Don't bring false charges. Be content with your wages.”[^12]

### One Mightier Than I


**15** The people had begun to wonder.*Something was moving through the crowd — a question held carefully, the kind of hope that doesn't say its name out loud.* Could John be the Messiah?[^13][^14]

**16** John answered them all: “I baptize you with water. But someone mightier than I is coming. I am not worthy to untie the strap of his sandals[^15] —*a task reserved for non-Jewish slaves, beneath even what a disciple would do for his teacher.* He will baptize you with Holy Spirit and fire.[^16]
**17** His winnowing fork is*already* in his hand.*Think of the threshing floor: grain tossed into the air, the wind taking the chaff, the wheat falling back. That separation, final and complete.* He will gather the wheat into his barn. And the chaff he will burn with unquenchable fire.”

**18** With many other words, John proclaimed good news[^17] to the people.

### Herod Silences the Prophet


**19** But*Herod Antipas,* the tetrarch, had been*publicly* rebuked by John. For Herodias, his brother's wife[^18] —*a marriage that violated the law in more than one direction.* And for every other evil thing he'd done.
**20** So Herod added*one more to the list of his evils:* he locked John in prison.[^19]

*The voice was silenced.*

### Jesus Is Baptized


**21** When all the people were being baptized, Jesus was baptized too.*Not before them. Not apart from them. He stood in line with everyone else.*

Then he prayed.[^20]

Heaven opened.[^21]

**22** The Holy Spirit descended on him in bodily form, like a dove*settling*.[^22] *In bodily form — Luke alone says this, and the phrase is deliberate. Something happened that could be seen.*

A voice came from heaven: “You are my beloved Son.[^23][^24] In you I am well pleased.”[^25][^26]

### Jesus's Family Tree


**23** Jesus was about thirty years old[^27] when he began. He was known as the son of Joseph.[^28]

*Before moving forward, Luke looks backward. He traces the line — not from Abraham forward to an heir, but in reverse: through kings, through patriarchs, past the flood, all the way to the first human being. A genealogy that makes a claim: Jesus belongs not only to Israel's story, but to every human story ever told.*

Joseph was son of Heli,

**29** son of Joshua, son of Eliezer, son of Jorim, son of Matthat, son of Levi,

**31** son of Melea, son of Menna, son of Mattatha, son of Nathan,[^30] son of David,

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *Tiberius*: Tiberius Claudius Nero (r. AD 14–37). In many provinces of the empire he was worshipped as a divine figure — the emperor the Roman world called a god. Luke names him first before pivoting to the God who actually speaks.
[^2]: **APOLOGETICS** — *fifteenth year*: Luke's synchronism — six officials named, seven time markers — is ancient historiographical practice for anchoring events precisely. Pontius Pilate as governor is confirmed by the Pilate Stone discovered at Caesarea Maritima (1961). Lysanias as tetrarch of Abilene, once thought anachronistic, is now attested by a Greek inscription from the region. The fifteen-year marker places John's ministry in 28/29 CE — consistent with Josephus (Antiquities 18.5.2) and archaeological evidence. Luke is doing journalism, not mythology.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *Annas and Caiaphas*: Jewish law prescribed one high priest. Annas served AD 6–15 but was deposed by Rome; his son-in-law Caiaphas held the official title (AD 18–36). Both wielded real power — a portrait of compromised religious authority under occupation.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *word of God came (ῥῆμα θεοῦ)*: The exact LXX prophetic commissioning formula — used at the opening of Jeremiah (1:1), Ezekiel (1:3), Hosea, Joel, and others. After 400 years of prophetic silence, God speaks again. Not in Rome. Not in Jerusalem. In the wilderness.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *repentance (μετάνοια)*: Not guilt, but orientation — a complete turning of direction. John wasn't preaching emotional remorse. He was calling people to change the direction of their lives, which in first-century Palestine carried concrete social and economic implications.
[^6]: **LEBEN** — *repentance*: Christian traditions have understood this turning differently, and each offers something. For Catholics and Orthodox, repentance is sacramental — externalized in confession, absolution, acts of restitution. For Protestants, it is interior — a change of mind that produces changed behavior. For Anabaptist and liberation traditions, repentance is structural — it requires changing one's relationship to power, money, and the marginalized. John's audience in this passage includes crowds (lifestyle), tax collectors (money), and soldiers (power). The text seems to say: all three.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *Isaiah*: Luke quotes Isaiah 40:3–5 more fully than Matthew or Mark, including the universal climax: “all flesh shall see God's salvation” (v.6). This is Luke's theological thesis. The chapter opens naming seven rulers in a specific corner of empire; it closes with every human being everywhere.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *brood of vipers (γεννήματα ἐχιδνῶν)*: Literally “offspring of vipers.” Not merely an insult — an accusation of origin. Children of the ancient serpent (Genesis 3), children of deception rather than of Abraham. Standard prophetic invective, designed to shatter complacency before it offers hope.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *Abraham as our father*: Many in first-century Judaism believed Abrahamic lineage provided a kind of divine safety net — ethnic privilege as spiritual insurance. John's counter — “God can raise up children for Abraham from these stones” — demolishes that assumption. The covenant continues through transformed lives, not inherited bloodlines.
[^10]: **LEBEN** — *two coats*: This is the simplest ethic in the New Testament — and one of the hardest. John doesn't call for revolution or legislation. He points to the specific surplus in hand: two coats when someone nearby has none. Christian traditions across centuries have applied this differently — Catholic social teaching (solidarity and the preferential option for the poor), Quaker simplicity (live at the level of genuine need, not comfort), Protestant stewardship (hold material goods loosely). The question the text raises in any era: what is the equivalent of my second coat?
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *tax collectors (τελῶναι)*: Local toll collectors — Jews who subcontracted Roman revenue collection, despised as collaborators and exploiters of their own people. In honor-shame culture (Bruce Malina), they were social and religious outcasts. Their presence among those seeking baptism was genuinely surprising.
[^12]: **LEBEN** — *Be content with your wages*: John's answer to those with coercive power is not “give it up” but “don't abuse it.” Three prohibitions — extortion, false accusation, general shakedown — describe the specific temptations of institutional power in any era. The application transfers readily: authority in any form (management, law enforcement, bureaucracy, medicine, parenting) faces the same temptations: leveraging power for personal gain, misusing information to harm, bending rules for preference. John doesn't moralize. He names the specific distortions and demands they stop.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *Messiah (χριστός)*: “The Anointed One” — the king God had promised, who would liberate Israel and bring justice. Under Roman occupation, longing for the Messiah was especially intense, and John was a figure who matched no previous category.
[^14]: **LEBEN** — *hope that doesn't say its name out loud*: The crowd's unspoken question — “is this the one?” — is more universal than its Jewish context suggests. Longing for justice to come, for things to be set right, for someone to break through — this is human in any era. Political disappointment, institutional failure, personal suffering all feed the same silent question. What the text adds: even genuine longing can misidentify its object. The crowd is right to hope; they have the wrong candidate. Hope must learn discernment, not merely passion.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *sandal strap*: Removing sandals was the work of non-Jewish household slaves — even disciples wouldn't do it for their teacher (Craig Keener). John's self-placement below this threshold is extreme: he isn't even the lowest servant in the household of the Coming One.
[^16]: **KONTEXT** — *Holy Spirit and fire*: Different traditions emphasize different aspects: Pentecostal — empowering fire (Acts 2:3). Catholic and Orthodox — purifying fire of sanctification. Protestant — judging fire of divine wrath on sin. Luke likely intends purification and judgment together, with Pentecost as partial fulfillment and final judgment as completion.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *good news (εὐηγγελίζετο)*: Luke calls John's harsh preaching “gospel” — the same word used for Jesus' ministry. Warning and hope are inseparable: even talk of axes and unquenchable fire serves the goal of rescue. God speaks in judgment because God desires to save.
[^18]: **KULTUR** — *Herodias*: This marriage violated Torah on multiple counts: Herodias had divorced her first husband Philip (a woman initiating divorce was nearly unprecedented), married his half-brother while Philip still lived (adultery), and entered a union the law classified as incestuous (Leviticus 18:16). John's rebuke was not moralizing — it was legal indictment of a reigning ruler.
[^19]: **LEBEN** — *locked John in prison*: Luke records John's imprisonment without drama or explanation — just a sentence, then silence. The Greek word for witness (μάρτυς) eventually became “martyr” because those who spoke truth to power so often paid for it. Church history across every tradition is populated with figures who named what shouldn't be named and were silenced — Bonhoeffer, Oscar Romero, the martyrs of early Christianity and modern persecution alike. The text doesn't say faithfulness guarantees safety. It says faithfulness is worth it anyway.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *while he was praying (προσευχομένου)*: Only Luke notes that Jesus was praying at his baptism. This is characteristic of Luke's Gospel: Jesus prays before choosing the Twelve (6:12), before Peter's confession (9:18), at the Transfiguration (9:28–29), in Gethsemane (22:41–44). In Luke's telling, heaven opens and God acts in the context of prayer.
[^21]: **KONTEXT** — *heaven opened*: An apocalyptic signal from Jewish literature (Ezekiel 1:1). Isaiah 64:1 had pleaded: “Oh, that you would tear open the heavens and come down!” Luke presents Jesus' baptism as God's answer to that ancient prayer.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *in bodily form (σωματικῷ εἴδει)*: Luke's unique phrase. Matthew and Mark simply say “like a dove.” Luke insists: this was visible, physical — not merely inner experience or symbolic vision. Something happened that could be seen.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *beloved Son (ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός)*: The heavenly voice simultaneously echoes three Old Testament passages: Psalm 2:7 (royal Messiah — “You are my Son”), Genesis 22:2 (the beloved Isaac, offered up), and Isaiah 42:1 (the suffering Servant). Jesus is at once King, sacrifice, and servant. His identity and his vocation arrive together in this single word: beloved.
[^24]: **LEBEN** — *You are my beloved Son*: This voice comes before Jesus has done anything. No miracles yet. No sermon. No disciples. Before any achievement, a declaration: beloved. The sequence matters: identity first, then vocation. Christian traditions have drawn different things from this — the mystical tradition (Meister Eckhart, Thomas Merton) understands it as a disclosure of what is already true of humanity restored in Christ; the contemplative tradition reads it as a pattern for spiritual life — receiving before striving; the Reformation tradition emphasizes that this declaration comes by divine gift, not human merit. All agree on the direction: from God to person, not the reverse.
[^25]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *voice from heaven*: Modern readers often struggle with divine voices. The text assumes God communicates directly with humans — not as metaphor but as historical event. The range of interpretations: inner spiritual experience made vivid through narrative; literary device to convey theological truth about Jesus' identity; objective divine speech (the Jewish bat qol was an accepted mode of post-prophetic divine communication). The text presents it as witnessed event. How one receives this depends on prior assumptions about whether God acts in history.
[^26]: **APOLOGETICS** — *Jesus was baptized*: The baptism of Jesus by John is considered by historians one of the most historically reliable events in the Gospel tradition, precisely because it was theologically awkward: why would the sinless Jesus undergo a baptism of repentance? This criterion of embarrassment — early Christians would have had every reason to minimize or omit it, yet all four Gospels report it — is a standard test for historical reliability (John Meier, N.T. Wright, James Dunn). Josephus independently confirms John as a historical figure (Antiquities 18.5.2). The early church struggled to explain the baptism (Matthew adds John's protest, 3:14-15), which is precisely why historians trust its historicity.
[^27]: **KULTUR** — *thirty years old*: Thirty was the traditional age of entry into public service: David became king at thirty (2 Samuel 5:4), Joseph entered Pharaoh's service at thirty (Genesis 41:46), Levites began temple ministry at thirty (Numbers 4:3). Jesus begins at the properly mature age.
[^28]: **TEXT** — *known as (ὡς ἐνομίζετο)*: The Greek verb means “was legally reckoned” — not “everyone guessed” but “was legally recognized as.” Joseph was Jesus' legal father. Luke quietly holds the tension between legal paternity and divine conception without resolving it here.
[^29]: **TEXT** — *Zerubbabel*: Led the return from Babylonian exile (Ezra 3:2). A Davidic heir, seen by prophets as a potential messianic figure (Haggai 1:1; Zechariah 4). His inclusion links Jesus to Israel's post-exilic restoration hope.
[^30]: **KONTEXT** — *Nathan*: Luke traces the Davidic line through Nathan, David's son, while Matthew follows the royal line through Solomon. Different ancient traditions preserved different genealogical records. Both Gospel writers affirm Jesus' Davidic descent — essential for messianic identity — arriving there through different routes.
[^31]: **KONTEXT** — *Abraham*: Matthew's genealogy ends here — Jesus as fulfillment of Jewish promises. Luke continues past Abraham to Adam, universalizing the scope. Matthew presents Jesus the Jewish Messiah; Luke presents Jesus the Savior of all humanity. Both emphases are essential.
[^32]: **TEXT** — *Noah*: The line reaches past the flood — back before nations were divided, before the judgment of Babel. Noah was the ancestor of all surviving humanity.
[^33]: **KONTEXT** — *Adam*: The genealogy culminates in Adam — not Abraham, not Noah, but the first human being. Luke's Jesus belongs to the entire human family. What was broken at the beginning (Genesis 3) finds in Jesus the one through whom restoration comes.
[^34]: **TEXT** — *son of God (τοῦ θεοῦ)*: Adam was created directly by God (Genesis 2:7) and is thus “son of God” by creation. Luke creates an inclusion: the heavenly voice just declared Jesus “my beloved Son” (v.22), and now the genealogy traces back to Adam “son of God.” Both are sons of God — but Jesus is Son in the deepest and final sense.
[^35]: **LEBEN** — *son of Adam, son of God*: The genealogy ends here — not with a title, not with a doxology, just two short phrases: son of Adam, son of God. The theological weight is available to anyone willing to stand in it: every human being is descended from the one Luke just connected to God. Human dignity in this reading is not earned, not conferred by merit or achievement or belonging to the right group. It is structural. The Imago Dei — the image of God — belongs to the species. This is why Christian traditions across every era have found in this text a basis for care for the poor, for human rights, for opposition to slavery and exploitation: not sentiment, but genealogy.

## Kapitel 4


### Tested in the Wilderness

\x - \xo 4:1-13 \xt See sidebar SB-LK04-01: The Temptation — Myth or Psychology?
\x - \xo 4:1-18 \xt See sidebar SB-LK04-03: Spirit Christology — Jesus as Spirit-Bearer

**1** Jesus came back from the Jordan River filled with the Holy Spirit. The Spirit led him out into the wilderness[^1] — that ancient charged space where Israel had been formed, where prophets were unmade and heard from God.
**2** *Forty days.* [^2] He ate nothing during that time. He was tested by the devil throughout — and when the forty days were over, hunger had stripped away everything else.
**3** The devil came close.[^3] “If you are the Son of God,” he said, “tell this stone to become bread.”

**4** Jesus answered: “It is written — ‘One does not live by bread alone.’” [^4]
**5** Then, in a single instant, the devil showed him all the kingdoms of the world — Rome, Parthia, every empire humans had built and bled for — compressed into one flash of vision.

**6** “All this authority,” the devil said. “All this glory. It has been given to me — I give it to anyone I choose.
**7** Bow down before me, and it’s yours.”
\p[^5]
**8** “It is written,” Jesus answered: “’Worship the Lord your God, and serve only him.’”

**9** Then the devil brought him to Jerusalem, to the parapet of the temple[^6] — the highest edge, where the valley dropped away far below. “If you are the Son of God,” he said, “throw yourself down from here.
**10** Because it is written: ‘He will command his angels concerning you, to guard you,
**11** and they will lift you up in their hands, so that you will not strike your foot against a stone.’”

**12** Even Scripture could be turned into a weapon. “It is also written,” Jesus said: “’Do not put the Lord your God to the test.’” [^7]
**13** The devil ran through every test he had. Then he left — but only until the right moment.[^8] The battle was not over.

### Return to Galilee


**14** Jesus returned to Galilee in the power of the Spirit. Word about him spread through the entire surrounding region.
**15** He taught in the synagogues, and people honored him.

### Rejected in His Hometown

**16** He came to Nazareth, where he had grown up. On the Sabbath he went to the synagogue — as he always did — and stood to read.[^12]
**17** They handed him the scroll of the prophet Isaiah — hand-copied sheepskin, the kind of text communities carried through generations.[^13] He unrolled it until he found the place. He read:
**18** “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me.[^14]

Abject destitution, not modest circumstances. The spiritualizing reading (“poor in spirit,” from Matt. 5:3) does not apply here: Luke's Jesus is announcing liberation for those materially crushed by poverty. Gustavo Gutiérrez (A Theology of Liberation) and the Latin American liberation theology tradition ground their “preferential option for the poor” precisely in the distinction between \fq πτωχός\fq* and \fq πένης\fq*.\f*

**19** \q1 to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord.”[^15]

**20** He rolled up the scroll. Gave it back to the attendant. Sat down — the teacher’s posture.[^17] Every eye in the room stayed on him.
**21** He began to speak: “Today this scripture has been fulfilled — in your hearing.”[^18]


**22** They spoke well of him at first. The words were gracious — compelling in a way that was hard to name. But then someone said, “Isn’t this Joseph’s son?”[^19] The murmur moved through the room.

**23** Jesus said to them: “You’ll say to me the old proverb: ‘Doctor, heal yourself.’ Whatever we heard you did in Capernaum — do it here too, in your hometown.”
**24** Then he said: “Truly I tell you — no prophet is acceptable in his hometown.[^20]

**25** “There were many widows in Israel in Elijah’s time, when the sky closed and no rain fell for three and a half years, and famine spread everywhere.
**26** Elijah was sent to none of them. He was sent to a widow in Zarephath — in Sidon.[^21] A Gentile woman.
**27** “And there were many lepers in Israel in Elisha’s time — and none of them were healed. Only Naaman. The Syrian.”[^22]

**28** The room erupted.

**29** They rose up together, drove him out of the town, and took him to the brow of the hill on which Nazareth stood — to throw him off. Their hands were on him.
**30** He walked through the midst of them. And walked away.

### Authority Over Evil Spirits


**31** He went down to Capernaum — a town on the lake in Galilee — and taught there on the Sabbath.[^23]
**32** The people were astonished. His teaching didn’t work the way other teachers’ teaching worked — citing this authority and that one. His words had their own weight.[^24]

**33** In the synagogue was a man with an unclean spirit[^25] — a defiling presence that kept him from fully belonging. Suddenly the man screamed:
**34** “Ha! What do you want with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are — the Holy One of God!”


**35** Jesus rebuked it: “Be silent — and come out of him.” No ritual. No invocation. Just the command.[^26] The demon flung the man to the ground, then came out of him — without hurting him.
**36** Terror settled over everyone in the place. They turned to each other: “What kind of word is this? He commands unclean spirits with authority and power — and they obey.”
**37** News about him spread through every village in the surrounding region.
[^27]

### Healing at Simon’s House


**38** Jesus left the synagogue and went to Simon’s house. Simon’s mother-in-law[^28] was burning with a high fever. They asked him about her.
**39** He stood over her and rebuked the fever[^29] — the same way he had rebuked the demon. The fever left her. She got up at once and began to serve them.
[^30]

**40** As the sun was setting[^31] — the Sabbath was ending — they brought everyone who was ill to Jesus. He laid his hands on each one of them.[^32] Not the crowd. Each person.
**41** Demons came out of many, crying out: “You are the Son of God!” He silenced them. He would not let them speak, because they knew he was the Messiah.[^33] But the moment for that announcement had not yet come.
[^34]

### The Mission Continues


**42** When day came, Jesus left. He went to a deserted place[^35][^36] — the kind of stillness the wilderness gives. The crowds searched for him. Found him. And tried to keep him from leaving.

**43** He said: “I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns as well.[^37] This is what I was sent to do.”[^38]
**44** And he moved on, proclaiming in the synagogues throughout Judea[^39] — throughout the Jewish land.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *Wilderness*: The Judean Desert — chalk hills descending toward the Dead Sea. In Jewish tradition, the wilderness was the site of divine encounter (Elijah, Moses, the Exodus formation of Israel) and demonic habitation (Lev. 16:10; Isa. 13:21). Jesus enters it not as a place of abandonment but under divine commission.
[^2]: **KONTEXT** — *Forty days*: The number echoes Israel's forty years of wilderness wandering (Num. 14:33), Moses's forty days on Sinai (Exod. 34:28), and Elijah's forty-day journey to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8). Luke places Jesus in a tradition of wilderness formation. This is recapitulation theology: Jesus does in concentrated form what Israel was given forty years to do — and where Israel repeatedly failed, Jesus holds.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *The devil (διάβολος)*: Luke uses \fq διάβολος\fq* (slanderer, accuser) throughout Luke 4, not \fq Σατανᾶς\fq* (Satan). The first-class condition “If you are” (\fq εἰ υἱὸς εἶ\fq*) does not express doubt but presents a premise as valid for argument's sake — the devil is not questioning Jesus' identity but challenging how he will use it.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *It is written (Deut. 8:3)*: Spoken after Israel's bread-complaint in the wilderness (Exod. 16). Jesus quotes the exact passage that named Israel's failure; he succeeds where the nation did not. The NA28 shorter reading ends here; the longer version (“but by every word of God”) is found in some manuscripts and in Matt. 4:4, but is likely harmonization.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *Given to me (ἐμοὶ παραδέδοται)*: The devil claims delegated authority over the world's kingdoms. Jesus neither confirms nor denies this claim. The issue is not who holds political power but whether worship belongs to God alone. Luke's Gospel consistently shows Jesus refusing the path of coercive domination (cf. 22:25-27).
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *Parapet of the temple*: The southeastern corner of the Temple Mount, overlooking the Kidron Valley. Josephus (Ant. 15.11.5) describes the height as dizzying — a sheer drop to the valley floor below. Jewish tradition expected the Messiah to appear suddenly at the Temple (Mal. 3:1). The temptation exploits this messianic expectation: perform a spectacular arrival, let the people recognize you, bypass the cross.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *Do not put the Lord to the test (Deut. 6:16)*: Spoken in reference to Israel's demand for proof at Massah (Exod. 17:1-7), where Israel tested God's care for them. Jesus refuses to manufacture a divine spectacle to confirm his identity; he received that identity at baptism and does not need to perform it.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *Until the right moment (ἄχρι καιροῦ)*: Forward-looking to the Passion narrative. The devil returns through Judas (22:3) and is explicitly present in the garden arrest (“this is your hour, the power of darkness” — 22:53). The wilderness encounter is one battle in a longer war.
[^9]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *The devil as personal adversary*: This account raises a direct question for modern readers: Is the devil a literal being — a personal agent of evil — or is this symbolic language for temptation's inner dynamic? The text uses personal language throughout: the devil speaks, leads, transports, departs. Three frames help situate the question. (1) Naturalistic-critical: Many scholars read the temptation as the dramatization of an interior spiritual crisis — the Deuteronomy quotations functioning as Jesus' internal theology, the “devil” as the personification of counter-pulls that any person committed to a radical vocation will experience. The psychological truth of the three temptation patterns (immediate satisfaction, coercive power, public spectacle) is undeniable regardless of framing. (2) Symbolic-theological: Some read the narrative as a theological statement in mythic form — the Exodus typology is the point (Israel failed in the wilderness; Jesus does not). The devil's role is structural, not necessarily ontological. (3) Historical-believing: Jesus himself speaks of the devil as a real spiritual being elsewhere in Luke (10:18, 11:15-22, 22:31). The Jewish world of the first century had a robust theology of evil as personal (Qumran texts, 1 Enoch, Testament of Job). Luke, writing in this context, presents the account as a real encounter. The text does not explain the devil's nature; it shows what happened when Jesus met that force — and what his response was.
[^10]: **KONTEXT** — *Israel's wilderness recapitulated*: The Deuteronomy quotations are not random proof-texts. Each addresses a specific failure from Israel's wilderness years. Deut. 8:3 (v. 4): Israel demanded bread at Exod. 16, receiving manna — and then complained. Deut. 6:16 (v. 12): Israel demanded proof of God's presence at Massah (Exod. 17). The implicit third quote (Deut. 6:13, v. 8) addresses Israel's idolatry at Sinai (Exod. 32). Jesus passes three tests that Israel failed over forty years — in forty days, through the word of God alone. Luke presents Jesus not merely as a righteous individual but as Israel-recapitulated: the one through whom the nation's vocation is fulfilled.
[^11]: **LEBEN** — *Recognizing temptation patterns*: Christian communities have long observed that temptation rarely announces itself as temptation. Ignatius of Loyola's Discernment of Spirits (16th c.) identified “consolation” and “desolation” as inner movements requiring interpretation — the very pattern Jesus demonstrates here: naming the offer, tracing it to its source, declining it. The three temptation patterns Luke names — immediate satisfaction (bread), coercive power, and performance for spectacle — map onto recognizable modern dynamics. Desert fathers and mothers of the 4th century called these patterns logismoi (destructive thought-patterns) and taught systematic recognition as the precondition for freedom. The tradition's consistent insight: those who can name a temptation hold power over it that those who cannot name it do not have.
[^12]: **KULTUR** — *Synagogue at Nazareth*: No confirmed first-century synagogue building has been excavated at Nazareth (Ken Dark, Archaeology of Jesus' Nazareth, Oxford 2023). The gathering likely took place in a large private room or courtyard. The synagogue (\fq συναγωγή\fq*) in the first century was an assembly — a social practice, not necessarily a dedicated building. Nazareth's population was approximately 400-1,000 (Dark 2023). This is not evidence against the account; it is consistent with the informal character of early synagogue life. Scroll ownership and reading practice in village assemblies is attested in the Mishnah (m. Meg. 4:1-5).
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *The scroll of Isaiah*: The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) from Qumran — the closest parallel — is 7.34 meters long, written on sheepskin. Reading required standing, unrolling from one side while rolling the other, finding the place, reading aloud. Luke writes that Jesus “found the place” (\fq εὗρεν τὸν τόπον\fq*) — suggesting intentional selection, not an assigned reading.
[^14]: **TEXT** — *The Spirit of the Lord is upon me (Isa. 61:1-2)*: This is a composite quotation: Isaiah 61:1-2a (LXX) combined with Isaiah 58:6 (“to liberate the oppressed”). The line “to heal the brokenhearted” (Isa. 61:1b) is absent from the best manuscripts and from Luke's quotation. Jesus stops before Isaiah 61:2b — “the day of vengeance of our God” — is deliberately not quoted. The proclamation is grace, not judgment. The Qumran scroll 11QMelchizedek (11Q13) interprets Isa. 61:1-2 through the Jubilee lens of Lev. 25, confirming this as a pre-Christian Jewish reading that understood the passage as announcing cosmic liberation.
[^15]: **KONTEXT** — *The acceptable year of the Lord*: The Jubilee year of Leviticus 25 — the fiftieth year when debts were cancelled, bonded servants freed, and land returned to ancestral owners. The Dead Sea Scroll 11QMelchizedek interprets Isaiah 61 through this Jubilee lens, confirming it as a pre-Christian Jewish reading. The word \fq δεκτόν\fq* (acceptable, welcome, favorable) will appear again at verse 24 — see note there.
[^16]: **LEBEN** — *Called and sent*: The Nazareth declaration describes a calling structured around three movements: anointing (a gift received, not claimed), mission (a direction given, not chosen), and target (those who lack, not those who prosper). Christian communities have wrestled with the relationship between vocation and career since the Reformation, when Luther's theology of Beruf (calling) relocated sacred purpose from monastery to ordinary work. Ignatian discernment asks: where do deep joy and the world's need intersect? Liberation theology asks: who is absent from the rooms where decisions are made? Both locate calling not in personal fulfillment alone but in a specific direction — toward the ones the text names: the destitute, the captive, the blind, the oppressed. The tradition has consistently found that such calling is more discovered than manufactured.
[^17]: **KULTUR** — *Sat down*: In the first-century synagogue, the reader stood and the teacher sat. Jesus' sitting signals that what follows is authoritative teaching, not merely a reading.
[^18]: **TEXT** — *Today (σήμερον)*: Placed in emphatic first position. Luke's distinctive “today” theology appears at 2:11, 5:26, 19:5, 19:9, and 23:43. This is inaugurated eschatology — God acts in the present moment, not only in past history or future expectation. The claim is staggering: not “this scripture points toward fulfillment” but “in your hearing, it is being fulfilled.”
[^19]: **KULTUR** — *Joseph's son*: An honor challenge in Mediterranean honor-shame culture (Bruce Malina, Jerome Neyrey, The Social World of Luke-Acts). “Joseph's son” is not merely identification — it is a credential check. They know his family, his origin, his economic class, his limitations. Who gave him the right to stand before them as an authoritative teacher? Hometown familiarity functions as a cap on honor: you cannot rise above your known social position.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *Acceptable (δεκτός)*: The same word used in verse 19 (“the acceptable year”). The irony is literary and deliberate: Jesus proclaims the year that is acceptable (\fq δεκτόν\fq*) before God, then discovers he himself is not acceptable (\fq δεκτός\fq*) among his own people. This echo was audible to ancient listeners and is preserved here for literary readers.
[^21]: **KONTEXT** — *Zarephath in Sidon*: See 1 Kings 17:8-16. Zarephath is in Phoenician territory — Gentile land. The widow there was on the edge of starvation when Elijah arrived and asked her for food. She gave what she had, and it was miraculously sustained. Her faith exceeded that of any Israelite widow. Luke's inclusion of these examples (Zarephath, Naaman) sets up the Acts narrative: God's mission will cross ethnic boundaries. This is not incidental — it is prophetically programmatic. The rejection at Nazareth foreshadows the turn to the Gentiles in Acts 10-11, 13:46.
[^22]: **KONTEXT** — *Naaman the Syrian*: See 2 Kings 5. Naaman was the commanding general of the Syrian army — an enemy of Israel — who was healed of leprosy by Elisha after initially resisting the prophet's instructions. He crossed ethnic and national boundaries to receive what Israel's own lepers did not. Luke pairs Elijah/Zarephath (a widow, a woman, a Gentile, economically destitute) with Elisha/Naaman (a general, a man, a Gentile, powerful). The two examples together bracket gender and class: God's reach is not limited by any human boundary.
[^23]: **KULTUR** — *Capernaum*: Unlike Nazareth, Capernaum has confirmed first-century archaeological evidence for a synagogue. Excavations beneath the 4th-5th century white limestone synagogue revealed basalt cobblestone pavement and walls from the Early Roman period — a structure too large for domestic use. The contrast with Nazareth (rejection) and Capernaum (acceptance) is narratively deliberate: outsiders receive what insiders refuse.
[^24]: **TEXT** — *Authority (ἐξουσία)*: Authority from within, not derived from tradition or academic credentials. First-century scribes taught by citing the chain of tradition (“Rabbi X said in the name of Rabbi Y”). Jesus teaches with direct, inherent authority. The same word \fq ἐξουσία\fq* appears in v. 36 when the crowd marvels at his command over demons — teaching authority and power over evil are the same authority.
[^25]: **TEXT** — *Unclean spirit (πνεῦμα δαιμονίου ἀκαθάρτου)*: Luke's distinctive double term — demon and unclean together. “Unclean” (\fq ἀκαθάρτου\fq*) signals ritual defilement: the man's condition isolated him from full community participation in the synagogue. Holy space has been invaded by a defiling presence. Luke's clinical precision (he is identified as a physician by Paul, Col. 4:14) is evident: he names the phenomenon precisely without explaining its mechanism.
[^26]: **KULTUR** — *No ritual*: Contemporary Jewish exorcists used elaborate procedures: incantations, fumigation with smelly roots, invocation of higher spiritual names (Josephus, Ant. 8.46-49). Jesus uses a single authoritative command. \fq Φιμώθητι\fq* (“be muzzled/silenced”) is a strong word — to gag something that cannot be permitted to speak. The contrast with contemporary exorcism practice would have been immediately striking to Luke's first readers.
[^27]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Exorcism*: The account of an exorcism raises immediate questions for contemporary readers. Three frames help hold the event. (1) Naturalistic-critical: Scholars in the tradition of John Hull and Graham Twelftree observe that first-century people attributed certain kinds of psychological and neurological distress to spirit possession — conditions we would today diagnose as epilepsy, severe dissociative disorders, or psychosis. The liberative result (a person freed from a condition that isolated him from community) is documentable regardless of the mechanism. Luke's description — the cry, the convulsion, the sudden resolution — has clinical precision without claiming to explain what caused it. (2) Symbolic-theological: The exorcism enacts what Jesus announced in vv. 18-19: liberation for the captive, sight for the blind. The “unclean spirit” names the forces — addictive, traumatic, structural — that hold people in captivity they cannot escape by willpower. Jesus liberates from what the self cannot resolve alone. (3) Historical-believing: Jesus himself consistently acted and taught as though evil was a personal reality that could be confronted and expelled (Luke 10:17-20, 11:14-23). The early church's continued practice of deliverance prayer across Catholic, Orthodox, and Pentecostal traditions reflects an unbroken conviction that spiritual forces of the kind Luke describes are real. The text presents the event as historical. Where readers locate the mechanism is a question the text itself leaves open.
[^28]: **KULTUR** — *Simon's mother-in-law*: Multigenerational households were the norm in first-century Galilee — caring for aging parents was expected, not optional. Simon's mother-in-law lived as part of the household. Her illness is a household crisis, not a private matter. The entire household's wellbeing depended on everyone who could work being able to work.
[^29]: **TEXT** — *Rebuked the fever (ἐπετίμησεν)*: The same verb used for the exorcism in v. 35. Luke treats the fever with the same authoritative category as the demon. Disease and evil oppression are related categories in Luke's worldview; both yield to Jesus' rebuke. On “she began to serve them” (\fq διηκόνει\fq*): this is the same verb used for Jesus' own mission (Mark 10:45; cf. Luke 22:27) and for the service of the women who followed Jesus (Luke 8:3). Beverly Gaventa and other scholars of women in Luke observe that her service represents not mere domestic return to labor but a paradigmatic act of discipleship — she is the first person in Luke's Gospel to model the proper response to healing.
[^30]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Fever rebuked like a demon*: Luke's framing here is unusual even by the Gospel's own standards: he uses the same verb for the fever (\fq ἐπετίμησεν\fq* — “rebuked”) that he used for the exorcism of the unclean spirit in v. 35. This is not accidental language — Luke is categorizing fever and demonic presence together as forces that yield to Jesus' authoritative command. For readers who accept the exorcism narrative, the parallel is illuminating: illness, in Luke's framework, is among the captivities that the Nazareth Manifesto promises to address. For readers skeptical of the exorcism, the parallel raises a different question: if the fever's departure can be understood in naturalistic terms, does that reframe the earlier account — or complicate any clean resolution?
[^31]: **KULTUR** — *As the sun was setting*: The Sabbath ended at sundown (roughly 6 pm). Under Sabbath law, carrying sick people through the streets was prohibited. The sunset marks the moment when those who had been holding back could finally bring their loved ones to Jesus.
[^32]: **TEXT** — *Each one individually (ἑνὶ ἑκάστῳ)*: Luke emphasizes that Jesus' healing was not a mass transaction but a series of personal encounters. In the midst of a crowd of the sick and suffering, each person received individual attention and touch.
[^33]: **KONTEXT** — *He silenced them*: The “messianic secret” — Jesus controls the timing and manner of his identity's disclosure. Demons confess what humans are still working out. His silence is not denial but deliberate management: he will not allow his identity to be defined by demonic proclamation, nor to become publicly fixed before the time is right.
[^34]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Mass healing and demon confessions*: The scene at sunset presents two kinds of events together: physical healings and expulsions of demons with verbal confessions of Jesus' identity. For readers who find miracle accounts difficult, it helps to note what Luke does and does not claim. He does not describe the mechanism of healing — no technique, no formula. He does describe the outcome and the response (awe). He does not resolve the question of what the demons “are” — he records what they say. The confession “You are the Son of God” is theologically loaded: the demons identify Jesus correctly when the hometown synagogue has just rejected him. Luke's literary strategy is deliberate: supernatural witnesses identify what human insiders deny. Whether one takes the demonic confessions literally or symbolically, the point lands either way.
[^35]: **TEXT** — *A deserted place (ἔρημον τόπον)*: Using the same root as \fq ἔρημος\fq* (wilderness) in 4:1. The chapter that began in wilderness ends with Jesus returning to wilderness-solitude. The rhythm of mission and withdrawal runs throughout Luke: public proclamation followed by withdrawal for prayer and renewal (cf. 5:16, 6:12, 9:18, 9:28).
[^36]: **LEBEN** — *Rhythm of withdrawal*: Withdrawal after intense activity is a recurring pattern in Jesus' life — not a reaction to exhaustion but an architecture. Luke notes it again and again (5:16; 6:12; 9:18; 22:39). It belongs to the form, not to the emergency. — A question that does not require any prior theological frame: when was the last time you were genuinely alone — not commuting with headphones, not scrolling before sleep, but in a stillness that produced nothing? Most people today have curated every waking moment. The verse invites a small experiment: clear half an hour in the calendar in which nothing has to happen. What rises in that space is often the most important information of the day. — For readers inside a faith, the scene opens a line that runs across the confessions. The desert fathers, later Benedict with his ora et labora, the Carthusians with their cell-silence, Quaker silent meeting, the Ignatian solitude that anchors the Spiritual Exercises — all stand in the same movement. Henri Nouwen, named clearly in The Way of the Heart, put it this way: those who cannot be alone cannot truly be present to others. Silence is the workshop in which presence is made. — Vocationally this has consequences. Professions of high responsibility — therapists, doctors, teachers, parents, leaders — burn out because they lack the structural silence that sustains presence. Not vacation, not wellness — the short daily withdrawal in which no one wants anything. Some monasteries still offer it as hospitality, because the world has nearly forgotten how. — Civically a society that monetises every moment loses the ability to hear its own crises before they escalate. Jesus' withdrawal is also a quiet political gesture. — This week: keep one slot in the calendar empty — no device, no plan, no voice. Listen to what begins to speak after the first ten minutes.
[^37]: **TEXT** — *I must (δεῖ)*: Divine necessity, not personal preference or inclination. The word appears at key moments in Luke-Acts to signal that an action is divinely ordained (cf. 9:22, 13:33, 17:25, 24:7, 24:26). Jesus is not moved by the crowd's affection or by his own success in Capernaum but by the prior commission of the Father.
[^38]: **KONTEXT** — *Kingdom of God*: The first explicit mention of “the kingdom of God” in Luke chapter 4. Luke uses this phrase over 30 times in his Gospel. Jesus does not define it here — he announces it. The kingdom (\fq βασιλεία\fq*) is not a territory but God's reign, breaking into history through Jesus' proclamation, healing, and exorcism. N.T. Wright (Jesus and the Victory of God) argues that “kingdom of God” was not a religious abstraction but a politically charged announcement: Israel's God is becoming king — which necessarily means Caesar is not.
[^39]: **TEXT** — *Judea (Ἰουδαίας)*: The NA28 follows the best manuscripts (P75, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) in reading “Judea” rather than “Galilee,” which appears in later manuscripts. Scribes changed “Judea” to “Galilee” to avoid apparent contradiction with 4:14. Luke, however, uses “Judea” broadly to mean the whole Jewish land, not merely the southern province (cf. 1:5, 6:17, 7:17, 23:5; Acts 10:37).

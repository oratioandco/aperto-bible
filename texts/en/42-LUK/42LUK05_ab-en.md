## Kapitel 5


### The Catch


**1** The crowd kept pressing closer. They had come to hear God's message, and they pushed toward the shore until there was nowhere left to stand.
**2** He saw two boats pulled up on the gravel, empty. The fishermen were out of them, crouching by the water, washing nets that had caught nothing.[^1]
**3** Jesus climbed into the one that belonged to Simon and asked him to push out a little from shore. He sat down in the boat and taught.
**4** When he finished, he turned to Simon. “Go out deeper. Let down your nets for a catch.”
**5** “Master —”[^2] Simon paused. *They'd fished through the darkness, the only hours worth fishing, and pulled up nothing. Every net empty. Every hour wasted.* “We've worked all night and caught nothing.” *He looked at the nets, still wet, still empty.* “But because you say so — I'll let them down.”
**6** *What happened next was not what anyone expected.* The nets filled*— not gradually but suddenly, violently*. The weight of fish pressed outward until the mesh began to tear.[^3]
**7** They waved to their partners, James and John, in the other boat. Both boats loaded until the water reached the gunwales. Both nearly sinking.
**8** Simon Peter stared at the catch. Then he fell to his knees in the bottom of the boat, *among the fish and the water and the torn nets,* and said: “Get away from me, Lord. I'm a sinful man*— I'm not fit to be near you*.”[^4]
**9** *It wasn't gratitude. It wasn't wonder.* Awe had seized everyone on those two boats*— not excitement, not relief. Fear. The kind that comes when you realize you are kneeling in the presence of something you were never meant to be this close to*.[^5]
**10** Jesus looked at him. \p
“Don't be afraid. From now on, you'll be catching people alive.”[^6]
**11** They brought the boats in. Left the catch on the shore — *the biggest haul any of them had ever seen, still gleaming and gasping in the hulls.* Left the nets, the boats, the partnerships, the livelihood, everything. And followed him.

### Touching the Untouchable


**12** In one of the towns — a man. Covered with leprosy.[^7] *He shouldn't have been there at all. The disease meant exile: from family, from worship, from touch. For years he'd announced his own contamination to anyone who came near.*
**13** He saw Jesus and fell face-down on the ground. “If you're willing — you can make me clean.” *Not a question of power. A question of willingness. He had stopped expecting anyone to be willing.*



**14** Jesus told him to tell no one. “Go to the priest. Offer what Moses commanded — as evidence for them.”[^8] *The same system that had declared him unfit would now have to certify his restoration.*[^9]
**15** *He didn't keep quiet.* The news spread. Crowds came — to hear, to be healed.
**16** And Jesus kept withdrawing, to empty places, alone, to pray.[^10]

### Who Can Forgive Sins?


**17** *One day* Pharisees and teachers of the law had come from everywhere — Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem itself. *They sat in the room where Jesus was teaching, seated the way judges sit: watching, evaluating, deciding. An investigation was underway.*

**18** *Then the roof opened.* Men arrived carrying a paralyzed friend on a mat*— a man carried because he couldn't carry himself*. They tried to bring him in but couldn't get through the crowd.
**19** So they climbed the outside stairs[^11], pulled back the tiles*— debris fell into the room —* and lowered him through the gap, down on ropes, until his mat touched the floor in front of Jesus.
**20** Jesus looked at the men on the roof. Saw their faith — not the paralytic's faith, but theirs. Then he looked at the man lying on the mat.

**21** *Silence. Not the silence of awe but the silence of alarm.* The teachers of the law began to reason: *Only God forgives sins. Everyone knows this. It is the most basic theology there is. If this man claims to speak God's forgiveness —* \p
“Who is this who speaks blasphemy? Who can forgive sins but God alone?”[^12] *Unless he isn't.*
**22** Jesus knew what they were thinking. “Why are you reasoning like this in your hearts?
**23** Which is easier to say — 'Your sins have been forgiven' or 'Get up and walk'?” *He let the question sit. One claim no one can verify. The other, everyone will see.*
**24** “But so that you know the Son of Man[^13] has authority on earth to forgive sins —”

**25** “Get up. Pick up your mat. Go home.”

**26** Something gripped everyone in the room. *Fear and praise mixed together — an emotion that didn't have a name.* “We've seen things today,” they said, “that shouldn't be possible.”[^14]

### Dinner with Outcasts


**27** Jesus went out and saw a man named Levi, sitting at a customs post on the border.[^15] *Tax collector — in that world, the word carried everything: collaborator, profiteer, traitor. Someone who extracted money from his own people to fund an occupation. No one with honor would speak to him.*
**28** Jesus looked at him*— not past him, not through him. At him*.


**29** Then he threw a feast*— a lavish one, the kind of feast only a tax collector's income could fund*.[^16] His guests were tax collectors and others*— people the respectable world had discarded. This was his entire social circle: the ones nobody else would eat with*.
**30** The Pharisees saw it and went to Jesus' disciples. \p
“Why do you eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners?” *It was not a small question. Eating together — reclining at the same table, sharing from the same dishes — meant acceptance. It meant endorsement. It meant you were declaring, publicly, that these people were yours. The Pharisees weren't being petty. They were enforcing the boundary that held their community together.*

**31** Jesus said: “The healthy don't need a doctor. The sick do.”[^17] *He let the proverb land.*
**32** *Then:* “I didn't come for the righteous. I came for sinners — to turn their lives around.”[^18][^19]

### New Wine, Old Wineskins


**33** “John's followers fast and pray,” they said. “The Pharisees' followers fast and pray. Yours eat and drink.”[^20] *The accusation came from an unlikely alliance: John's radicals and the establishment Pharisees, who agreed on almost nothing, now agreeing on this — that Jesus' movement lacked discipline.*

**34** “Can you force wedding guests to mourn while the groom is right there with them?” Jesus asked.[^21]
**35** “But days will come when the bridegroom is taken from them.”


**36** He told them a parable. “No one cuts a piece from a new coat to patch an old one — you'd wreck the new coat, and the patch wouldn't match anyway.
**37** And no one pours fresh wine into old wineskins.[^22] *The wine is still fermenting. The skins have already stretched as far as they can.* The skins will split, the wine will spill*— you lose both. Everything gone*.
**38** New wine needs new skins.”
**39** *And then, almost as an afterthought:* “No one who's been drinking aged wine wants the new vintage. They say, 'The old is good.'”[^23]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *Fishing industry*: First-century Galilean fishing was a commercial enterprise, not subsistence. Recent excavations at Magdala (2007-2025) uncovered a large marketplace with 28 shops, approximately 300 fishing weights, and over 4,000 ancient coins. Boats were capital investments; partnerships (κοινωνοί) reflect cooperative business structures archaeologically attested. “Leaving everything” (v. 11) meant abandoning a potentially profitable enterprise with trade connections — real economic sacrifice, not romantic simplicity.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *Master (ἐπιστάτα)*: Luke's unique title for Jesus — used only by Luke among the Gospel writers. More formal than “teacher” (διδάσκαλε), acknowledging authority without the worship implied by “Lord” (κύριε). Peter shifts from ἐπιστάτα here to κύριε in verse 8, after the miracle reveals something about Jesus that “Master” can no longer contain.
[^3]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *The miraculous catch*: A fishing story — literally. Ancient fishermen told tales of great catches (Pliny, Oppian), and modern readers may see this as another one. Three frames: (1) Naturalistic: an unusually large school of fish happened to pass; the timing was coincidence, and the story grew in the telling. The narrative's psychological realism — Peter's professional skepticism, his reluctance, his raw confession afterward — argues against pure legend, but doesn't rule out natural explanation. (2) Symbolic-theological: the catch represents the future mission; fish = people gathered into the net of the kingdom. The story functions as a prophetic sign-act, not a nature miracle. (3) Theophany: divine power over creation demonstrated — the same God who parted the sea now fills nets. Peter's response (terror, not gratitude) is the classic human reaction to theophany throughout scripture (Isaiah 6, Exodus 3, Judges 6). The text does not ask which frame is “right.” It shows what Peter did next.
[^4]: **KONTEXT** — *Isaiah 6 pattern*: Peter's response echoes the classic theophany pattern: divine presence → human unworthiness → “fear not” → commission. Isaiah cried “Woe to me! I am undone!” (Isa. 6:5). Moses hid his face at the burning bush (Exod. 3:6). Gideon feared he would die (Judg. 6:22-23). Peter joins this line — not guilt over specific sins, but the terror of standing too close to holiness. His shift from ἐπιστάτα (“Master”) to κύριε (“Lord”) marks the moment of recognition.
[^5]: **APOLOGETICS** — *Historical reliability*: The psychological realism of this account argues for authenticity, not against it. Peter's professional skepticism (“we worked all night and caught nothing”), his reluctant obedience (“because you say so”), and his raw, unpredictable confession (“Get away from me — I'm a sinful man”) are not the markers of propaganda. If constructing a recruitment narrative, why include Peter's resistance, his fear, his plea for Jesus to leave? The details resist the pattern of hagiography. The fish themselves are forgotten by verse 11 — the story moves past the miracle to the call, which is the point. Whatever happened on the lake, the account preserves something psychologically and narratively coherent: a man whose expertise was overturned, whose fear became faith, and who walked away from everything.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *Catching people alive (ζωγρέω)*: The Greek ζωγρέω is a military term meaning “to capture alive, to take prisoner without killing.” It appears in the Septuagint at Joshua 2:13 and 2 Timothy 2:26. The word transforms the Old Testament's judgment-fishing imagery — where Jeremiah 16:16 describes God sending “fishers” to catch Israel for punishment — into rescue imagery. Not “fishing for people” (a phrase dulled by overuse) but capturing them alive, bringing them to safety. The mission is rescue, not conquest.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *Leprosy (λέπρα)*: Biblical “leprosy” (λέπρα) is not identical to modern Hansen's disease. The term covered a range of skin conditions — any visible skin affliction that could render a person ritually unclean under Leviticus 13-14. Luke's medical precision (“full of leprosy,” πλήρης λέπρας) indicates an advanced, severe case. The significance was social and ritual, not primarily medical: a person declared “leprous” was exiled from community, worship, and human contact.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *Leviticus 14 protocol*: The purification of a healed leper required an elaborate seven-day ritual: two birds (one killed, one released), cedar wood, scarlet thread, hyssop, shaving of all body hair, multiple sacrifices, and priestly inspection and certification (Lev. 14:1-32). This was public restoration — the system that had declared a person unfit would now formally certify their return to community, worship, and human contact. Jesus upholds the Torah requirement while demonstrating authority that transcends it.
[^9]: **KONTEXT** — *Prophetic precedent*: In the Old Testament, only God cures leprosy. Naaman the Syrian was healed through Elisha's instruction (2 Kings 5). Miriam was healed through Moses' intercession (Num. 12:10-15). When the king of Israel received Naaman's request, he tore his robes: “Am I God, to kill and make alive, that this man sends to me to cure his leprosy?” (2 Kings 5:7). The implication Luke draws: Jesus exercises divine prerogative.
[^10]: **LEBEN** — *The untouchable*: Who are today's “lepers” — the people our systems declare unfit for contact? Every culture has them: those whose condition, status, or identity triggers avoidance. The mentally ill on city streets. The formerly incarcerated seeking employment. The chronically ill whose insurance has run out. Jesus' first act with the leper was not healing but touch — the restoration of human contact before the restoration of skin. Christian communities across traditions have found that the sequence matters: presence before program, contact before cure. Francis of Assisi identified kissing a leper as his conversion moment — the point where revulsion became recognition.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *Roof access*: Palestinian houses had flat roofs accessible via outside staircases, used for sleeping, storage, and socializing. Roofs were constructed of branches and rushes laid over wooden beams, packed with dried mud — and could be dismantled and repaired without major structural damage. Luke adapts for his Greco-Roman audience, writing “tiles” (κεράμων) rather than the mud-and-branch construction Mark describes (Mark 2:4). The friends' action is not vandalism but controlled access through a familiar architectural feature.
[^12]: **KONTEXT** — *Blasphemy*: In Second Temple Judaism, blasphemy meant usurping divine prerogatives or dishonoring God — not merely “saying something offensive about religion.” The scribes' objection is theologically precise: only God forgives sins (Isa. 43:25, 44:22). If Jesus claims to declare God's forgiveness outside the Temple system and without priestly mediation, he is either committing the gravest possible offense — or he possesses an authority no one has categories for. The text forces a binary: blasphemer or divine.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *Son of Man (υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου)*: An enigmatic self-designation drawn from Daniel 7:13-14, where “one like a son of man” receives authority, glory, and sovereign power from the “Ancient of Days.” The title is ambiguous by design: in Aramaic, bar enasha can simply mean “a human being” or “someone like me,” but in the context of Daniel 7, it denotes a figure of cosmic authority. The early church rarely used this title — preferring “Lord” and “Christ” — which, by the criterion of dissimilarity, argues for its authenticity as Jesus' own usage. Here it anchors an extraordinary claim: the Son of Man has authority on earth to do what only God does in heaven.
[^14]: **APOLOGETICS** — *Son of Man authenticity*: The title “Son of Man” is the strongest argument for the historical authenticity of Jesus' self-claims. The early church overwhelmingly preferred “Lord” (κύριος) and “Christ” (χριστός) — titles that communicated clearly in the Greco-Roman world. “Son of Man” is obscure, ambiguous, and rooted in Jewish apocalyptic literature that most Gentile converts would not recognize. If the church was inventing Jesus' words, why consistently put the most difficult and least useful title in his mouth? The criterion of dissimilarity — that traditions unlikely to have been invented by the early church are more likely authentic — supports this as Jesus' own characteristic self-designation.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *Tax collectors*: Tax collectors (τελῶναι) in first-century Palestine worked for Rome or the Herodian client kings, collecting customs duties at border posts and tolls along trade routes. The system incentivized corruption: collectors purchased the right to collect taxes and kept any surplus above the contracted amount. Jewish sources grouped them with “robbers, murderers, and prostitutes” — the same moral category. They were disqualified from serving as judges or witnesses in court. Capernaum, situated on the border between the territories of Herod Antipas and Philip, was a natural location for a customs post. Levi's wealth (he hosts a great feast in v. 29) reflects years of profitable extraction from his own people.
[^16]: **KULTUR** — *Table fellowship*: Mediterranean meals were communal events: reclining on one elbow around a shared table, eating from common dishes, conversing for hours. Eating together was the definitive act of social acceptance — a public declaration that the people at your table belong with you. The Pharisees' concern is not merely about dietary rules but about social endorsement: by reclining at Levi's table, Jesus is publicly declaring solidarity with those the religious system has excluded. “You are who you eat with” was not metaphor but social reality.
[^17]: **KONTEXT** — *Physician metaphor*: Behind Jesus' proverb lies Hosea 6:6: “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.” Jesus reframes the question from purity (who is clean enough to eat with?) to healing (who needs a doctor?). The Pharisees' framework treats sinners as contaminants to avoid; Jesus' framework treats them as patients to treat. Both frameworks take sin seriously — the difference is in the response.
[^18]: **TEXT** — *To turn their lives around (εἰς μετάνοιαν)*: Luke adds “εἰς μετάνοιαν” — absent from the parallel passages in Mark 2:17 and Matthew 9:13. The addition is theologically deliberate: Jesus' table fellowship with sinners is not mere social inclusion or tolerance. It is invitation to μετάνοια — a radical reorientation of life direction. The Greek μετα-νοέω literally means “to change one's mind/direction” (μετά = change + νοῦς = mind). It is not primarily guilt or remorse but transformation — turning from one direction toward another. Luke's addition clarifies: inclusion and transformation are not opposites. Jesus eats with sinners AND calls them to change.
[^19]: **LEBEN** — *The collaborator's feast*: Levi's wealth came from extracting money from his neighbors — and everyone at that feast knew it. His party celebrates leaving that life behind. But the text doesn't say Levi returned what he'd stolen (contrast Zacchaeus in 19:8). The feast exists in the space between old life and new — funded by exploitation, offered in gratitude. Who among us profits from systems we know are unjust — and what would it look like to host a feast that celebrated walking away? The tradition has found that repentance rarely begins with purity. It begins with a meal.
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *Fasting practices*: The Torah commanded fasting only on Yom Kippur (Lev. 16:29-31). Pharisees voluntarily fasted twice weekly — Monday and Thursday — as acts of personal piety and national mourning. John the Baptist's disciples fasted in apocalyptic anticipation, mourning Israel's condition under Roman occupation. Jesus' followers conspicuously did not fast, which looked like religious laxity — or worse, a claim that there was nothing to mourn. The accusation comes from an unlikely alliance: John's radical ascetics and the establishment Pharisees, who agreed on almost nothing, now united in their critique.
[^21]: **KONTEXT** — *Bridegroom = Messiah*: In the Old Testament, Yahweh is Israel's bridegroom (Hosea 2:16-20, Isaiah 62:5, Jeremiah 2:2). The messianic age is depicted as a wedding feast (Isaiah 25:6-9). By calling himself the bridegroom, Jesus makes an implicit messianic claim — and more: he claims the role that the Hebrew scriptures assign to God. The shift from “groom” (v. 34) to “bridegroom” (v. 35) heightens the claim. “Taken away” (ἀπαρθῇ) is a violent passive — the bridegroom will not depart willingly but be forcibly removed. This is the first hint of the cross, spoken inside a wedding metaphor.
[^22]: **KULTUR** — *Wineskins*: Wineskins were made from whole animal hides (goat or sheep), sewn and sealed for liquid storage. New wine, still fermenting, produces carbon dioxide gas that expands the leather. New skins are supple enough to stretch with the pressure. Old skins, already stretched to capacity by previous use, have no flexibility left — new wine will burst them. The metaphor is precise: the issue is not that old skins are bad but that they have already given all they can. Jesus' new reality requires containers that can accommodate growth and pressure. Both wine and skins are lost in a mismatch — the incompatibility is total.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *“The old is good” (ὁ παλαιὸς χρηστός ἐστιν)*: This verse appears only in Luke — absent from Mark and Matthew. Its tone is deliberately ambiguous. Is Jesus endorsing the old? Critiquing those who resist the new? Simply naming the human tendency to prefer what's familiar? The text doesn't resolve the tension, and interpreters have divided sharply. Some read it as sympathetic — Jesus understands why people resist change. Others read it as ironic — the preference for old wine will cause some to miss the new thing God is doing. The variant reading χρηστότερος (“better”) in some manuscripts sharpens the comparison but is likely secondary. The ambiguity may be the point: Jesus names the reality of resistance without condemning those who feel it.

## Kapitel 6


### Lord of the Sabbath


**1** One Sabbath, Jesus was passing through the grain fields with his disciples.[^1] *They were hungry.* They plucked heads of grain, rubbed them open in their hands, and ate.[^2]
**2** Some Pharisees challenged them: \p
“Why are you doing what is not lawful on the Sabbath?”

**3** Jesus turned to them. “Haven't you read this — what David did when he and his companions were hungry?
**4** How he entered the house of God and took the bread of the Presence[^3], bread that only priests were allowed to eat, and ate it himself, and gave it to his men?”
**5** *No one answered.*


### A Withered Hand Restored


**6** On another Sabbath, Jesus went into the synagogue to teach. A man was there with a withered right hand[^4]*— the fingers curled and useless*.
**7** The scribes and Pharisees watched*. Not out of curiosity*. They were looking for evidence — something to charge him with.
**8** But Jesus knew their thoughts. “Stand up,” he said to the man. “Come here, to the middle.”

**9** Jesus faced the authorities. “Let me ask you something. Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good, or to do evil? To save a life, or to destroy it?”[^5]

**10** He looked around*the room — every face, one by one*. Then he said to the man: “Stretch out your hand.”

**11** A senseless rage took hold of them, and they began talking among themselves about what they could do to Jesus.[^6]

### Choosing the Twelve


**12** In those days Jesus went up the mountain[^7] to pray, and he stayed all night*— through the cold and the dark*, not sleeping.
**13** At dawn he called his disciples together. From them he chose twelve[^8], and named them apostles.
**14** Simon — he called him Peter. Andrew, Peter's brother. James and John. Philip. Bartholomew.
**15** Matthew. Thomas. James son of Alphaeus. Simon the Zealot[^9]. Judas son of James.
**16** And Judas Iscariot. Who became a traitor.

### Crowds from Everywhere


**17** He came down with them and stood on level ground. A great crowd of disciples was waiting, and *beyond them* a mass of people — from all over Judea and Jerusalem, and from the coast, from Tyre and Sidon.[^10]
**18** They had come to hear him. They had come to be healed.

**19** Everyone was trying to touch him, because power was flowing out of him, and it healed them all.

### Blessings and Warnings


**20** He lifted his eyes, looked straight at his disciples, and said:


**21** “Blessed are you whose stomachs are empty right now — you will be filled.


**22** “Blessed are you when people hate you, when they shut you out and insult you and reject your name as evil — all because of the Son of Man.
**23** When that day comes, leap for joy.[^11] Your reward in heaven is great. Their ancestors did the same to the prophets.

**24** “But how terrible for you who are rich. You have received your comfort*— all of it. Nothing more is coming*.[^12]
**25** “How terrible for you who are full right now. You will go hungry.


**26** “How terrible for you when everyone speaks well of you. Their ancestors said the same things about the false prophets.”
[^13]

### Love Your Enemies


**27** “But to you who are listening, I say: Love your enemies.[^14] Do good to those who hate you.
**28** Bless those who curse you. Pray for those who abuse you.
**29** “When someone hits you on the cheek, offer the other one too.[^15] When someone takes your cloak, let them have your shirt as well.[^16]
**30** Give to anyone who asks. When someone takes what belongs to you, don't ask for it back.
**31** “Treat others the way you want to be treated.

**32** “If you only love people who love you, what credit is that?[^17] Even sinners love those who love them back.
**33** If you only do good to those who do good to you — what credit is that? Sinners do that too.
**34** If you only lend when you know you'll be repaid — what credit is that? Even sinners lend to sinners, expecting repayment in full.

**35** “But love your enemies. Do good to them. Lend to them, expecting nothing back. Your reward will be great. You will be children of the Most High — because he is kind even to the ungrateful and the wicked.

**36** “Be merciful. As your Father is merciful.”[^18]
[^19]

### Don't Judge


**37** “Don't judge, and you won't be judged. Don't condemn, and you won't be condemned. Forgive, and you'll be forgiven.
**38** Give, and it will be given to you — good measure, pressed down, shaken together, spilling over, poured into the fold of your garment.[^20] Whatever measure you use, that's what you'll get back.”

**39** He also told them a parable. “Can a blind person guide another blind person? Won't both of them fall into a pit?
**40** Students are not above their teacher. But everyone who is fully trained will be like their teacher.

**41** “Why do you notice the splinter in someone else's eye and miss the plank in your own?
**42** How can you say, 'Let me get that splinter out of your eye,' when you've got a plank sticking out of yours? Hypocrite[^21] — get the plank out of your own eye first. Then you might see well enough to help with the splinter in theirs.”

### Trees and Their Fruit


**43** “No good tree produces bad fruit, and no bad tree produces good fruit.
**44** Each tree is known by its own fruit. You don't pick figs from thornbushes. You don't harvest grapes from a briar.

**45** “A good person brings good things out of the good stored up in their heart.[^22] An evil person brings evil out of the evil stored up in theirs. The mouth speaks from whatever fills the heart.”

### Two Foundations


**46** “Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and not do what I tell you?

**47** “Everyone who comes to me, hears my words, and acts on them — I'll show you what they're like.
**48** They're like a man building a house who dug and kept digging, down to the rock, and laid his foundation there. When the flood came and the river burst against that house, it could not shake it, because it was well built.[^23]

**49** “But the one who hears and does not act is like a man who built a house on the ground without any foundation. The river burst against it, and immediately it collapsed. And the ruin of that house was complete.”[^24]
[^25]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *Sabbath*: The Sabbath was far more than a day off. It was the sign of God's covenant with Israel (Exod. 31:13-17), a gift to freed slaves who had never known a day of rest. Jews died rather than violate it during the Maccabean revolt. Sabbath observance defined Jewish identity against Roman assimilation. The debate here is not “religion vs. freedom” but an intra-Jewish argument about proper interpretation.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *Rubbing them open*: The Pharisees would have found in this scene four distinct Sabbath violations in one mouthful: plucking = reaping, rubbing = threshing, separating = winnowing, and eating = preparing food. The legal question is real, not petty — the categories come from the thirty-nine prohibited categories of work later codified in the Mishnah (Shabbat 7:2). Gleaning from field edges was itself legally permitted for the poor (Deut. 23:25). The act was lawful; the day was the issue.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *Bread of the Presence*: Twelve sacred loaves set before God's presence in the holy place, replaced weekly — reserved for priests alone (Lev. 24:5-9). Yet the priest gave them to David, hungry and fleeing for his life from Saul (1 Sam. 21:1-6). Jesus uses qal vachomer reasoning — light to heavy — a standard rabbinic method: if David could eat sacred bread, how much more can the Son of Man authorize his hungry disciples on the Sabbath?
[^4]: **TEXT** — *His right hand*: Luke alone specifies the right hand — the dominant hand in the ancient world. In a manual labor economy, a withered right hand meant the man could not work, earn, or support a family. This is not merely a disability but economic death. Luke's specificity — often attributed to his medical eye — underscores the stakes of the healing.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *To do good or to do evil?*: Jesus reframes the entire debate. The Pharisees' position was reasonable: this is a chronic condition — the man can wait until tomorrow. But Jesus forces a binary: there is no neutral position on the Sabbath. Inaction when you can help is itself an act — and it falls on the side of evil and destruction. The question is unanswerable because any response concedes Jesus' framing.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *Senseless rage (ἀνοία)*: Not mere anger or annoyance. The Greek ἀνοία means irrational fury — literally “without mind,” the rage of the publicly shamed who have lost rational control. This is the first moment in Luke where opposition to Jesus turns toward plotting his destruction. The word signals the dangerous, shame-driven violence that will eventually lead to the cross.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *The mountain*: In Jewish tradition, mountains are spaces of divine encounter — Moses on Sinai, Elijah on Horeb. Luke's definite article (“the mountain”) signals theophany space. That Jesus spends all night in prayer before choosing the Twelve echoes Moses' vigil on Sinai before receiving the covenant. The paradox is striking: the one who just claimed lordship over the Sabbath spends the entire night in dependent prayer.
[^8]: **KONTEXT** — *Twelve*: The number is not arbitrary. Twelve tribes constituted Israel. By selecting twelve from among his followers and naming them “apostles” (ἀποστόλους — authorized representatives, from the Hebrew concept of shaliach, one who carries the full authority of the sender), Jesus claims the authority to reconstitute the people of God. This is a founding act, not a staffing decision.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *The Zealot*: The Zealots were a Jewish political movement committed to violent resistance against Roman occupation. Simon's designation identifies him as a revolutionary — someone who believed armed revolt was the path to Israel's liberation. That Jesus' inner circle included both Simon the Zealot and Matthew the tax collector (a collaborator with Rome) reflects the explosive political diversity of the group. These men would have been natural enemies.
[^10]: **KONTEXT** — *Tyre and Sidon*: Pagan coastal cities in Phoenician territory — Gentile land. Their inclusion signals that Jesus' reach already extends beyond Jewish boundaries. This anticipates Luke's larger arc: the mission to the nations that will unfold in Acts. The movement from mountain (theophany space) to level ground (accessible to all, including Gentiles) is itself theological — Jesus descends to where people can reach him.
[^11]: **TEXT** — *Leap for joy (σκιρτήσατε)*: Not generic “rejoicing.” The Greek σκιρτάω means to leap, skip, spring — physical, bodily, exuberant movement. The same word describes the unborn John leaping in Elizabeth's womb (Luke 1:41, 44). Jesus commands a physical response: when persecution comes, don't just endure — leap.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *Received your comfort (ἀπέχετε)*: A commercial term used on receipts meaning “paid in full.” The verb ἀπέχω appears on ancient papyrus receipts as a stamp of completed transaction — the debt is discharged, nothing further is owed. Jesus uses marketplace language to devastating effect: the rich have already received everything they are getting. The receipt has been stamped. The account is closed.
[^13]: **LEBEN** — *Reversal and the comfortable*: The beatitudes and woes confront a question that has troubled Christian communities for two millennia: what does it mean for the comfortable to hear “how terrible for you who are rich”? The Franciscan tradition took poverty literally — choosing destitution as solidarity. Reformed theology emphasized stewardship — wealth as responsibility, not possession. Liberation theology (Gustavo Gutiérrez) argues for a “preferential option for the poor” grounded precisely in Luke's πτωχός. Dorothy Day's Catholic Worker movement lived the reversal in urban poverty. What unites these responses: none of them treat the woes as someone else's problem. The text asks every reader: which list describes your life?
[^14]: **TEXT** — *Love (ἀγαπᾶτε)*: The present imperative indicates ongoing, habitual, volitional action — not a one-time feeling. “Love” here is not emotional warmth but a chosen posture: act for the good of those who act against yours. Scholars widely regard “love your enemies” as authentic Jesus material and the peak statement of his ethics, because it is difficult, counter-cultural, and serves no obvious community-building purpose.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *Turn the other cheek*: Walter Wink's influential interpretation argues this is creative nonviolent resistance, not passive submission. In the ancient world, a superior struck an inferior with the back of the right hand on the right cheek — a blow of domination, not a fist fight. Turning the left cheek forces a dilemma: the striker cannot backhand (wrong cheek), cannot use the left hand (ritually unclean), and a fist blow would acknowledge the victim as an equal. Jesus teaches his followers to refuse the game — neither submitting nor retaliating, but exposing the violence of the system. Critics note this reading works better for public confrontation than private abuse.
[^16]: **KULTUR** — *Cloak and shirt*: In a poor person's lawsuit, the outer garment (ἱμάτιον, cloak) might be seized as collateral. Jesus says: give the inner garment (χιτών, tunic) too — which would leave you naked. In the ancient Near East, the shame of nakedness falls not on the naked person but on the one who sees their nakedness (Gen. 9:20-27). By stripping, the debtor exposes the creditor's cruelty for all to see. Another act of creative resistance.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *What credit (ποία χάρις)*: The word χάρις here means “credit” in the social exchange economy — not spiritual “grace.” Jesus dismantles the reciprocity system that governed all ancient (and modern) social life: I do something for you, you owe me, the ledger balances. “Even sinners” operate this way. God's children are called to break the cycle entirely.
[^18]: **TEXT** — *Merciful (οἰκτίρμονες)*: Luke's summary word is deliberately different from Matthew's “Be perfect” (Matt. 5:48). The Greek οἰκτίρμων connects to the Hebrew rahamim — gut-compassion, womb-love — the same visceral mercy that describes God's own character throughout the Hebrew Bible. Luke's Jesus does not command perfection but compassion — the kind that comes from the body, not just the mind. This is the sermon's ethical center.
[^19]: **LEBEN** — *Loving enemies in practice*: Christian communities have wrestled with enemy-love across two millennia. The early church's refusal to retaliate (even under persecution) was noted by Roman observers as extraordinary. Martin Luther King Jr. grounded the civil rights movement explicitly in Luke 6:27-36, teaching that love of enemies was not weakness but the highest form of moral strength. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, facing Nazism, argued that “cheap grace” avoids the cost of enemy-love while “costly grace” accepts it. Desmond Tutu's Truth and Reconciliation Commission in South Africa attempted institutional enemy-love — accountability without revenge. The tradition's consistent discovery: loving enemies is never natural. It is always a choice. And it always costs.
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *Poured into the fold of your garment*: A marketplace image. Grain sellers measured grain in standardized containers, then poured the purchase into the customer's κόλπος — the fold of the outer garment gathered at the waist to form a pouch. The three participles — pressed down (πεπιεσμένον), shaken together (σεσαλευμένον), overflowing (ὑπερεκχυννόμενον) — accumulate: God doesn't skim. This is generosity that exceeds the container. The image is physical, commercial, and vivid.
[^21]: **TEXT** — *Hypocrite (ὑποκριτά)*: The Greek ὑποκριτής originally meant “actor” — someone wearing a theatrical mask, performing a role. Jesus' use is deliberately theatrical: you are playing the part of an eye surgeon while a construction timber protrudes from your own face. The humor is intentional. Ancient audiences would have laughed — and the laughter opens the door to self-awareness. This is not moral condemnation but the exposure of absurd performance.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *Stored up in their heart (θησαυροῦ τῆς καρδίας)*: The “heart” in ancient Hebrew and Greek thought is not the seat of emotions but a storehouse — the accumulated character of a person, built over years of choices and habits. The “overflow” (περίσσευμα) is involuntary: speech reveals what you truly are, whether you intend it to or not. The mouth is not the problem. The storehouse is.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *Dug and kept digging (ἔσκαψεν καὶ ἐβάθυνεν)*: Two aorist verbs compressed: he dug and he deepened. Luke emphasizes the labor more than Matthew's version — this is exhausting, deliberate work. The rock (πέτρα) beneath the topsoil is bedrock, not a surface stone. The flood (πλημμύρη) is a wadi flash flood — dry riverbed one moment, a wall of water the next. The contrast is physical: sustained effort vs. sudden catastrophe.
[^24]: **TEXT** — *The ruin was complete (τὸ ῥῆγμα... μέγα)*: The Greek ῥῆγμα means total collapse — not partial damage, not something repairable. The adjective μέγα (great) intensifies: this is catastrophic, final ruin. The Sermon on the Plain ends not with comfort but with warning. Jesus claims his words are the foundation of reality itself — not Torah, not temple, not tradition, but his words. The christological claim embedded in this parable is enormous.
[^25]: **LEBEN** — *Hearing and doing*: The parable's distinction is not between those who hear and those who don't — both builders hear. The distinction is between hearing and acting. Christian contemplative traditions (from the Desert Fathers through Ignatius and the Quaker meeting) have consistently emphasized that spiritual hearing without corresponding action produces exactly the collapse Jesus describes. James 1:22 echoes the same principle: “Be doers of the word, not hearers only.” The question the parable leaves with every listener is not “Did you hear?” but “What did you do with what you heard?”

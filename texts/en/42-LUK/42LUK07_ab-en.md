## Kapitel 7


### The Faith of a Roman Officer


**1** After Jesus had finished saying all this to the people, he entered Capernaum.
**2** There was a centurion there*— a Roman officer in the occupying army —* who had a slave he valued deeply. This slave was dying.
**3** The centurion had heard about Jesus, so he sent Jewish elders from the town to ask for help.
**4** They found Jesus and pressed his case urgently. \p
“This man deserves your help,” they told him.
**5** “He loves our people — he even built our synagogue.”
[^1]
**6** Jesus went with them. But before he reached the house, friends of the centurion met him*on the road* with this message: \p
“Lord, don't come all the way. I'm not worthy of that — not worthy for you to step under my roof.
[^2]
**7** I didn't even think I had the right to come to you myself. Just say the word, and my slave will be healed.
**8** *I know how authority works.* I'm a man placed under authority, and I have soldiers under me. I say to one, 'Go,' and he goes. I say to another, 'Come,' and he comes. I say to my slave, 'Do this,' and it's done.*You don't need to be here for this to work.*”
**9** When Jesus heard this, he was amazed.*He stopped.* He turned to the crowd behind him.

[^3]
**10** When the messengers got back to the house, the slave was well.
[^4]

### A Widow's Only Son


**11** Soon afterward, Jesus traveled to a town called Nain, with his disciples and a large crowd.
[^5]
**12** As they neared the town gate, a funeral procession was coming out*— mourners, neighbors, what seemed like the whole town*. On the stretcher lay a young man: the only son of his mother.

[^6]
**13** When the Lord saw her, something broke inside him. He went to her. “Don't cry,” he said.
[^7]
**14** *Then he did something no one expected.* He walked up to the stretcher and touched it. *The bearers froze. Everyone knew what that meant — touching a bier carrying a dead body made you unclean.* But Jesus spoke to the dead man directly.
[^8]

**15** The dead man sat up. He began to speak.

[^9]
**16** Fear gripped them — all of them. “A great prophet has risen among us!” they said. “God has come to his people!”
[^10]
**17** The news spread through all of Judea and the surrounding countryside.

### Are You the One?


**18** John heard about all this*in prison*.
**19** He called two of his disciples and sent them to the Lord: “Are you the one who is to come, or should we expect someone else?”
[^11]

**20** They found Jesus and asked him directly.
**21** At that very moment, Jesus was healing — diseases, torments, evil spirits driven out, blind people given their sight.*He turned to John's disciples.*

**22** “Go back,” he said, “and tell John what you've seen and heard. Blind people are seeing again. The lame are walking. Lepers are being made clean. The deaf are hearing. The dead are being raised. And the poor*— the poor* are hearing good news.”
[^12]
**23** *He paused.*

[^13]

**24** After John's messengers left, Jesus turned to the crowds.

[^14]
**25** “A man in fine clothing? People who dress like that live in palaces, not deserts.
**26** So what did you go out to see? A prophet? Yes — and more than a prophet.
**27** This is the one Scripture speaks of: 'I am sending my messenger ahead of you, who will prepare your way.'
**28** “I tell you — among everyone ever born, no one is greater than John.”

[^15]

**29** When the people heard this, they — even the tax collectors — acknowledged that God was right. They had accepted John's baptism.
**30** But the Pharisees and the legal experts had refused it,*and in doing so,* they rejected what God had planned for them.

**31** Jesus continued: “What can I compare this generation to?
**32** They're like children in a marketplace, shouting at each other: 'We played *wedding* music, and you wouldn't dance! We sang funeral songs, and you wouldn't cry!'
[^16]
**33** John came — he didn't eat bread, he didn't drink wine — and you said, 'He has a demon.'
**34** The Son of Man came — he eats, he drinks — and you say, 'Look at him. A glutton. A drunk. A friend of tax collectors and sinners.'”
[^17]
**35** *He shook his head.*

[^18]

### A Woman's Tears


**36** A Pharisee named Simon invited Jesus to dinner. Jesus came and reclined at the table.
**37** In that town there was a woman*everyone knew —* known for the life she led*, the kind of woman respectable people avoided*. She heard Jesus was at Simon's house. She came carrying an alabaster jar of perfume, and she stood behind him where he reclined, at his feet.
[^19]
**38** She was weeping. Her tears fell on his feet. She let down her hair*— in front of everyone —* and wiped them. She kept kissing his feet,*over and over.* She poured the perfume.
[^20]
**39** Simon watched this and thought: If this man were really a prophet, he'd know exactly who this woman is. What she is.*He'd never let her touch him.*
**40** “Simon,” Jesus said, “I want to tell you something.”

**41** “A moneylender had two debtors. One owed five hundred *days' wages*. The other owed fifty.
[^21]
**42** Neither could pay him back, so he forgave them both. Which one do you think loved him more?”
**43** “The one who was forgiven more, I suppose,” Simon said.

**44** “Look at her. I came into your house. You gave me no water for my feet. She has washed them with her tears and dried them with her hair.
[^22]
**45** You gave me no *greeting* kiss. She hasn't stopped kissing my feet since she came in.
**46** You didn't anoint my head with oil. She has poured perfume on my feet.”
**47** *He paused.*

[^23]
**48** Then he turned to her.

[^24]
**49** The guests began whispering to each other: “Who is this man, who even forgives sins?”
**50** *But Jesus spoke only to her.* “Your faith has saved you,” he said. “Go in peace.”
[^25]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *built our synagogue*: Financing a synagogue represented years of a centurion's salary — extraordinary patronage, not a casual donation. In the ancient Mediterranean, such benefaction created mutual obligation between the centurion and the Jewish community. The elders' advocacy here is the reciprocal side of that relationship.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *not worthy (ἱκανός)*: This is not self-deprecation. In the honor-shame culture of the ancient Mediterranean, declaring yourself “not worthy” was a public act of honor-giving — a Roman officer publicly placing himself beneath a Jewish teacher. The centurion also likely understood Jewish purity boundaries: entering a Gentile home would have made Jesus ritually unclean.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *not anywhere in Israel*: This is not ethnic supersessionism — it is not a rejection of Israel's faith. The Jewish elders who vouched for the centurion demonstrated their own trust in Jesus. What amazes Jesus is the unexpected depth of this outsider's understanding: a Gentile grasps something about his authority that Israel's religious insiders have not yet articulated. The text challenges, but does not replace.
[^4]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *the slave was well*: How can someone heal at a distance — without contact, without even seeing the patient? Healing-at-a-distance traditions appear in multiple independent strands of the gospel tradition (compare John 4:46–54, a related but distinct account). Luke, whom early tradition identifies as a physician (Colossians 4:14), reports the outcome matter-of-factly: the messengers “found the slave well” — a verifiable observation, not a visionary scene. Within a naturalistic framework, this remains unexplained. The text reports a finding — “they found him well” — without explaining how. What readers make of that depends on what kind of reality they consider possible.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *Nain*: Nain is geographically precise: a village on the northern slope of the Hill of Moreh, about twenty miles southeast of Nazareth in the Jezreel Valley. Archaeological surveys have confirmed the site. Luke consistently names specific locations — a hallmark of historical reporting, not myth. The specificity invites verification: anyone who knew Nain could have asked questions. Notably, Nain lies near ancient Shunem, where the prophet Elisha raised a widow's son (2 Kings 4:32–37) — a parallel Luke's earliest readers would have caught immediately.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *only son (μονογενής) ... widow*: In the ancient world, a widow without sons had no income, no legal protection, and no social standing. “Only son” (\fq μονογενής\fq*) means: no other children, no one to provide for her in old age, no one to carry the family name. This funeral is not just grief — it is the complete collapse of her economic future and social identity. The whole town has come out because everyone understands what this death means for her.
[^7]: **LEBEN** — *something broke inside him (σπλαγχνίζομαι)*: The Greek splagchnizomai — literally the intestines churn — describes compassion as a bodily event, not a mental decision. Jesus does not pity this woman from a distance. He is physically moved by her pain, and that movement leads directly to action. This way of being affected — not charitable detachment but visceral solidarity — is what many wisdom traditions identify as the core of genuine humanity. Compassion as something that happens to you, not something you decide to perform. — A question that lands without any prior theological frame: when was the last time something actually moved you — not intellectually, but in the chest, in the gut? In a culture that floods us with images and thereby blunts us — war as a stream, hunger as a banner, suffering as an algorithm — being moved has become a scarce resource. The verse is a reminder that being affected is still possible and that it matters when it is allowed. — For readers inside a faith, splagchnizomai opens a line that the Christian tradition has named divine pathos. A Jewish theology of the prophets, named clearly by Abraham Heschel, put it this way: God is not impassive — injustice arrives at God. Franciscan spirituality with its attentiveness to the poor, the Ignatian practice of compositio loci — placing oneself inside a scene until the body feels it — and the Orthodox tradition of sympatheia all live from the same intuition: those who draw nearer to God do not become harder, they become softer. — Vocationally this has consequences. Professions in which compassion is the substance — care work, chaplaincy, therapy, teaching — suffer when being moved is trained out as inefficiency. The answer is not less feeling; it is better accompaniment. — This week: once, do not click away when something actually arrives at you.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *touched the stretcher (σορός)*: The \fq σορός\fq* was not a closed coffin but an open stretcher carrying a wrapped body. Touching it made a person ritually unclean for a day; touching the corpse itself meant seven days of impurity (Numbers 19:11–22). Jesus reached out deliberately. Instead of death contaminating him, life flowed from him to the dead man. The expected direction of contamination reversed entirely.
[^9]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *The dead man sat up*: This is one of three accounts of Jesus raising the dead in Luke's Gospel (compare Luke 8:49–56; John 11). For modern readers, it is the most radical claim in the text. Three ways of reading: (1) Symbolic-theological — the scene dramatizes Jesus' claim over death as a literary and theological statement. (2) Medical-historical — in a world without reliable death verification, some “raisings” may describe resuscitation from deep coma or apparent death. (3) Literal-historical — Jesus actually restored a dead man to life, consistent with early Christian claims about his own resurrection. Luke writes with restraint: “He sat up and began to speak.” No elaborate ritual, no drawn-out process — just a command and a result. Which framework a reader finds compelling depends on assumptions the text itself does not resolve. What Luke records in every case: the event left people terrified — and praising God.
[^10]: **KONTEXT** — *God has come to his people (ἐπεσκέψατο)*: The Greek \fq ἐπεσκέψατο\fq* (“has visited”) is Old Testament language for God's active intervention in history (Exodus 4:31; Ruth 1:6; Psalm 106:4). This is not a spontaneous exclamation — it is a theological recognition. What the crowd is saying: the ancient promise that God would visit, would act, would intervene — that promise is being fulfilled before our eyes. Luke uses this word at key moments: Zechariah's prophecy (1:68, 78), here, and Jesus' lament over Jerusalem (19:44).
[^11]: **TEXT** — *the one who is to come (ὁ ἐρχόμενος)*: “The Coming One” (\fq ὁ ἐρχόμενος\fq*) is a messianic title rooted in Malachi 3:1 and Psalm 118:26. John had preached a Coming One who would bring fire and judgment (Luke 3:7–17). Instead, Jesus was healing the sick and eating with outcasts. John's question is not skepticism — it is genuine theological crisis. What he expected and what he sees do not match.
[^12]: **KONTEXT** — *Blind people are seeing ... the poor are hearing good news*: Jesus does not answer “Yes, I am the Messiah.” He points to evidence: the signs Isaiah prophesied for the messianic age (Isaiah 35:5–6; 61:1). Go look at what is happening and decide. The list builds to its climax: “the poor are hearing good news.” This is not an afterthought — it is the summit. The kingdom of God is for those at the bottom. The present tense matters: these things are happening now. The new age is breaking in.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *does not lose faith in me (σκανδαλίζω)*: The Greek \fq σκανδαλίζω\fq* does not mean “be offended” — that is far too weak. It means to stumble in faith, to fall away, to lose trust. Jesus knows he defies messianic expectations. This beatitude is not a threat but pastoral care — a gentle word spoken across the miles to a man in prison who is confused, not faithless. It says: “I know I'm not what you expected. Hang in there, John.”
[^14]: **KULTUR** — *a reed blown by the wind (κάλαμος)*: Herod Antipas used the image of a reed on his coins. Jesus' audience would have caught the political jab immediately: John is the opposite of a political weathervane who bends whichever way the wind blows. He spoke truth to power and is in prison for it.
[^15]: **KONTEXT** — *the least in the kingdom is greater*: This is not a contradiction but an announcement of a new era. John stands at the border between two ages. He is the greatest figure of the old covenant — the last and greatest prophet. But the new reality that Jesus inaugurates surpasses even the greatest of the old. This is not a demotion of John; it is a statement about the magnitude of what is arriving.
[^16]: **KULTUR** — *children in a marketplace*: Children in the ancient world played at weddings and funerals — they would take turns playing the flute for dancing or singing dirges for mourning. Jesus' point: this generation refuses to play along no matter what. John came with fasting and austerity (funeral-style), and they rejected him. Jesus comes with feasting and inclusion (wedding-style), and they reject him too. Nothing satisfies them.
[^17]: **KULTUR** — *a friend of tax collectors and sinners*: In the honor-shame culture of the first century, eating publicly with people labeled as “sinners” — tax collectors, the ritually unclean, the morally compromised — meant sharing their shame. To call someone “a friend of sinners” was a devastating social accusation. Jesus does not deny the charge. He embraces it.
[^18]: **KONTEXT** — *Wisdom (σοφία)*: “Wisdom” (\fq σοφία\fq*) is personified here — a feminine figure in Jewish tradition (Proverbs 8; Sirach 24). John and Jesus are Wisdom's “children.” Their actions — the fruits of their ministry — vindicate the wisdom of God's plan, regardless of how “this generation” responds. The tree is judged by its fruit.
[^19]: **KULTUR** — *known for the life she led (ἁμαρτωλός)*: The Greek \fq ἁμαρτωλός\fq* is a social label — someone living outside recognized religious and moral norms. Everyone in town knew her by this designation. What specifically she had done, the text leaves open. This woman is not Mary Magdalene — that identification was first proposed by Pope Gregory I in 591 CE, conflating three distinct women (the sinful woman of Luke 7, Mary of Bethany in John 12, and Mary Magdalene in Luke 8:2). The Roman Catholic Church officially corrected this in 1969. Eastern Orthodox tradition always kept them separate.
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *let down her hair*: Letting down one's hair in public was considered shameful — it was an intimacy reserved for a woman's husband. Either this woman had no social reputation left to protect, or her devotion was so overwhelming that convention ceased to matter. The Greek verb \fq καταφιλέω\fq* (kept kissing) is in the imperfect tense — continuous, repeated action, not a single gesture. Every detail of what she does violates social expectation.
[^21]: **KULTUR** — *five hundred days' wages*: The Greek says “five hundred denarii.” A denarius was roughly a day's wage for a laborer. Five hundred denarii represents nearly two years of income — a crushing debt. Fifty denarii is about two and a half months. Both debts are forgiven. The difference in scale is the point: the one forgiven more, loves more.
[^22]: **KULTUR** — *no water for my feet*: Providing water for a guest's feet, a greeting kiss, and oil for the head were the most basic courtesies a host owed in the ancient Mediterranean world. Simon withheld all three. Whether this was deliberate insult or calculated testing, the effect was public humiliation of Jesus as a guest. The woman provides — with her own body — everything the host refused.
[^23]: **KONTEXT** — *as her great love has shown (ὅτι ἠγάπησεν πολύ)*: The parable (vv. 41–43) makes the direction of causation unmistakable: forgiveness comes first; love is the response. She loves much because she has been forgiven much — not the other way around. Her tears, her perfume, her devotion are the evidence of grace already received, not its price of admission. The Greek \fq ὅτι\fq* can mean “because” or “as shown by.” Context demands the latter.
[^24]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Your sins are forgiven*: This is one of the most radical claims in Luke's Gospel. The guests' reaction (v. 49) shows they understood immediately: in Jewish theology, only God can forgive sins — not as abstract doctrine, but because sin is fundamentally a breach with God, and only the offended party can repair that breach. Whoever claims the authority to forgive sins is claiming divine prerogative. The early church read this scene as self-revelation: Jesus acts with the authority of God. The claim is not softened by context — it is sharpened by it. What readers conclude about who Jesus was depends on what they make of this moment.
[^25]: **LEBEN** — *Go in peace (εἰρήνην)*: The Greek eirene translates the Hebrew shalom — a word that means far more than the absence of conflict. It means wholeness, integrity, restored relationships, a life put back together. Jesus' final word to this woman is not a religious formula. It is a statement about her condition: she entered this room carrying a label that defined her. She leaves as a whole person. What was broken has been mended. What was shameful has been honored. The scene ends not with instruction but with dignity. — A question that does not need any prior religious frame: which label has been stuck on you for years that no longer fits — but no one in your life seems willing to peel it off? The addiction you walked away from a decade ago. The failure your family still tells in the present tense. The diagnosis that became your nickname. Shalom is also the right to walk forward with what is actually still true of you, without carrying the weight of an old story that everyone else still finds convenient. — For readers inside a faith, this word opens a sacramental line. The Catholic sacrament of reconciliation, the Orthodox absolution, the Reformed liturgy of confession, the free-church practice of public testimony — all attempt what Jesus performs here in a single sentence: take the label off so the person can walk. Bonhoeffer in Life Together described it this way: sin wants to remain alone — forgiveness happens before witnesses. The church is the room in which shalom is spoken aloud. — Politically shalom carries an edge that the English peace tends to lose. In the prophetic Hebrew tradition, shalom is always also justice — mishpat and shalom belong together. Peace negotiated over the heads of the wounded is not shalom. — This week: tell one person whose past is no longer who they are that they are free to walk forward. Say it out loud.

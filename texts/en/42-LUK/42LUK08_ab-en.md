## Kapitel 8


### The Women


**1** After this, Jesus traveled from town to town and village to village, bringing the good news of God's kingdom. The Twelve were with him.
**2** So were a number of women who had been healed — freed from evil spirits and diseases. Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had been driven out.[^1]
**3** Joanna, whose husband was steward of Herod's court*— the same court that had arrested John*. Susanna. And many others. They funded *the movement* from their own means.[^2][^3]

### The Sower


**4** People were streaming in from every direction — town after town — until a great crowd had gathered. Jesus told them a story:
**5** “A farmer went out to sow. As he scattered seed, some fell along the path. It was trampled. Birds devoured it.
**6** Some fell on rock and sprouted, but without moisture it withered.
**7** Some fell among thorns. The thorns grew alongside it and choked it out.
**8** But some fell into good soil. It grew and produced a crop — a hundred times what was sown.”[^4]


**9** The disciples asked him what the parable meant.
**10** “To you,” he said, “the secrets of God's kingdom have been given. But for the rest it comes in parables — so that looking, they do not see; listening, they do not understand.[^5]
**11** “This is what it means: The seed is God's word.
**12** The ones along the path — they hear, and then the devil comes and takes the word from their hearts, so they won't believe and be saved.
**13** The ones on rock receive the word with joy when they hear it, but they have no root. They believe for a time, but when they're tested, they fall away.
**14** What fell among thorns — those are people who hear, but as life goes on, they're choked by worries, by wealth, by pleasures, and they never bear ripe fruit.
**15** But the good soil — those are people with a good and honest heart, who hear the word, hold fast to it, and bear fruit through patient endurance.”[^6]

**16** “No one lights a lamp and covers it with a jar, or puts it under a bed. You set it on a stand so people coming in can see the light.
**17** Whatever is hidden will come into the open. Whatever is secret will come to light.
**18** So be careful how you listen — because whoever has will be given more, and whoever has nothing, even what they think they have will be taken away.”

**19** His mother and his brothers came, but the crowd made it impossible to reach him.
**20** Someone told him, “Your mother and your brothers are outside. They want to see you.”
**21** He said, “My mother and my brothers — they are the ones who hear God's word and do it.”[^7]

### The Storm


**22** One day Jesus got into a boat with his disciples. “Let's cross to the other side of the lake,” he said.
**23** As they sailed, he fell asleep. A squall of wind came down on the lake. The boat was swamping. They were in *real* danger.

**24** They went to him and shook him awake. “Master! Master! We're going to die!”


**25** “Where is your faith?” he asked them.


### The Man Among the Tombs


**26** They crossed to the region of the Gerasenes, on the opposite shore from Galilee.
**27** As Jesus stepped onto land, a man came at him from the town — demon-possessed, long without clothes, living not in any house but among the tombs*, among the dead*.[^8]

**28** He saw Jesus and screamed. He threw himself down and cried out, \p
“What do you want with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? I beg you, don't torment me!”
**29** Jesus had been commanding the unclean spirit to come out of him. It had seized him again and again. They had chained him, bound him, kept him under guard — but he would snap the chains and the demon would drive him back to the wastelands.

**30** “What is your name?” Jesus asked.


**31** They begged Jesus not to send them into the abyss.[^9]
**32** There was a large herd of pigs feeding on the hillside, and the demons begged to be allowed into the pigs. He let them.[^10]
**33** They came out of the man and entered the pigs, and the whole herd stampeded down the steep bank into the lake. They drowned.

**34** The herders fled. They told everyone in the town and countryside what had happened.
**35** People came to see *for themselves*. They found the man sitting at Jesus' feet — clothed*, calm*, in his right mind. And they were afraid.
**36** Witnesses told them how the man had been saved.
**37** But the whole population of the region asked Jesus to leave. They were all afraid. He got into the boat and turned back.

**38** The man begged to go with him. Jesus said, “Go home. Tell them what God has done for you.”

**39** He went through the entire town, telling everyone what Jesus had done for him.[^11]

### Twelve Years


**40** When Jesus came back *across the lake*, the crowd was waiting for him.
**41** Then a man *pushed through* — Jairus, a synagogue leader. He fell at Jesus' feet. He begged him to come to his house.
**42** His only daughter, twelve years old, was dying.

**43** In that crowd was a woman who had been bleeding for twelve years — *twelve years of constant impurity, twelve years with no one willing to touch her,* twelve years of spending every coin she had on doctors who could not help.[^12]
**44** She *slipped up* behind Jesus and touched the edge of his cloak.[^13]


**45** “Who touched me?” Jesus said.


**46** “Someone touched me,” Jesus said. “I felt power leave me.”

**47** The woman realized she could not hide. She came forward. She was trembling. She fell at his feet and told the whole story, in front of everyone — why she had touched him, and how she had been healed at once.[^14]
**48** He said, “Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace.”[^15][^16]

**49** He was still speaking when a messenger arrived from Jairus's house: “Your daughter is dead. There's no need to trouble the teacher.”

**50** Jesus heard it. He said to Jairus, “Don't be afraid. Just believe — and she will be well.”[^17]

**51** At the house, he let no one enter with him except Peter, John, and James, and the child's mother and father.
**52** *The mourning had already begun —* everyone weeping, wailing.

**53** They laughed at him. They knew death when they saw it.

**54** He took her hand. “Child, get up.”[^18]
**55** Her spirit returned. She stood up at once.[^19] He told them to give her something to eat.
**56** Her parents could not speak. He told them not to tell anyone what had happened.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *Mary Magdalene*: Mary of Magdala is NOT the sinful woman of Luke 7:36-50 — a conflation created by Pope Gregory I in 591 CE and never accepted by Eastern Christianity. Luke introduces Mary here as someone healed of severe affliction (“seven demons” signifies completeness of suffering, not sinfulness), immediately after the anointing scene, as a clearly distinct figure. The tradition of Mary as repentant prostitute has no basis in the text and has been formally corrected by the Catholic Church (1969).
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *Women as patrons*: The Greek διηκόνουν αὐτοῖς ἐκ τῶν ὑπαρχόντων αὐταῖς means “they provided for them from their own resources” — financial patronage, not domestic service. These women were benefactors (a recognized social role in Greco-Roman culture), funding an itinerant movement from personal wealth. Joanna's position is particularly striking: her husband Chuza was ἐπίτροπος (steward/manager) of Herod Antipas — the same ruler who imprisoned and executed John the Baptist. Her support of Jesus represents a quiet defection from the power structure, at considerable personal risk.
[^3]: **APOLOGETICS** — *Mary... Joanna... Susanna*: Luke names three women as patrons and followers — an unusual detail for a fabricated account. In the ancient world, women's testimony carried little legal weight (Josephus, Ant. 4.8.15). A writer inventing a religious movement would not highlight female financial backers. The specificity — Joanna's husband Chuza, his role as Herod's steward — invites verification rather than deflecting it. The criterion of embarrassment applies: naming women as primary supporters would diminish, not enhance, the movement's credibility in first-century culture. Luke names them anyway, suggesting authentic tradition preserved by the community.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *A hundred times (ἑκατονταπλασίονα)*: In first-century Palestinian agriculture, a yield of four to tenfold was considered good. Thirtyfold was exceptional. A hundredfold was miraculous — Isaac's harvest in Genesis 26:12 at a hundredfold is explicitly attributed to God's blessing. Jesus' parable sets up a paradox: three out of four sowings fail completely, but the one that succeeds produces an impossible abundance. The kingdom of God, Luke suggests, operates by a different arithmetic.
[^5]: **KONTEXT** — *Isaiah 6:9-10*: Jesus quotes Isaiah's commissioning vision, where the prophet is told his message will harden hearts rather than open them. This is not predestination — it is prophetic judgment on willful rejection. Isaiah's audience had every opportunity to hear; they chose not to. The parables function as both invitation and judgment — they reveal truth to those who seek and conceal it from those who will not engage. The “so that” (ἵνα) has generated centuries of debate: does it express purpose or result? Most scholars read it as consequence rather than divine intention to blind.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *Patient endurance (ἐν ὑπομονῇ)*: The Greek ὑπομονή is not passive patience — waiting quietly for something to happen. It is a Stoic and early Christian virtue meaning active, tested faithfulness under pressure. The word appears in contexts of endurance through persecution (Romans 5:3-4), sustained effort (Hebrews 12:1), and the kind of resilience that does not quit when things get hard. Luke's good soil doesn't just receive the word; it holds on through difficulty and produces fruit over time.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *Family redefined*: In a collectivist culture where family was everything — your identity, your security, your place in the world — Jesus' words are not a gentle expansion of the concept. They are a shock. To say “my mother and brothers are those who hear God's word and do it” is to create an alternative kinship group that competes with blood ties. Ancient listeners would have heard this as either deeply offensive or deeply liberating, depending on where they stood. For those excluded from respectable families — the women of 8:1-3, tax collectors, the marginalized — this is the most radical invitation imaginable.
[^8]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Demons and diagnosis*: Three frames: (1) Psychiatric: the man's symptoms — social isolation, self-harm, extreme agitation, superhuman strength during episodes, altered identity — correspond to recognizable conditions in modern psychiatry. Ancient cultures lacked diagnostic categories; “demon possession” was the available framework for severe mental illness. The healing may represent a therapeutic encounter. (2) Spiritual: the ancient world understood spiritual forces as real agents with power over human lives. The detailed interaction — naming, negotiation, transfer — follows established exorcism patterns and reflects a worldview where human suffering has cosmic dimensions. Reducing this to psychiatry may miss the text's deeper claim about forces that dehumanize. (3) Both/and: some contemporary readers hold that spiritual and psychological realities are not mutually exclusive — that the language of “demons” names something real about destructive forces, whether understood as external beings or internal patterns. The text is more interested in the result than the mechanism: a man restored to clothing, community, and coherence.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *The abyss (ἄβυσσος)*: In Jewish cosmology, the abyss was the place of ultimate imprisonment — the cosmic deep beneath the earth where fallen powers are confined (1 Enoch 10:4-6, Revelation 9:1-2, 20:1-3). The demons beg not for mercy but for a lesser punishment: anything but the abyss. Their terror reveals the geography of the unseen world as first-century Jews understood it — a cosmos with layers, where some places are worse than others, and where even demons know their final destination.
[^10]: **KULTUR** — *Pigs*: Pigs were the paradigmatic unclean animal in Jewish law (Lev. 11:7, Deut. 14:8). Their presence confirms Gentile territory — no Jewish community would raise them. The scene layers uncleanness upon uncleanness: demons, tombs (death contamination), nakedness (shame), and now pigs. Jesus enters the most impure space imaginable — and his authority extends even here. The destruction of the herd raises an uncomfortable question the text does not answer: was the economic cost to the community justified by one man's liberation?
[^11]: **KONTEXT** — *God and Jesus*: Notice the shift: Jesus tells the man to proclaim “what God has done for you” (ὅσα σοι ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός). The man goes and proclaims “what Jesus had done for him” (ὅσα ἐποίησεν αὐτῷ ὁ Ἰησοῦς). Luke does not correct this. The equation is deliberate: what God does and what Jesus does are, for Luke, the same thing. This is christology by narrative structure rather than theological statement — the most characteristically Lukan way of making the highest possible claim.
[^12]: **KULTUR** — *Twelve years of impurity*: Under Leviticus 15:25-30, a woman with a continuous discharge of blood was ritually impure for as long as the bleeding lasted — plus seven days after it stopped. For twelve years, this woman could not enter the Temple, could not participate in community worship, and rendered impure anyone or anything she touched. Chairs she sat on, beds she slept in, people who brushed against her — all became unclean. The social isolation was total and enforced by religious law. When she touches Jesus, she knowingly risks making him impure. What happens instead — power flowing from him to her — reverses the entire purity system.
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *The edge of his cloak (κράσπεδον)*: The κράσπεδον is the tassel or fringe (Hebrew: tzitzit) commanded in Numbers 15:38-41 — a visible reminder of God's commandments, worn on the four corners of a Jewish man's garment. The woman's touch is not random; she reaches for the most religiously significant part of the garment. Her approach from behind — furtive, desperate, terrified of exposure — reveals someone who knows that her touch contaminates. She expects healing but fears the social consequences of being identified.
[^14]: **LEBEN** — *came forward... trembling*: The woman's instinct was anonymity — get the healing, slip away unnoticed. Twelve years of isolation had taught her to be invisible. But Jesus refuses anonymous healing. He stops, asks, makes her tell her story “in front of everyone.” This looks like exposure; it is actually restoration. Private healing would have left her carrying a secret. Public acknowledgment restores her to community. She came for physical cure; she left with a name (“Daughter”), a relationship, and public vindication. The pattern resonates across traditions: healing often requires coming out of hiding. The courage to be seen — to name the need aloud — is itself an act of faith.
[^15]: **TEXT** — *Made you well (σέσωκέν σε)*: The Greek σῴζω means both “to heal” and “to save” — and Luke refuses to choose between them. “Your faith has made you well” is medically accurate; “your faith has saved you” is theologically accurate. Both are happening simultaneously. Luke uses this verb at pivotal moments (7:50, 8:48, 17:19, 18:42) always in contexts where physical restoration and spiritual restoration overlap. The double meaning is not ambiguity but precision: in Luke's theology, there is no salvation that does not touch the body, and no healing that is merely physical.
[^16]: **KONTEXT** — *Twelve and twelve*: Jairus's daughter has lived twelve years. The woman has suffered twelve years. The structural parallel is not accidental — Luke uses the number to bind two stories about female bodies and divine power. The girl's twelve years mark the threshold of womanhood in Jewish tradition; the woman's twelve years of bleeding began when the girl was born. One life beginning as another was being consumed. Jesus' authority encompasses both: the chronic and the acute, the long suffering and the sudden crisis.
[^17]: **LEBEN** — *Don't be afraid — just believe*: The messenger has just said the worst words a parent can hear. Jesus' response asks faith to operate past the point where hope makes sense. This is not optimism or denial. It is an invitation to trust in a reality that contradicts the evidence. Communities of faith across traditions have wrestled with this moment: does “just believe” mean suppress grief? Ignore reality? The text suggests otherwise. Jesus does not minimize the death — he enters the house of mourning. He does not rebuke the mourners for grieving but for laughing. Faith here is not the absence of fear but its companion: belief that holds when the facts say otherwise.
[^18]: **KONTEXT** — *Get up (ἔγειρε)*: The verb ἐγείρω is resurrection vocabulary — the same word used for Jesus' own rising (Luke 24:6, 24:34) and for the eschatological raising of the dead. When Jesus says “Child, get up,” ancient ears heard not just a command to a sleeping girl but an anticipation of something larger. This moment — a dead child restored by a word and a touch — foreshadows the ultimate claim: that death itself is not final.
[^19]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Her spirit returned*: Three frames: (1) Medical: Children declared dead in antiquity sometimes presented symptoms mimicking death — catalepsy, coma, severe shock. Jesus' own words (“She is not dead, but sleeping”) have been read as literal diagnosis. Ancient communities lacked diagnostic tools to distinguish clinical death from deep unconsciousness, and the mourners' certainty does not constitute medical confirmation. (2) Symbolic-theological: The raising functions as the climax of a chapter about escalating authority — over nature (the storm), over demonic forces (Legion), and now over death itself. Whether historical event or theological narrative, the text presents a deliberate progression that reveals who Jesus is. (3) Resurrection anticipation: The verb ἐγείρω (“get up”) is the same word used for Jesus' own resurrection (24:6, 34). Luke frames this moment as preview — death is not the final word. The mourners laugh because they know death is irreversible. The parents cannot speak because they have just witnessed evidence that it is not.

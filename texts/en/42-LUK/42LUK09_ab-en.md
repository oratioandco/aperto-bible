## Kapitel 9


### The Sending of the Twelve


**1** Jesus called the Twelve together.*Twelve — the number of Israel's tribes, as if he were forming God's people all over again.* He gave them power over all demons and authority over every disease.*Not just information. Not just a message. Power — and the right to use it.*
[^1]
**2** Then he sent them out to proclaim the kingdom of God and to heal the sick.
**3** “Take nothing for the journey. No staff, no bag, no bread, no money. Don't even pack a second shirt.”
[^2]
**4** *They would walk into each village empty-handed, dependent on whoever opened a door to them.* “Whatever house takes you in, stay there until you move on.
**5** And wherever they won't receive you — leave. Shake the dust from your feet as you go.”*It was the gesture Jews reserved for leaving pagan territory. Directed at a Jewish town, it said: you have placed yourselves outside God's people.*
[^3]
**6** So they went out, village by village, proclaiming the good news and healing people everywhere they went.

### Herod's Perplexity


**7** Word reached Herod*— Rome's appointed ruler in Galilee, the same man who had silenced John the Baptist by taking his head*. Reports were coming in from everywhere: healings, exorcisms, crowds gathering around a teacher from Nazareth.
**8** Some said John had been raised from the dead. Others said Elijah had appeared. Others said one of the ancient prophets had risen.
[^4]
**9** “John,” Herod said. “I beheaded him.”*The admission hung in the air.* “So who is this I keep hearing about?”

[^5]

### Jesus Feeds Five Thousand


**10** When the apostles returned, they told Jesus what they had done. He took them and withdrew privately toward Bethsaida.
**11** But the crowds found out where he was going and followed him. He welcomed them — spoke to them about the kingdom of God, healed those who needed it.

**12** Late in the day, the Twelve came to him. “Send them away. Let them find food and lodging in the villages. There's nothing here.”

**13** “You give them something to eat,” he said.

[^6]
**14** There were five thousand men alone*— women and children uncounted*.

[^7]
**15** They did.
**16** Then Jesus took the five loaves and the two fish. He looked up to heaven. He blessed them. He broke them. And he kept giving them to the disciples*— piece after piece after piece —* to set before the crowd.
[^8]
**17** They all ate. They all had their fill. And when the disciples gathered what was left over, they filled twelve baskets.*Twelve — the number of Israel's tribes.*
[^9]

### Peter's Confession


**18** Jesus was alone, praying. His disciples were nearby. He turned to them.
**19** “Who do the crowds say I am?”

**20** “But you.” He looked at them. “Who do you say I am?”

[^10]
**21** *The word hung between them — the most dangerous word in the language. Messiah: the long-awaited deliverer, the one who would set everything right.* Jesus ordered them to tell no one.
[^11]
**22** “The Son of Man must suffer greatly — be rejected by the elders, the chief priests, the legal scholars — be killed.” He paused. “And on the third day, be raised.”

[^12]

### The Cost of Following


**23** Then he said to them all*— not just the Twelve, but everyone*:

[^13]
[^14]

**24** “Whoever tries to hold on to their life will lose it. But whoever lets go of their life for my sake — that person will save it.
[^15]
**25** What does it profit you to gain the whole world and forfeit your very self?
**26** If you're ashamed of me and my words, the Son of Man will be ashamed of you when he comes in his glory — and in the glory of the Father and the holy angels.”
**27** Then he added: “I tell you the truth. Some of you standing right here will not taste death before you see the kingdom of God.”
[^16]

### The Transfiguration


**28** About eight days later, Jesus took Peter, John, and James up a mountain to pray.
[^17]
**29** While he was praying, his face changed. His clothes turned dazzling white — bright as lightning,*the kind of light that hurts to look at*.
[^18]
**30** Then two figures appeared beside him, shining with the same glory: Moses and Elijah*— the Law and the Prophets, everything God had ever revealed*.
**31** They were speaking with him about his departure*— his exodus, the word itself —* which he was about to accomplish in Jerusalem.
[^19]
**32** Peter and the others had been heavy with sleep. When they woke — fully, suddenly — they saw the glory. They saw the two men standing with Jesus.
**33** And as Moses and Elijah began to leave, Peter blurted out: “Master, it's good that we're here. Let us put up three shelters — one for you, one for Moses, one for Elijah.” He had no idea what he was saying.
[^20]
**34** He was still talking when a cloud came down and closed around them.*Not weather. The presence of God — the Sinai cloud, the Temple cloud.* Fear seized them as it swallowed them whole.
**35** Then a voice, from inside the cloud:

[^21]
**36** When the voice had finished, Jesus was alone.*No Moses. No Elijah. No cloud.*


### Healing of a Boy with an Unclean Spirit


**37** The next day, they came down from the mountain.*Glory behind them, chaos ahead.*
[^22]
**38** A crowd was waiting. A man pushed through it, shouting: “Teacher, I beg you — look at my son. He's my only child.
**39** A spirit seizes him — he screams, convulses, foams at the mouth. It barely lets him go, and when it does, it leaves him broken.
[^23]
**40** I begged your disciples to drive it out.”*His voice cracked.* “They couldn't.”

**41** “You faithless, twisted generation,” Jesus said. “How long must I be with you? How long must I put up with you?” Then, quieter: “Bring him here.”
[^24]
**42** Even as the boy approached, the demon slammed him to the ground. But Jesus rebuked the spirit, healed the boy, and gave him back to his father.
**43** Everyone stood in silence — stunned at the majesty of God.

### The Second Prediction and the Dispute About Greatness


**44** “Let these words sink into your ears.”*His voice was low.* “The Son of Man is about to be handed over — into human hands.”
[^25]
**45** They didn't understand. The meaning was hidden from them, somehow sealed off, and they were afraid to ask what he meant.
**46** Then*— while he carried that knowledge alone —* an argument broke out among them: which of them was the greatest.
**47** Jesus saw it. He took a child*— a child, who in that world had no status, no voice, no leverage —* and set the child beside him,*in the place of honor*.
[^26]
**48** “Whoever welcomes this child in my name welcomes me,” he said. “And whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me. For the least among all of you — that is the one who is great.”

**49** John said, “Master, we saw someone driving out demons in your name, and we tried to stop him. He's not one of us.”

**50** “Don't stop him,” Jesus said. “Whoever is not against you is for you.”

### A Samaritan Village Rejects Jesus


**51** The time was approaching*— the time for everything that Jerusalem held*. Jesus set his face toward it.*The old Hebrew expression for resolve that does not flinch: he set his face like flint, knowing what was ahead.*
[^27]
[^28]
**52** He sent messengers to a Samaritan village to arrange lodging.
**53** They came back empty-handed. The Samaritans wouldn't receive him — not because of who he was, but because of where he was going.*Jerusalem. The rival city. Centuries of enmity between Jews and Samaritans, and Jesus was walking straight toward the other side's shrine.*
[^29]
**54** James and John were furious. “Lord, do you want us to call fire down from heaven to destroy them?”*They meant it. Elijah had done exactly that.*
[^30]
**55** Jesus turned — and rebuked them.*Not the Samaritans. The disciples.*
**56** They went on to another village.

### The Cost of Following Jesus


**57** As they walked, someone said to Jesus, “I will follow you wherever you go.”

**58** Jesus replied, “Foxes have dens. Birds have nests. But the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.”
**59** To another he said, “Follow me.”

[^31]
[^32]
**60** “Let the dead bury their own dead,” Jesus said. “You — go and proclaim the kingdom of God.”
**61** Another said, “I will follow you, Lord. But first let me go back and say goodbye to my family.”
[^33]

**62** Jesus said, “No one who puts a hand to the plow and then looks back is fit for the kingdom of God.”
[^34]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *the Twelve*: In Jewish thought, an authorized agent (shaliach) carried the full authority of the one who sent them — “a person's agent is like the person himself” (later rabbinic principle). The number twelve is not incidental: it corresponds to the twelve tribes of Israel. By commissioning twelve agents, Jesus was symbolically reconstituting God's people.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *take nothing*: Ancient travelers depended on hospitality — and Jewish culture obligated hosts to receive teachers and travelers. Carrying no bag (used by Cynic philosophers for begging) distinguished Jesus' disciples from wandering philosophers. The radical poverty was a prophetic sign of total dependence on God's provision through human generosity.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *shake the dust from your feet*: Pious Jews shook the dust off their feet when leaving Gentile (non-Jewish) land, treating it as ritually unclean. To perform this gesture toward a Jewish town was an extreme prophetic judgment — declaring that this community, by rejecting God's messengers, had placed itself outside the covenant people. It was street theater with theological weight.
[^4]: **KULTUR** — *Elijah had appeared*: The prophet Malachi promised that Elijah would return before “the great and terrible day of the Lord” (Malachi 4:5). This expectation was alive in Jesus' time — many believed Elijah's reappearance would signal that God was about to act decisively. All three popular identifications of Jesus (John raised, Elijah returned, ancient prophet risen) placed him squarely in the prophetic tradition.
[^5]: **KONTEXT** — *kept trying to see him*: This haunting detail creates a narrative thread that runs through Luke's Gospel. Herod's “seeking to see” Jesus will not be fulfilled until the trial narrative (23:8), when Pilate sends Jesus to Herod. By then it is too late for both of them — Herod treats Jesus as entertainment, and Jesus refuses to speak.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *buy food for all of them*: Mark's account specifies the cost at roughly 200 denarii — about seven months' wages for a day laborer. The economic impossibility was not just inconvenience but absurdity. The disciples were doing basic math: even if they could find a market in this wilderness, the bill would be ruinous.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *groups of fifty*: The organization echoes Exodus 18:21, where Moses arranged Israel in the wilderness by thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. Luke's readers steeped in Scripture would have noticed: Jesus is the new Moses, organizing the new Israel in a deserted place, preparing to feed them with bread from heaven.
[^8]: **KONTEXT** — *took... blessed... broke... kept giving*: These four verbs — took, blessed, broke, gave — are the exact sequence Jesus will perform at the Last Supper (Luke 22:19). Early Christians heard the Eucharist prefigured in this feeding. The Greek verb for “gave” (ἐδίδου) is in the imperfect tense, indicating continuous action: he kept giving, and the bread did not run out.
[^9]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *they filled twelve baskets*: How does a single meal for one or two people feed thousands? The text presents this as a miraculous sign, not a parable. Three readings coexist without needing to cancel each other: (1) a literal multiplication — divine power overriding natural limits; (2) an inspired sharing — Jesus' generosity unlocking the crowd's hoarded food; (3) a theological sign — this is the messianic banquet, the new manna, God feeding the new Israel in the wilderness as he fed the old. The twelve baskets (κόφινοι, distinctly Jewish wicker baskets) — one for each tribe — signal that the provision is for all of God's people, with abundance to spare.
[^10]: **KULTUR** — *God's Messiah (τὸν χριστὸν τοῦ θεοῦ)*: “Messiah” (Hebrew) or “Christ” (Greek) means “the Anointed One” — not a name but a title, and the most politically loaded title in the Jewish vocabulary. In Jesus' time, most expected the Messiah to be a military-political liberator: a descendant of David who would drive out Rome, purify the Temple, and restore Israel's independence. Peter's confession was correct but catastrophically incomplete — he said the right word while imagining the wrong thing.
[^11]: **TEXT** — *tell no one*: Why silence? Because popular messianic expectations were so politically charged that premature disclosure would have distorted Jesus' mission. He needed to redefine what “Messiah” meant — through suffering, not military triumph — before the title could be used publicly without triggering the wrong kind of following.
[^12]: **KULTUR** — *the Son of Man*: Jesus' preferred self-designation, drawn from Daniel 7:13-14 — a heavenly figure who receives an eternal kingdom from God. Less politically explosive than “Messiah,” it allowed Jesus to fill the title with new content: the Son of Man who would receive glory through suffering. The word “must” (δεῖ) expresses divine necessity — this is God's redemptive plan, not bad luck or fate.
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *take up their cross*: Crucifixion was Rome's punishment for slaves, criminals, and political rebels — the most shameful death available. Condemned men carried the crossbeam (patibulum) through jeering crowds to the execution site. Jesus' listeners had seen this spectacle. “Take up your cross” was not a metaphor for inconvenience — it meant: walk toward your own death. Luke alone adds “daily,” transforming a single heroic act into repeated, ordinary self-denial.
[^14]: **LEBEN** — *set themselves aside (ἀρνησάσθω ἑαυτόν)*: Self-denial is not self-hatred. The Greek means “disown yourself” or “dethrone the ego” — reordering what matters so that self-preservation is no longer the organizing principle of your life. Christian traditions across centuries have wrestled with this: the monastic movement took it literally (renouncing possessions), the Reformers emphasized daily repentance, liberation theologians connect it to solidarity with the marginalized. None equate it with destroying the self God created and loves.
[^15]: **TEXT** — *their life (ψυχή)*: The Greek ψυχή means “life” or “self” — the whole person, not the “soul” in the later Greek philosophical sense of a separate entity trapped in a body. Jesus is not offering afterlife insurance. He is talking about your entire existence: how you spend your days, what you organize your life around, what you cling to and what you release.
[^16]: **TEXT** — *will not taste death before they see the kingdom of God*: Among the most debated sayings in the Gospels. The most common scholarly readings: (1) the Transfiguration, which follows immediately — three disciples see Jesus' glory revealed; (2) the resurrection and Pentecost — the kingdom powerfully manifested; (3) the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE — God's judgment enacted. The text creates urgency: something is imminent, and these listeners will witness it.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *about eight days*: Mark writes “six days”; Luke writes “about eight.” Some scholars see resurrection symbolism: in early Christianity, the “eighth day” was associated with new creation and resurrection (Christ rose on the first day of the new week — the eighth day of the old). Others simply note Luke's approximating style. Either way, the deliberate variation suggests theological intention.
[^18]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *his face changed*: The Transfiguration is one of the most explicitly supernatural events in the Gospels: Jesus' face changes, his clothes become radiant beyond natural explanation, two long-dead figures appear and speak with him, a cloud of divine presence descends, and a voice speaks from inside it. For modern readers, this raises obvious questions. Three frameworks coexist: (1) Symbolic — the story is a literary or theological construct expressing Jesus' divine identity, drawing on Moses at Sinai (Exodus 34:29-35) and Elijah's heavenly departure (2 Kings 2:11). (2) Visionary — the disciples had a genuine experience of altered consciousness, like Paul's Damascus road encounter (Acts 9) or mystics across centuries who report overwhelming light. (3) Historical-faith — Jesus' divine nature was briefly revealed in its full reality; what the disciples saw was not illusion but disclosure of who Jesus actually is. The earliest Christians treated this as historical event, not metaphor — Peter's second letter references it as eyewitness testimony: “We were eyewitnesses of his majesty” (2 Peter 1:16-18). All three Synoptic Gospels report it independently. The text's own framing is unmistakable: this happened, it was terrifying, and the disciples never forgot it.
[^19]: **KONTEXT** — *his departure — his exodus (ἔξοδον)*: Luke's word for Jesus' impending death is ἔξοδος — the same word as the book of Exodus. This cannot be accidental. Jesus' death and resurrection will be a new liberation: as God freed Israel from slavery in Egypt, so this “exodus” in Jerusalem will free people from bondage to sin and death. Moses and Elijah — both associated with Mount Sinai/Horeb — are discussing the new Exodus with the one who will accomplish it.
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *three shelters (σκηνάς)*: Peter's suggestion echoes the Jewish Festival of Sukkot (Tabernacles/Booths), which celebrated God dwelling with Israel during the wilderness wandering and anticipated God's future permanent dwelling with his people. Peter may have been trying to prolong the divine encounter — or misunderstanding the moment as the arrival of the messianic age. Luke's editorial note (“he had no idea what he was saying”) is gentle but devastating.
[^21]: **KONTEXT** — *my Son, my Chosen One. Listen to him*: Three Old Testament citations compressed into one declaration: “my Son” echoes Psalm 2:7 (royal enthronement of the Messiah-king); “my Chosen One” echoes Isaiah 42:1 (the Suffering Servant — Luke uses this instead of Mark's “Beloved”); “listen to him” quotes Deuteronomy 18:15 (the prophet greater than Moses). God commands obedience to Jesus over everything that came before — Law, Prophets, and all.
[^22]: **KONTEXT** — *came down from the mountain*: The descent from mountaintop glory to valley chaos mirrors Moses descending from Sinai (Exodus 32). Moses came down with his face shining and found Israel worshipping the golden calf. Jesus comes down from the Transfiguration and finds a faithless generation. Glory above, disorder below — the pattern repeats.
[^23]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *a spirit seizes him*: Ancient people attributed these symptoms — seizures, convulsions, foaming, sudden collapse — to spiritual forces. Whether understood as literal demonic possession or as a way of describing conditions we might now call epilepsy or other neurological disorders, the text's point is not the diagnosis but the authority: Jesus has power over whatever destroys human life. The father's desperation is the same regardless of the framework — his child is suffering, and nothing has helped.
[^24]: **KONTEXT** — *faithless, twisted generation*: Jesus' lament echoes Deuteronomy 32:5, 20 — Moses' frustrated words about Israel in the wilderness: “They are a warped and crooked generation” and “children who are unfaithful.” Jesus, the new Moses, encounters the same faithlessness. But where Moses shattered the stone tablets in anger, Jesus heals.
[^25]: **TEXT** — *handed over (παραδίδοσθαι) ... into human hands*: “Handed over” is a technical term in the passion narrative — the same word used for Judas' betrayal (22:4, 6, 21, 48). Ancient Christians hearing this would have flinched. “Into human hands” is doubly ominous: the Son of Man, who belongs in God's hands, will be delivered into human ones. The contrast between divine origin and human violence is the point.
[^26]: **KULTUR** — *took a child*: In the ancient Mediterranean world, children had no social status, no legal standing, and no voice in public affairs. They were not sentimentalized as innocent or precious — they were simply powerless. Jesus placing a child beside himself, in the position of honor where his most important associate would stand, was a deliberate provocation. He was physically demonstrating what he had just said about the “least.”
[^27]: **KONTEXT** — *set his face*: “He set his face” echoes Isaiah 50:7 — the Suffering Servant who faces opposition without flinching: “I set my face like flint, and I know I will not be put to shame.” Luke sees Jesus' journey to Jerusalem as the Servant's journey to suffering. This verse opens the “travel narrative” (9:51–19:44) that will dominate Luke's Gospel from here to the triumphal entry.
[^28]: **TEXT** — *the time for him to be taken up (ἀνάλημψις)*: Luke's word ἀνάλημψις (“being taken up”) is deliberately comprehensive — it includes not just Jesus' death but his resurrection and ascension as well. The entire arc of what Jerusalem holds: suffering, death, vindication, and exaltation. Jesus faces all of it with resolute clarity.
[^29]: **KULTUR** — *the Samaritans wouldn't receive him*: Jews and Samaritans had been enemies for centuries. Samaritans maintained their own version of the Torah and worshipped at their own temple on Mount Gerizim, not in Jerusalem. The hostility was mutual and deep — comparable to the most entrenched ethnic-religious conflicts of any era. A Jewish teacher heading to Jerusalem was heading to their rival's shrine, and they refused him for it.
[^30]: **KONTEXT** — *fire down from heaven*: The disciples' request directly echoes Elijah calling fire from heaven on the king's soldiers (2 Kings 1:10-12). James and John see themselves as Elijah figures, defending God's honor through divine violence. Jesus refuses the role entirely. The Transfiguration has just confirmed that Jesus supersedes Elijah — and his way forward is not fire but the cross.
[^31]: **KULTUR** — *bury my father*: Burying one's parents was among the most sacred obligations in the ancient world, rooted in the fifth commandment (“Honor your father and mother”). It took precedence over almost every other duty — including Torah study, in rabbinic tradition. Refusing or delaying this duty was nearly unthinkable and would bring deep shame on the entire family. Jesus' response would have been genuinely shocking — scandalous, even.
[^32]: **TEXT** — *let me first go and bury my father*: Some scholars suggest the father may not yet be dead. “Let me bury my father” could mean “let me stay home until my father dies and the estate is settled” — an indefinite delay. If so, Jesus is responding not to immediate grief but to an open-ended postponement of commitment. Either reading preserves the scandal: Jesus subordinates even sacred family obligations to the urgency of the kingdom.
[^33]: **KONTEXT** — *let me say goodbye*: When Elijah called Elisha to be his disciple (1 Kings 19:19-21), Elisha asked to say goodbye to his parents — and Elijah allowed it. Elisha went home, slaughtered his oxen, burned his plowing equipment, and only then followed. Jesus does not grant even this. The implication is staggering: Jesus' call is more urgent than Elijah's, his authority greater than the greatest prophet's.
[^34]: **LEBEN** — *hand to the plow*: The Palestinian single-handled plow required total forward attention — one hand on the plow, the other on the ox goad. Looking back meant a crooked furrow and a ruined field. The image was immediately vivid to ancient listeners and remains so today: following Jesus demands undivided commitment, not because God is harsh but because the kingdom's arrival creates a now-or-never moment. The question is not “Am I worthy?” but “Am I available?” — with my whole attention, not just my leftover time.

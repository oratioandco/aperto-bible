## Kapitel 10


### Sending the Seventy-Two


**1** After this, the Lord chose seventy-two others and sent them out ahead of him — two by two, to every town and place he planned to visit.
[^1]
**2** He told them: “The harvest is vast, and the workers so few. Beg the lord of the harvest to drive workers out into his fields.
**3** “Go. I am sending you like lambs into a pack of wolves.
**4** “Carry no money. No bag. No extra sandals. And don't stop for roadside greetings*— there is no time*.
**5** “When you enter a house, say this first: 'Peace to this house.'
**6** If a person of peace lives there*— someone whose heart is open —* your peace will settle on them. If not, it will come back to you.
**7** Stay in that house. Eat and drink whatever they give you — a worker earns his keep. Don't go looking for better lodgings.
**8** “When you enter a town and they welcome you, eat whatever they set before you.
[^2]
**9** Heal the sick among them. And tell them: 'God's kingdom has come close to you.'
**10** “But when you enter a town and they turn you away, go into their streets and say:
**11** 'Even the dust of your town clinging to our feet — we wipe it off against you. But know this: God's kingdom has*still* come close.'
**12** “I tell you, on that day it will go easier for Sodom than for that town.”
[^3]

**13** “What grief awaits you, Chorazin. What grief awaits you, Bethsaida.”*These were lakeside towns that had seen his miracles firsthand.* “If the miracles done in you had been done in*the pagan cities of* Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago, sitting in sackcloth and ashes.
**14** But at the judgment, Tyre and Sidon will fare better than you.
**15** “And you, Capernaum”*— his own home base, the town that knew him best —* “do you think you'll be lifted to heaven? You will be brought down to the depths.”
[^4]
**16** *He looked at them.* “Whoever listens to you listens to me. Whoever rejects you rejects me. And whoever rejects me rejects the one who sent me.”

### The Return of the Seventy-Two


**17** The seventy-two came back breathless with excitement. “Lord,” they said, “even the demons submit to us in your name!”
**18** He said to them, “I was watching Satan fall from heaven like lightning.
[^5]
[^6]
**19** “I have given you authority to trample on snakes and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy. Nothing will harm you.
[^7]
[^8]
**20** But don't rejoice because the spirits submit to you. Rejoice that your names are *already* written in heaven.”
[^9]

**21** In that same moment, Jesus was overcome with joy through the Holy Spirit. “I praise you, Father,” he said — “Lord of heaven and earth — because you have hidden these things from the wise and the educated and revealed them to children. Yes, Father. This was your good pleasure.”
**22** *Then he said:* “Everything has been entrusted to me by my Father. No one knows who the Son is except the Father, and no one knows who the Father is except the Son — and anyone the Son chooses to reveal him to.”
[^10]
**23** He turned to his disciples alone. “How privileged you are — seeing what you see.
**24** I tell you, many prophets and kings longed to see what you are seeing — and never saw it. They longed to hear what you are hearing — and never heard it.”

### The Good Samaritan


**25** An expert in the Law stood up to test Jesus. “Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?”
[^11]
**26** “What is written in the Law?” Jesus asked. “How do you read it?”
**27** “Love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your strength, and with all your mind,” the man replied. “And love your neighbor as yourself.”
**28** “Correct,” Jesus said. “Do this, and you will live.”
**29** But the man wanted to justify himself. “And who,” he asked, “is my neighbor?”

**30** Jesus said:

[^12]
**31** A priest came down that same road. He saw the man*— half-dead, possibly already a corpse — and touching a corpse would disqualify a priest from temple service*. He crossed to the other side and walked on.
[^13]
**32** A Levite came to the same spot. He looked at the man. He too crossed to the other side.
**33** Then a Samaritan*— the last person anyone wanted in this story —* came along the road.
[^14]

**34** He saw the beaten man. Something turned in his gut.
[^15]

**35** The next morning he took out two silver coins*— two days' wages —* and gave them to the innkeeper. “Look after him,” he said. “Whatever more you spend, I'll repay when I come back.”
[^16]

**36** *Jesus turned to the scholar.* “Which of these three proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the bandits?”
**37** *The scholar couldn't bring himself to say the word.* “The one,” he said, “who showed him mercy.”
[^17]


### At the Home of Martha and Mary


**38** They came to a village where a woman named Martha welcomed him into her home.
**39** Her sister Mary sat at the Lord's feet*— the posture of a disciple —* and listened to his teaching.
[^18]
**40** But Martha was being pulled in every direction by all the preparations. She came to him and said:


**41** “Martha, Martha,” the Lord answered. “You are worried and restless about so many things.
**42** But one thing is needed. Mary has chosen the better part — and no one will take it from her.”
[^19]
[^20]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *seventy-two*: Some manuscripts read “seventy” rather than “seventy-two.” The difference matters: seventy connects to Moses appointing seventy elders who received the Spirit (Numbers 11:24–25). Seventy-two connects to the table of nations in Genesis 10 (LXX), where seventy-two peoples descend from Noah — representing all humanity. If Luke wrote “seventy-two,” the mission to these towns prefigures the gospel going to all nations. The earliest and best manuscripts (P75, Codex Vaticanus) support “seventy-two.”
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *eat whatever they set before you*: This instruction may carry more weight than it appears. Observant Jews followed dietary laws (kashrut) that regulated what could be eaten and how food was prepared. “Eat whatever they set before you” could mean: don't ask questions about the food's preparation. If the seventy-two entered Gentile or Samaritan households, this would mean eating non-kosher food — a radical loosening of purity boundaries for the sake of mission. The same principle appears later in Acts 10, when Peter is told to eat what he formerly considered unclean.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *easier for Sodom*: Modern readers associate Sodom primarily with sexual sin. But in Jewish tradition, Sodom's defining crime was the violation of sacred hospitality — specifically, the violent rejection of divine messengers (Genesis 19). The prophet Ezekiel names Sodom's sins as pride, excess of food, prosperous ease, and failure to help the poor and needy (Ezekiel 16:49). Jesus' comparison is precise: towns that reject God's messengers repeat Sodom's original offense. And yet Sodom never saw the miracles these towns witnessed — making their rejection worse.
[^4]: **KONTEXT** — *lifted to heaven ... brought down to the depths*: Jesus is quoting Isaiah 14:13–15, originally addressed to the king of Babylon, who boasted: “I will ascend to heaven... I will make myself like the Most High.” God's response: “You are brought down to Sheol, to the far reaches of the pit.” By applying Babylon's judgment to Capernaum — his own base of operations — Jesus pronounces devastating sentence on the town that saw the most and responded the least. Privilege without response becomes the deepest form of rejection.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *I was watching (Ἐθεώρουν)*: The Greek verb is imperfect tense — “I was watching,” not “I saw.” This is not a report of a past event (like a primordial fall of angels) but a description of something Jesus was observing in real-time during the seventy-two's mission. As they cast out demons in villages across the countryside, Jesus perceived the cosmic dimension of what was happening: Satan's dominion crumbling, one exorcism at a time. The local and the cosmic are happening simultaneously.
[^6]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *demons submit ... Satan fall*: Demons obeying. Satan plummeting from heaven. For modern readers, this language raises immediate questions. The text presents a worldview in which invisible spiritual forces are real — and Jesus claims to perceive their cosmic activity during an ordinary mission trip across the Galilean countryside. Three frameworks for reading this: (1) Symbolic — Satan and demons are metaphors for systemic evil and oppressive structures, and Jesus describes their collapse in vivid mythological language. (2) Psychological — “demons” represent forms of mental illness, social exclusion, or spiritual bondage, and the disciples' ministry brought healing and restoration that Jesus interprets in cosmic terms. (3) The historic Christian reading — spiritual beings exist, Satan is a real adversary, and Jesus perceived the cosmic dimension of what was happening as his followers proclaimed the kingdom in village after village. The text itself presents this as real-time observation: Jesus was watching (imperfect tense) something actually unfolding. His overwhelming joy in verse 21 is the response of someone witnessing victory, not constructing a metaphor.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *trample on snakes and scorpions*: This echoes Psalm 91:13 — “You will tread on the lion and the cobra; you will trample the great lion and the serpent.” In that psalm, God promises protection to the one who trusts him completely. Jesus takes a promise made about God's protection and applies it to his disciples' authority. The language is both literal (snakes and scorpions were real dangers on Palestinian roads) and symbolic (representing hostile spiritual forces). The disciples receive what the psalm attributes to God's own care.
[^8]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Nothing will harm you*: A bold promise — and one that raises immediate questions. Did the early Christians experience harm? They did: persecution, imprisonment, execution. Three ways to read this: (1) A situational promise for the seventy-two's specific mission, not a blanket guarantee for all time. (2) A spiritual promise — nothing can harm your ultimate standing with God, even if your body suffers. Paul makes this argument in Romans 8:35–39. (3) A literal promise that held true during the apostolic mission, as Acts records remarkable protections (Acts 28:3–6, Paul bitten by a viper and unharmed). The text doesn't resolve which reading is definitive. What it does establish: those sent by Jesus operate under his authority and his protection.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *names written in heaven*: The image of a heavenly book recording the righteous appears throughout Jewish literature — Exodus 32:32, Isaiah 4:3, Daniel 12:1, Malachi 3:16, and extensively in the Book of Enoch and Jubilees. For people living under Roman occupation with no earthly security, heavenly enrollment meant ultimate citizenship in a kingdom no empire could overthrow. Jesus redirects the disciples' joy: not from what they can do (cast out demons) but from who they are (known and claimed by God). Identity over performance.
[^10]: **TEXT** — *No one knows who the Son is except the Father*: This verse stands out in Luke as an extraordinarily high Christological claim — closer in tone to the Gospel of John than to the rest of Luke's narrative. Jesus claims exclusive mutual knowledge between himself and God: a relationship so intimate that only the Father truly knows the Son, and only the Son truly knows the Father. This is not a teacher pointing to God; this is someone claiming to stand in a unique, unrepeatable relationship with God. The claim would have been either deeply compelling or deeply offensive to first-century Jewish listeners — and still is.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *expert in the Law (νομικός)*: Not a modern lawyer or attorney. A νομικός was a professional Torah scholar — someone who had devoted years to studying, interpreting, and applying the Jewish Law (Torah). Think of a top professor of constitutional law, not a courtroom advocate. His question (“What must I do to inherit eternal life?”) was standard rabbinic format. But Luke tells us his motive: ἐκπειράζων — “testing” Jesus, the same verb used for Satan tempting Jesus in the wilderness (Luke 4:12). This is a public challenge, not a sincere inquiry.
[^12]: **KULTUR** — *the road from Jerusalem down to Jericho*: This was a real road with a grim reputation. It descended 3,400 feet over seventeen miles — from Jerusalem at 2,500 feet elevation to Jericho at 800 feet below sea level. The route wound through barren Judean wilderness with caves perfect for ambush. It was known as the “Way of Blood” (דֶּרֶךְ הַדָּם) because of frequent robberies and violence. Jesus' audience knew this road. Some had walked it. The danger was not hypothetical.
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *touching a corpse would disqualify a priest*: Under Jewish purity law (Numbers 19:11–22), contact with a dead body made a person ritually unclean for seven days — unable to perform temple duties, excluded from worship, requiring an elaborate purification process. The beaten man was “half-dead” — the Greek word (ἡμιθανής) creates deliberate ambiguity. Is he alive or dead? The priest cannot tell without touching him. If dead, the priest is contaminated. The parable forces a question: when do religious obligations excuse you from human ones?
[^14]: **KULTUR** — *a Samaritan*: Modern English uses “Good Samaritan” to mean a helpful stranger — which completely erases the original shock. Samaritans and Jews had despised each other for centuries. Samaritans worshipped on Mount Gerizim, not Jerusalem; they accepted only the Torah, not the Prophets; they were considered half-breed heretics by mainstream Judaism. The hostility was mutual: Luke records that a Samaritan village refused to receive Jesus (9:52–53). When Jesus makes a Samaritan the hero of this story — the one who does what two Jewish religious professionals fail to do — he is not telling a heartwarming tale. He is detonating his audience's deepest prejudices.
[^15]: **LEBEN** — *Something turned in his gut*: Compassion in this story is not a moral decision — it is a physical event. The Greek (σπλαγχνίζομαι) describes an involuntary gut response to the sight of suffering — the same word used when Jesus sees crowds, lepers, grieving mothers. The Samaritan does not weigh costs or calculate risks. Something seizes him before reasoning begins. The parable's uncomfortable question is not “Should I help strangers?” but “What have I learned to walk past?” Every profession, every routine, every social position develops its own forms of trained blindness — the suffering that no longer registers because it has become familiar. The priest and Levite were not monsters. They were professionals managing competing obligations. The Samaritan was the outsider whose defenses had not been trained to filter this particular suffering out.
[^16]: **KULTUR** — *two silver coins — two days' wages*: Two denarii was roughly two days' wages for a day laborer. But the real generosity is the open-ended promise: “Whatever more you spend, I'll repay.” The Samaritan doesn't set a limit. He provides first aid (oil to soothe, wine to disinfect), transportation (his own animal — meaning he walked), overnight care (personal nursing), financial provision (two days' wages upfront), and a blank check for ongoing expenses. Each detail is a rebuke to the priest and Levite who did nothing. And the Samaritan promises to return — this is not a one-time rescue but an ongoing commitment.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *The one who showed him mercy*: Notice what the scholar does not say. He does not say “the Samaritan.” He cannot bring himself to name the enemy as the hero of the story. Instead he uses a circumlocution: “the one who showed mercy.” This evasion is itself the point. The parable has cornered him: he must acknowledge that the despised outsider did what the religious insiders failed to do, but his prejudice is too deep to speak the word. Luke records this detail with devastating precision. And Jesus' final command — “Go and do the same” — means: become like the person you can't even name.
[^18]: **KULTUR** — *sat at the Lord's feet — the posture of a disciple*: “Sitting at someone's feet” was not a casual posture. It was the formal position of a student learning from a rabbi — what Paul describes when he says he was “educated at the feet of Gamaliel” (Acts 22:3). In first-century Judaism, this educational relationship was reserved for men. Women could listen to teaching from the margins, but they did not sit as formal disciples. Mary is crossing a gender boundary. And Jesus, rather than correcting her, will affirm her choice as “the better part.” This scene is one of the earliest recorded validations of women as full participants in religious learning.
[^19]: **KONTEXT** — *the better part (τὴν ἀγαθὴν μερίδα)*: “The good portion” uses inheritance language — choosing your share. It echoes Psalm 16:5: “The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup.” Mary has chosen her inheritance, and it is the presence and teaching of Jesus himself. The phrase “no one will take it from her” responds directly to Martha's request — Martha asked Jesus to remove Mary from this position. His answer: this choice is permanent, irrevocable. What Martha wanted redistributed, Jesus declares secured.
[^20]: **LEBEN** — *worried and restless about so many things ... one thing is needed*: Luke places this scene immediately after the Good Samaritan, which commands action — “Go and do the same.” Then Martha, the one doing, is gently corrected in favor of Mary, the one being present. The juxtaposition is deliberate and uncomfortable. It resists easy resolution. Christian communities have wrestled with this tension for two millennia: the Benedictine tradition built “ora et labora” (prayer and work) around it; the Ignatian tradition sought “contemplation in action”; liberation theology insisted that contemplation without justice is escapism. The passage does not say Martha's work is wrong — it says it is not the “one thing needed.” The question it leaves every reader: in your own frantic busyness, what is the one thing you keep postponing?

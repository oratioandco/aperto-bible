### The Finger of God


**14** He was driving out a demon — one that had stolen a man's voice.[^10] *In that world, a man who could not speak could not testify in court, could not recite the Shema, could not seal a contract or call out his own name in a crowd. He had been cut off from everything.*

**15** The crowd was stunned. But some of them said:[^11][^12]

**16** Others demanded a sign from heaven.

**17** He knew what they were thinking*— even those who had said nothing*.

**18** If Satan is fighting himself, how will his kingdom stand?
**19** And your own people drive out demons too — by whose power? They will be your judges.

**20** But if it is by the finger of God[^13] that I drive out demons*— the same finger that broke Egypt's hold on Israel —* then the kingdom of God has come upon you."[^14]


**21** "When a man fully armed guards his compound, everything he owns rests secure.[^15]
**22** But when someone stronger attacks, overpowers him, strips away every piece of armor he trusted, and divides the spoils*— that stronghold has fallen*.

**23** Whoever is not with me is against me. Whoever does not gather with me, scatters."

**24** Then he said: "When an unclean spirit leaves a person, it wanders through dry, waterless places looking for rest.[^16]
**25** Finding none, it says, 'I will return to the house I left.' It arrives and finds the house swept clean and put in order*— but empty*.
**26** So it goes and brings seven other spirits worse than itself. They move in. They settle.[^17] And the last state of that person is worse than the first."

### True Blessedness


**27** While he was still speaking, a woman raised her voice from the crowd*— uninvited*:


**28** "Yes," he said.[^18] "And even more blessed are those who hear the word of God and keep it."[^19]


### The Sign of Jonah


**29** The crowd pressed closer.[^20] He looked at them and said:

**30** Jonah was himself the sign to Nineveh*— a preacher, nothing more*. And the Son of Man will be the same to this crowd.

**31** "The Queen of the South will rise at the judgment and condemn you.[^21] She crossed the known world to hear the wisdom of one king. And something greater than Solomon is here.

**32** The men of Nineveh will stand at the judgment and condemn you.[^22] *They heard five words of judgment from a reluctant prophet — and an entire city turned.* And something greater than Jonah is here."

### The Lamp and the Eye


**33** Then he said: "No one lights a lamp and hides it. You set it where everyone who comes in can see.

**34** Your eye is the lamp of your body.[^23] When your eye is sound*— open, generous —* your whole self fills with light. But when your eye is evil*— grasping, hoarding —* you are darkness all the way through.

**35** Be careful. What you think is light inside you may actually be darkness.

**36** But if your whole being is full of light, with no corner left dark, you will be radiant — the way a dark room blazes to life the instant someone brings a lamp."

### At the Pharisee's Table


**37** While he was still speaking, a Pharisee invited him to eat. Jesus went in and reclined at the table.

**38** He had not washed.[^24]
*Not a matter of hygiene. A ritual boundary: the immersion that removed the contamination of the marketplace from your hands before you touched food in a purity-observant home. Jesus had crossed it deliberately and openly.* The Pharisee stared, astonished. *His guest had violated the norms of his own house.*

**39** Jesus looked at him. "You Pharisees clean the outside of the cup and the dish, but inside you are full of plunder and wickedness.
**40** Fools. Did not the one who made the outside make the inside too?
**41** Give what is inside as alms — and everything becomes clean for you.

**42** "Woe to you Pharisees — you tithe your mint, your rue, every herb in the garden*, counting out a tenth of each leaf*.[^25] And you pass right over justice and the love of God. You should have practiced these without neglecting those*— but you weighed the herbs and forgot what they were for*.

**43** "Woe to you Pharisees — you love the front seats in the synagogue*, the ones facing the congregation where every eye can find you*. You love the elaborate greetings in the marketplace*, the titles, the deference*.

**44** "Woe to you — you are like unmarked graves.[^26] People walk over them and never know*they have been defiled*."

**45** One of the experts in religious law spoke up:[^27] "Teacher, when you say these things, you insult us too."

**46** *Jesus turned to him.* "Then woe to you as well, you experts in the law. You load people with burdens that are crushing to carry — and you yourselves will not lift a single finger to help.

**47** "Woe to you — you build tombs for the prophets, and it was your own ancestors who killed them.
**48** So you are witnesses and approve of what they did. They killed. You build.*Neither generation listened.*

**49** "This is why the Wisdom of God said: 'I will send them prophets and apostles. Some of them they will kill. Some they will drive out.'
**50** So that the blood of every prophet shed since the foundation of the world may be charged against this generation[^28] —
**51** from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah,[^29] murdered between the altar and the sanctuary. Yes. It will be required of this generation.

**52** "Woe to you experts in the law — you have taken away the key of knowledge.[^30] You yourselves did not enter, and those who were trying to enter, you stopped*at the door*."

**53** When he left that house, the scribes and Pharisees closed ranks against him.[^31] They began pressing him with question after question, baiting him, trying to get him to say something they could use.
**54** They were lying in wait now — hunting his words.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *Teach us to pray*: In Second Temple Judaism, every movement had a distinctive prayer — the words that identified them as a community. John's disciples had theirs. The disciple is not asking for technique; he is asking for identity: give us the prayer that marks us as YOUR people.
[^2]: **KONTEXT** — *Father*: The prayer opens with a single word — "Father" — startlingly direct compared to the elaborate blessings that opened the Amidah and the Kaddish ("May your great name be glorified and sanctified..."). The intimacy is deliberate. In a Roman province, addressing God as Father also displaced Caesar's claim as *pater patriae* — "father of the fatherland." Another Father, another kingdom.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *The bread we need*: The Greek word ἐπιούσιος (epiousion) appears nowhere else in all of ancient Greek literature — a true hapax legomenon. Origen, writing around 230 CE, believed the Gospel writers coined it. Four main interpretations: "for today," "for the coming day," "necessary for existence," and "supersubstantial" (a eucharistic/eschatological reading). What is clear: this is a subsistence petition. Most of Jesus' hearers lived hand-to-mouth — "daily bread" was not metaphor but the prayer of people who did not know where tomorrow's food would come from. The echo of the manna story (Exodus 16:4) is direct: daily bread given each morning, enough for the day, not to be hoarded.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *Sins / who owes us*: Luke uses "sins" (ἁμαρτίας — moral/personal) where Matthew uses "debts" (ὀφειλήματα — economic). Both dimensions are present in Jesus' teaching. But notice: in the second half of the line, Luke switches to debt language — "everyone who owes us" (ὀφείλοντι). The community is characterized by both receiving forgiveness and extending it — the grammar describes who we are ("we are people who forgive"), not a transaction ("forgive us because we have earned it").
[^5]: **TEXT** — *Time of trial*: The traditional "lead us not into temptation" implies God might otherwise tempt — which James 1:13 explicitly denies ("God cannot be tempted by evil, and he himself tempts no one"). The Greek πειρασμόν covers both "testing" and "temptation." Pope Francis called the traditional rendering "a bad translation." The prayer asks God to shield, not to restrain from doing harm.
[^6]: **LEBEN** — *Forgive as we forgive*: HARM ACCOUNTABILITY: This phrase has been used to demand that abuse victims forgive their abusers on demand, sometimes in contexts of domestic violence and clergy abuse. The grammar does not support this: it describes a community posture ("we are people who forgive"), not a transactional prerequisite ("God won't forgive you until you forgive them"). Forgiveness of an abuser may be a long, complex spiritual journey — it is not a requirement that must be produced on demand for God's favor to activate.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *To avoid the disgrace*: The Greek word ἀναίδεια has been traditionally translated as "persistence" or "importunity" — but this misreads the parable. The word refers to the sleeping neighbor's desire to avoid social shame, not the petitioner's persistence in asking. In a village where reputation was everything, the neighbor rises because he cannot afford to be known as someone who refused a legitimate request for hospitality. The theological point shifts entirely: God does not respond because we wear God down with persistent prayer. God responds because God's own character — God's honor — makes refusal impossible. The ground of prayer is God's nature, not our effort.
[^8]: **LEBEN** — *Ask, seek, knock*: HARM ACCOUNTABILITY: "Ask and you shall receive" has been used in prosperity gospel contexts to blame the sick and poor for "insufficient faith." The promise is contextual: it follows a parable about God's character, not a guarantee of specific outcomes. The ultimate gift is the Holy Spirit (v.13), not health, wealth, or consumer goods. The Spirit is given to transform, accompany, and empower — not to deliver what the consumer desires.
[^9]: **TEXT** — *The Holy Spirit*: Matthew's version of this saying ends with "good things" (Matt 7:11). Luke deliberately replaces this with "the Holy Spirit" — the supreme gift. The entire prayer section has been building toward this: from bread to forgiveness to protection, and now to God's own presence through the Spirit. This is Luke's pneumatological distinctive: the best thing a praying person can receive is not any material provision but the Spirit of God.
[^10]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *demon*: For modern readers, the claim that a spiritual being could steal someone's capacity for speech raises fundamental questions about reality. The text presents a world where spiritual forces directly affect the human body. Three approaches: (1) The symptoms — sudden muteness, then recovery — correspond to conditions modern medicine treats neurologically or psychiatrically; "demon possession" was the diagnostic framework of the first century. (2) "Demons" function as a way of naming oppressive forces that silence people — the man is a figure for anyone whose voice has been taken. (3) The text describes real encounters with spiritual beings, consistent with a worldview in which God and spiritual forces are active agents in human experience. The historical datum matters: even Jesus' opponents acknowledged that something happened (11:15). The debate was over the source of his power, never whether anything occurred.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *Beelzebul*: The name means "lord of the (exalted) house" — possibly a deliberate corruption of Baal-zebub, "lord of flies" (2 Kings 1:2). By the first century it had become a name for the ruler of demons, equivalent to Satan. The accusation is not a theological argument — it is a political strategy: delegitimizing Jesus' authority by attributing it to the worst possible source.
[^12]: **APOLOGETICS** — *Beelzebul accusation*: This controversy is among the most historically secure material in the Gospels. It meets the criterion of embarrassment: the early church would never invent the charge that their Lord was allied with Satan. It meets multiple attestation: independent versions appear in Mark 3:22-27, Matthew 12:22-29, Luke 11:14-23, and the Gospel of Thomas (logion 35). The core historical claim — that Jesus performed actions his contemporaries identified as exorcisms, and that opponents attributed this to demonic power rather than denying it — is as well-attested as any event in his ministry.
[^13]: **KONTEXT** — *Finger of God*: Luke deliberately uses this phrase where Matthew's parallel has "Spirit of God" (Matt 12:28), preserving the exact words of Exodus 8:19. During the plagues, Pharaoh's own magicians, unable to replicate the gnats, conceded: "This is the finger of God." By echoing that phrase, Jesus frames his exorcisms as a new exodus — the same power that broke Egypt's grip on Israel is now breaking demonic grip on this man. The allusion would have been unmistakable to anyone steeped in Torah.
[^14]: **TEXT** — *Has come upon you*: The Greek verb ἔφθασεν (ephthasen) means "has arrived" — not "is approaching" or "is near." Luke uses a different verb (ἤγγικεν, "has drawn near") elsewhere for the kingdom's proximity (10:9, 11). Here the claim is bolder: the kingdom is not on its way. It is here. In this room. In this act of liberation. The aorist tense marks a completed event, not an anticipated one. This is arguably the most audacious claim in the entire chapter.
[^15]: **KONTEXT** — *The strong man*: The parable uses military vocabulary throughout: πανοπλίαν (full armor — the word gives us "panoply"), σκῦλα (spoils of war), νικήσῃ (conquer). Jesus is describing a raid, not a robbery. In Luke's theology, every exorcism is a territorial operation — the stronger one (Jesus) entering the domain of the strong man (Satan) and freeing captives. Liberation theology reads this parable as paradigmatic: wherever oppressive power holds people captive, binding the strong man is kingdom work.
[^16]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *unclean spirit*: This passage presents a detailed cosmology of spiritual beings: they have agency, memory, social networks, preferences. They wander, return, recruit. For modern Western readers, this worldview is deeply foreign. In Jewish tradition, desert wastelands were the domain of demons (Isaiah 34:14; Tobit 8:3). Whether read as literal spiritual reality — the dominant view in the Global South, where exorcism remains a living practice — or as a powerful metaphor for how destructive patterns return with greater force when the underlying emptiness is not addressed, the warning is practical and psychologically acute: liberation without ongoing filling creates a dangerous vacuum.
[^17]: **LEBEN** — *The empty house*: The warning is direct: liberation without inhabitation is incomplete. The house is clean but unoccupied — freed from the old inhabitant but without a new one. In the context of Luke's pneumatology, what should fill the liberated person is the Holy Spirit — the same gift promised as the climax of the prayer teaching (v.13). Freedom without ongoing filling creates a vacuum worse than the original bondage. The Desert Fathers drew on this text for the monastic tradition of sustained spiritual practice after conversion.
[^18]: **TEXT** — *"Yes — and even more"*: The Greek particle μενοῦν (menoun) is critical for the passage's tone. It can mean "rather, on the contrary" (correction) or "yes indeed, and more than that" (affirmation followed by extension). Here the context supports the affirmation reading: Jesus does not reject the woman's blessing but receives it and widens it. Mary is doubly blessed — as the biological mother who bore Jesus (11:27) and as the first and model hearer-and-keeper of God's word (Luke 1:38: "Let it be to me according to your word"). The traditional "rather" risks making Jesus sound dismissive of a sincere and generous honor gift.
[^19]: **KONTEXT** — *Hear and keep*: "Those who hear the word of God and keep it" echoes the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4-9) — Israel's foundational call to attentive listening followed by faithful action. The verb φυλάσσω means to guard, protect, maintain — not passive receipt but active keeping, like guarding a treasure. The same pairing appears in Luke 8:21, where Jesus redefines family: "My mother and my brothers are those who hear the word of God and do it." The criterion of blessedness is the criterion of covenant faithfulness.
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *This crowd*: The Greek γενεὰ αὕτη ("this generation") refers specifically to Jesus' sign-demanding contemporaries — not to the Jewish people as a whole or to any ethnic group. The demonstrative "this" (αὕτη) is crucial: it identifies those present who are demanding cosmic proof rather than responding to what they have already witnessed. The prophetic critique is directed at a specific response (sign-demanding), not at an identity. This distinction matters because the phrase has been historically misused to characterize "the Jews" collectively.
[^21]: **KULTUR** — *The Queen of the South*: The queen of 1 Kings 10 — identified by Josephus as the queen of Ethiopia and Egypt — who crossed from Africa's southernmost reach to hear Solomon's wisdom. She is presented as a Gentile woman of enormous power and initiative who responded to wisdom with a journey spanning the known world. That a foreign queen will rise at the last judgment to condemn "the men of this generation" (note the gendered language) is a devastating honor-shame reversal: an outsider who sought wisdom will shame insiders who demand proof.
[^22]: **KULTUR** — *The men of Nineveh*: The Ninevites were Assyrians — the empire that destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel, one of ancient Israel's most feared enemies. That THEY repented at Jonah's five-word message of judgment, while Jesus' own contemporaries demanded more proof, is the most devastating comparison Jesus could invoke. The outsiders who had less access to revelation responded more faithfully than those with maximal access.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *Sound eye / evil eye*: In Jewish economic idiom, a "good eye" (ὀφθαλμὸς ἁπλοῦς) meant generosity and open-handedness — "the one with a generous eye will be blessed" (Proverbs 22:9). An "evil eye" (ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός) meant covetousness and stinginess — "do not let your eye be evil against your needy neighbor" (Deuteronomy 15:9); "an evil eye grudges bread" (Sirach 14:10). This is NOT about occult folk-belief or supernatural harm. It is a concrete ethical teaching: when your fundamental orientation is generous, your whole life is illuminated. When it is grasping, you are darkness itself.
[^24]: **KULTUR** — *Ritual handwashing*: The Greek ἐβαπτίσθη means "was immersed" — not a hygienic hand rinse but a ritual immersion removing the impurity of the marketplace before eating. The Pharisees had extended priestly washing requirements (Exodus 30:19-21) to all Jews as an expression of priestly holiness: every person at their table as a priest at the altar. Jesus' refusal was a deliberate, socially legible act — not rudeness but a prophetic statement about what actually makes a person clean. The Pharisee's shock (ἐθαύμασεν — "was astonished") signals the social gravity of the transgression.
[^25]: **KULTUR** — *Tithing herbs*: Torah required tithes of grain, wine, and oil (Deuteronomy 14:22-23). Whether small garden herbs counted as "agricultural produce" requiring tithing was debated among the Pharisees. Jesus explicitly affirms tithing ("these you should have practiced"). The critique is of prioritization — applying maximum precision to counting herb leaves while passing over justice and the love of God. The small and the large are not in competition; the problem is when the small becomes a substitute for the large.
[^26]: **KULTUR** — *Unmarked graves*: Contact with a corpse or grave caused the severest category of ritual impurity in the Levitical system (Numbers 19:16). Graves were whitewashed each spring before Passover to make them visible to approaching pilgrims. An unmarked grave was the worst-case scenario: you could walk over it and contract the highest level of defilement without knowing. Jesus' comparison is devastating: these religious leaders who appear honorable and clean actually spread the most severe contamination to everyone who comes near — and no one can see the danger.
[^27]: **TEXT** — *Experts in the law*: The Greek νομικός does not mean "lawyer" in the modern sense. These were Torah legal specialists — scholars who determined how the Mosaic law applied to specific daily situations. They controlled access to the interpretation of Scripture. The knowledge-as-power critique that follows (11:46-52) depends on understanding this role: these are the gatekeepers of religious understanding, the people who decide what the text means and what it requires.
[^28]: **KULTUR** — *This generation*: As in 11:29, the Greek γενεᾶς ταύτης ("this generation") refers specifically to the religious leaders Jesus is addressing — not to the Jewish people across time or as an ethnic category. This distinction is critical: "the blood of the prophets... required of this generation" has been historically misread as a permanent "blood curse" on all Jews, driving centuries of persecution. Luke's text is intra-Jewish prophetic critique — a Jewish prophet (Jesus) in the tradition of Amos, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel confronting specific leaders who repeat the pattern of killing prophets. Luke notably does NOT contain Matthew 27:25. The accountability is directed at a specific response pattern, not at an identity.
[^29]: **KONTEXT** — *Abel to Zechariah*: The span from Abel (Genesis 4) to Zechariah (2 Chronicles 24:20-22) covers the entire Hebrew Bible from first book to last — in the Hebrew canonical order, 2 Chronicles is the final book. Abel is the first murder victim in Scripture; Zechariah was killed "between the altar and the sanctuary" (his dying words: "May the LORD see and avenge!" — 2 Chronicles 24:22). Jesus invokes a complete prophetic accounting: every truth-teller murdered by the powerful, from the first page of Scripture to the last.
[^30]: **KULTUR** — *The key of knowledge*: The "key" (κλεῖδα) is access to understanding God's word — the interpretive key that unlocks Scripture for ordinary people. The lawyers' role was to help people understand what Torah meant and required. Instead, by making interpretation arcane, multiplying legal opinions that only specialists could navigate, and creating a knowledge economy in which they held all the power, they locked the door. The aorist "took away" (ἤρατε) indicates a completed action: the key has already been removed. The indictment is double: they don't enter themselves, and those trying to enter, they actively block.
[^31]: **KONTEXT** — *lying in wait*: The vocabulary turns predatory: ἀποστοματίζειν (cross-examine — a legal baiting technique, forcing someone to speak on demand), ἐνεδρεύοντες (lying in ambush), θηρεῦσαι (to hunt, to catch prey). The shift from dinner conversation to calculated entrapment marks a turning point. From this moment, the religious establishment is not merely offended — they are strategizing. Luke shows his readers the road to the cross: the same coalition that will demand Jesus' death is already assembling, and their method is surveillance and entrapment.

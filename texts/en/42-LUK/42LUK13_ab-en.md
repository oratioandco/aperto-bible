### Lament over Jerusalem


**31** At that very hour, some Pharisees came to him.*Not to challenge him — to warn him.*
[^32]
\f + \fr 13:31 \fk UNGLAUBLICH \fq Pharisees came to warn him \ft Pharisees — the same group elsewhere shown in opposition to Jesus — arrive to warn him about Herod. Modern readers can be forgiven for asking why these people, here, would do this.
On the believing reading the moment is exactly what it looks like: religious people in Galilee with no obvious motive doing a small dangerous act of care, and the one warned standing in such authority that he names a king \it that fox and continues toward Jerusalem on his own time. Christian tradition has read this scene as a glimpse of the freedom of the Son: not anxious, not defensive, anchored in a horizon longer than Herod's reach. \it „Today and tomorrow and the third day I finish my course" (V. 32) is one of the most theologically dense self-statements in Luke — Jesus refuses to be hurried by political threat and insists on his own timetable, three days, the same three days that will frame the resurrection. Christians have heard in this answer the disclosure of a vocation that knows where it is going and is not bargaining with the kings of this world. The Pharisees who warn him here become, in the believing reading, a quiet sign of what the church has always glimpsed in scripture: that the line between insider and outsider runs through every group, and grace recruits its messengers from anywhere.
For the reader who can't engage the scene as Christological self-disclosure, the warning still functions as the setup that makes Jesus' answer the centre. The Pharisees are messengers of a worldly clock; Jesus answers in the time of God. As portrait, the scene shows what vocation under pressure looks like — refusing the panic the warning intends to produce, and walking on.
And for the reader who can't take even that, historical-Jesus scholarship of the last forty years (the work of E. P. Sanders, James Dunn, Amy-Jill Levine, Paula Fredriksen) has worked hard to dismantle the older caricature of \it the Pharisees as Jesus' uniform enemies. The historical Pharisees were a diverse renewal movement; Jesus shared with them more than he disagreed about, and the Gospels themselves preserve sympathetic Pharisees (Luke 7,36; 11,37; 14,1; Acts 5,34). The textured picture — some warning, some opposing — fits what is otherwise known about first-century Judaism better than a flat hostile portrait would.
The text doesn't tell us what the Pharisees meant. It tells us what Jesus said back. How far you are willing to walk with the man who was not in a hurry is what the scene leaves open.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *Galileans whose blood Pilate mixed with their sacrifices*: No record of this specific atrocity survives in Josephus, but it is entirely consistent with Pilate's documented brutality — he had soldiers beat protestors, infiltrated crowds with concealed daggers, and provoked violence through deliberate provocation. That Roman soldiers killed Jewish pilgrims during the act of sacrifice, so that human blood mingled with sacrificial blood on the Temple floor, represents an ultimate desecration — violence in the holiest space, during the holiest act.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *Galileans whose blood Pilate mixed*: Luke's account of this atrocity, though not directly attested in Josephus, is confirmed as historically plausible by multiple converging lines of evidence. Josephus documents at least three separate instances of Pilate's extreme brutality: the aqueduct massacre where soldiers with concealed clubs beat and killed Jewish protestors (Antiquities 18.60-62), the incident of smuggling military standards into Jerusalem to provoke confrontation, and the massacre of Samaritans at Mount Gerizim (Antiquities 18.85-89) that eventually led to his removal from office. The detail that blood mixed with sacrificial blood places the event during a festival when non-priests brought their own animals — exactly when Pilate would have stationed troops in Jerusalem. Archaeological confirmation of the Pool of Siloam (mentioned in 13:4), excavated by Eli Shukron and Ronny Reich beginning in 2004, adds further credibility to Luke's knowledge of Jerusalem's topography.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *worse sinners*: The assumption Jesus names — disaster proves guilt — was the default theological framework of the ancient world. Job's friends articulated it: suffering means God is punishing. The crowd brings the news to Jesus as a test case, expecting a prophetic verdict. Jesus refuses the entire framework, twice.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *turn (μετανοέω)*: The Greek word metanoeō means literally "to change direction" — a turning of the whole person toward a new path, not primarily an expression of remorse. The traditional English "repent" imports a guilt-laden, backward-looking emotional register that obscures the forward-facing summons. Jesus is not demanding self-accusation but course correction.
[^5]: **KULTUR** — *perish in the same way*: Jesus is not threatening divine punishment. He is naming historical consequence — the political and spiritual trajectory his audience is on. Within forty years, the Roman legions would destroy Jerusalem and the Temple (70 CE). The "likewise" (\fq ὁμοίως\fq*) points to the manner of death — sudden, violent — not to the reasons. The same logic Jesus just refused (disaster = divine punishment) must not be reintroduced through his own words.
[^6]: **LEBEN** — *unless you turn*: The first instinct when tragedy strikes is to ask why — and the second is to find someone to blame. Jesus refuses both moves and redirects: instead of decoding the victims' guilt, examine the direction of the one still standing. This is not about guilt. It is about orientation. The Desert Fathers called this metanoia: not self-punishment but a turning of the whole self toward what matters, the kind of turn a body makes when it finally sees where it has been walking. When bad news arrives, the question is not "what did they do wrong?" but "what am I doing with the time I have?" — A question that lands without theological prelude: when did you last hear of someone else's catastrophe and feel a quiet, ugly relief — the relief of distance, the small voice that says "well, at least I didn't"? The instinct is universal and the verse names it directly. The pivot Jesus offers is not from relief to guilt but from distance to direction: the news of someone else's interrupted life is not material for moral commentary, it is an invitation to look at the angle of your own. — For readers inside a tradition the verse opens a long thread. Augustine in the Confessions narrates conversion as a long, halting turn rather than a single moment of regret; Ignatius of Loyola builds the Spiritual Exercises around the daily examen — a small turn made daily so that the large turn does not require catastrophe; the Wesleyan tradition calls it "continual repentance," which is less about feeling bad and more about staying oriented; the contemplative current from Thomas Merton through Cynthia Bourgeault treats metanoia as the heart's slow reorientation toward what is real. — Communally and civically: every public tragedy now arrives mediated, often within seconds. The verse is a small discipline against the reflex of commentary. Before opinion, ask: where am I walking? — This week: when the next piece of bad news arrives, sit with it for sixty seconds before forming a sentence about it. Let the time make the sentence different.
[^7]: **KULTUR** — *tower of Siloam*: The Tower of Siloam was located near the Pool of Siloam, south of Jerusalem — possibly part of an aqueduct construction project. Jesus deliberately pairs political violence (Pilate's massacre of worshippers) with a random construction accident (a tower collapsing on workers). The pairing dismantles any theodicy logic: if neither political atrocity nor random accident proves divine punishment, then the entire framework is broken. The Greek word opheiletai (rendered "more guilty") means literally "debtors" — people who owed a debt to God. Jesus refuses to classify disaster victims as people who "deserved" what happened.
[^8]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *disaster and divine punishment*: This passage directly addresses one of the oldest and most persistent theological claims: that natural disasters, accidents, and political violence are divine punishment for the victims' sins. Three perspectives. First, critical-historical: Jesus dismantles the retribution theology that dominated Second Temple Judaism (and that persists in many traditions). He pairs two utterly different events — political massacre and construction accident — and declares neither proves guilt. Second, theological: Jesus relocates responsibility from the victims to the audience. The question is not "why did they suffer?" but "what are you doing?" The redirect from theodicy to ethics is the passage's radical move. Third, contemporary: this logic resurfaces after every disaster — Hurricane Katrina blamed on New Orleans' immorality, earthquakes attributed to God's wrath against specific populations. Jesus refuses this reasoning as firmly as any modern ethicist, but from within the prophetic tradition.
[^9]: **KONTEXT** — *fig tree in his vineyard*: In the Hebrew prophets, both the fig tree and the vineyard are images for Israel's relationship with God. The fig tree appears in Jeremiah 8:13 ("I will take away their harvest... there will be no figs on the tree"), Hosea 9:10 ("Like finding grapes in the wilderness, I found Israel... like the first fruit on the fig tree"), and Micah 7:1. The vineyard is Isaiah 5:1-7's great Song of the Vineyard, where God plants a vineyard (Israel), cultivates it, and is devastated when it produces only wild grapes. A fig tree inside a vineyard is a double prophetic symbol: Israel's fruitfulness on trial.
[^10]: **TEXT** — *dig around it and spread manure (κόπρια)*: The Greek word kopria means animal dung — manure, not "fertilizer." The earthiness is the point. This is what mercy looks like in the parable: not clean or theological but agricultural labor. A gardener on his knees in the dirt, digging around roots, spreading dung. Cyril of Alexandria read the gardener as Christ himself, interceding before the Father. If so, divine grace comes in the form of the most humble, physical work — the smell of the soil, the patience of seasons.
[^11]: **LEBEN** — *leave it one more year*: The gardener does not argue theology with the vineyard owner. He does not debate whether the tree deserves another chance, does not catalog the tree's potential, does not appeal to abstract principles of mercy. He offers his own labor: let me dig, let me fertilize, let me do the work. Advocacy that changes things is rarely eloquent — it is patient, physical, unglamorous, often invisible. The gardener's posture — kneeling in the dirt, hands in manure, interceding through labor rather than speech — is a model of what it looks like to stay with someone who has not yet borne fruit. Not just waiting, but actively tending. For one more season. — A question that lands without theological prelude: when did you last advocate for someone, and what did the advocacy actually cost you? The honest answer often distinguishes between cheap advocacy — a post, a signature, a phrase at dinner — and the kind the gardener performs, which costs time, attention, dirt under the fingernails, and the willingness to look foolish if the tree does not produce. Both kinds matter, but the verse honors the second. — For readers inside a tradition the parable opens a long lineage of patient ministry. Benedictine stability — the vow to stay with this monastery, this brother, this sister, especially when it is hard — is the gardener's theology made architectural; the Catholic Worker movement under Dorothy Day refused triage logic and stayed with people other systems had given up on; contemporary recovery culture (twelve-step sponsorship, trauma-informed care) practices the same theology in different vocabulary: the work is the prayer. — Communally and vocationally: every classroom, every hospital ward, every parish has its barren fig trees, and every institution under stress is tempted to chop them down for efficiency. The gardener is a small subversion of that logic. — This week: identify one person in your life that everyone has subtly written off, including, possibly, you — and do one act of digging. A note, a meal, an hour. Not because it will work. Because the digging is the point.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *If it bears fruit next year—*: The parable ends without resolution. The gardener's speech breaks off mid-sentence: "If it bears fruit — well and good. But if not, then cut it down." The conditional hangs in the air. Ancient parable listeners expected this: the unfinished ending forces the listener to complete the story for themselves. The tree is neither saved nor cut. It hangs between the axe and another season. The invitation is open but not indefinite — the "until" marks a temporal limit.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *spirit of weakness (πνεῦμα ἀσθενείας)*: The Greek describes "a spirit causing weakness" — a causal description, not a moral verdict. The text assigns no guilt to this woman. Jesus requires no confession or repentance from her. The condition is something that happened to her, not something she caused.
[^14]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *spirit of weakness — harm history*: This phrase has been used to tell disabled and chronically ill people that their condition is evidence of demonic bondage or personal sin. The text explicitly does not support this reading: Jesus sees her, calls her, and declares her free without any moral prerequisite. No prayer of repentance. No exorcism ritual. No confession. The healing is initiated entirely by Jesus, and the cause of the condition is located entirely outside the woman's agency — "whom Satan bound" (v.16). For readers with chronic illness or disability: this passage is about liberation, not accusation.
[^15]: **TEXT** — *set free (ἀπολέλυσαι)*: The Greek verb apolyō means "to release, to set free, to liberate" — the same word used for releasing prisoners, manumitting slaves, sending someone away free. Jesus does not say "you are healed" (a medical word) but "you are set free" (a liberation word). The perfect passive tense marks the act as already accomplished — a declaration, not a promise. The Sabbath, grounded in Deuteronomy's version of the commandment (Deuteronomy 5:12-15) as a commemoration of Israel's liberation from Egypt, is precisely the right day for this liberation.
[^16]: **LEBEN** — *Jesus noticed her*: In a crowded room full of people listening to a teacher, Jesus saw the one person no one was looking at — a woman bent double, invisible for eighteen years. The text says he "noticed her" and then "called her forward." He did not wait for her to approach. He did not require her to ask. He did not test her faith first. The initiative was entirely his. There is something in this small sequence — see, call, touch — worth slowing down on, because being truly seen, not for productivity or usefulness but simply for being there, can be the beginning of liberation. Chronic invisibility is its own form of bondage. — A question that lands without theological prelude: who in your daily orbit are you not seeing? The list often surfaces with embarrassment: the cleaner whose name you have never asked; the colleague in the corner of meetings who has stopped speaking; the relative who has gone quiet for months; the person on the bench you walk past on the way to work. Eighteen years is a long time to be bent. People go invisible inside marriages, inside congregations, inside organizations, and the visibility deficit accumulates until even they have stopped expecting to be seen. The verse offers a small discipline: in any room, look for the one you are not looking at. — For readers inside a tradition the verse opens a wide stream. The desert fathers and mothers taught that the spiritual life begins with attention; Simone Weil wrote that attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity; Henri Nouwen's pastoral theology, particularly in Lifesigns and The Wounded Healer, makes seeing the wounded other the doorway to seeing God. The fourteenth-century anchoress Julian of Norwich grounds her whole theology in the conviction that God "beholds" us — and that this beholding is the condition of healing. — Communally and vocationally: in workplaces and parishes alike, the people who are systemically less visible are also the people most ground down by daily life. — This week: identify one person in your weekly orbit you tend to overlook, and call them forward. By name, by question, by a small public gesture of dignity.
[^17]: **KULTUR** — *six days for work — Sabbath healing controversy*: The synagogue leader's objection has genuine halakhic logic. Jewish Sabbath law distinguished between life-threatening conditions (which could be treated) and chronic conditions (which could wait). A woman who had been bent for eighteen years was, by this reasoning, not in immediate danger. Jesus does not dismiss the legal tradition — he wins the argument on its own terms, using a standard form of Jewish legal reasoning called qal vahomer (from lesser to greater): if you untie an animal on the Sabbath for water, how much more should a human being be freed from eighteen years of bondage? Note the synagogue leader's displacement: he addresses the crowd, not Jesus and not the woman. He cannot confront the authority directly, and he will not blame the blameless woman.
[^18]: **TEXT** — *daughter of Abraham*: This title — "daughter of Abraham" — appears nowhere else in the entire New Testament. "Sons of Abraham" was a standard covenant title for men, conferring full religious identity and belonging. By calling her "daughter of Abraham," Jesus formally includes women in the covenant naming — not as someone's wife or mother, but in her own person. This is not a generic compliment but a radical innovation: this woman, bent and invisible for eighteen years, is granted the same covenantal standing that "sons of Abraham" conveyed to men.
[^19]: **KULTUR** — *necessary that she be set free on the Sabbath*: The Greek word dei ("it was necessary") is Luke's signature term for divine compulsion — events that must happen because God wills them (Luke 2:49; 4:43; 9:22; 24:7). Jesus makes the Sabbath not the wrong day for healing but the theologically right day. The Sabbath, in the Deuteronomic tradition (Deuteronomy 5:15), commemorates Israel's liberation from slavery in Egypt. What better day to free a woman from eighteen years of bondage? The verbal echo is deliberate: the same root (lyō) links "untying" the ox in verse 15 to "setting free" (lythēnai) the woman in verse 16. You untie animals; you set free daughters of Abraham.
[^20]: **LEBEN** — *daughter of Abraham*: Jesus calls this woman "daughter of Abraham" — not someone's wife, not someone's mother, not a nameless recipient of charity, not a case study, not the bent woman from chapter thirteen. He gives her a title of full belonging, spoken publicly, in the place of worship, in front of the officials who were trying to use her body as a pretext for argument. The dignity is not earned by her faith or her behavior; it is declared over her by someone who sees her as she already is. In communities where belonging is conditional — on performance, appearance, productivity, conformity, doctrinal correctness, mental health, or marital status — this naming is a disruption. Sometimes the most transformative thing that can happen is to be told, in front of everyone, that you belong here. — A question that lands without theological prelude: where in your life have you accepted the diminutive name people gave you? "The shy one." "The difficult one." "The complicated one." "The one who never finished." These names have a way of becoming a small architecture inside the chest. The verse hands a different one over: daughter of Abraham. Son of Abraham. Heir of the promise. Not as flattery — as ontology. — For readers inside a tradition the verse opens a deep current. Paul's whole argument in Galatians is built on this kind of renaming — "you are all sons and daughters of Abraham through faith" — and the early baptismal liturgy named the new convert with a new name to make the renaming audible; the Catholic Worker tradition under Dorothy Day and Peter Maurin built houses of hospitality on the simple practice of calling the guest by full name and full dignity, in front of others; contemporary trauma-informed pastoral care (in writers like Serene Jones and Diane Langberg) makes the public declaration of belonging a key act of repair. — Communally: every parish, every workplace, every family system has people quietly using a smaller name than they were given. — This week: choose one person whom the room reduces — by joke, by role, by silence — and name them at their full size, audibly, where others can hear.
[^21]: **TEXT** — *opponents put to shame (κατῃσχύνοντο)*: The Greek katēischynonto means "were put to shame" — a formal honor-shame reversal. The synagogue leader attempted to control the space and the interpretation; he ends shamed. The crowd's simultaneous rejoicing creates a split screen: institutional authority deflated, popular joy released. Two utterly different experiences of the same moment.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *mustard seed*: The mustard seed was proverbially the smallest seed known. The plant — an annual shrub, sometimes reaching three meters — was notable for its disproportionate growth, though hardly a noble tree. The theological subversion is deliberate. In Ezekiel 17:23 and Daniel 4:12, empires are described as great world-trees — towering cedars whose branches shelter all nations. Jesus replaces the cedar with a mustard plant: a common, fast-growing garden weed. The kingdom of God is not an empire. It does not look like what the powerful expect. The scholar John Dominic Crossan called this a "burlesque of the imperial tree" — the kingdom shelters, but in an entirely unglamorous way.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *leaven (ζύμη)*: The Greek word zymē refers to ancient sourdough starter — a piece of fermented dough saved from a previous batch and worked into new flour. The lexicon BDAG explicitly cautions: "the rendering 'yeast' popularly suggests a product foreign to ancient baking practice." The woman hid (ekrypsen) the leaven inside the flour — the Greek verb means "concealed, embedded." The kingdom works by hiddenness, not proclamation. It transforms from within, invisibly. Three measures (sata tria) equals approximately 36-40 liters of flour — enough bread for roughly a hundred people. The same quantity appears in Genesis 18:6, when Sarah bakes for the three divine visitors at the oaks of Mamre. The extraordinary abundance is part of the image.
[^24]: **KONTEXT** — *paired parables — man/woman*: Luke deliberately pairs a male protagonist (the man planting, v.19) with a female protagonist (the woman baking, v.21). This is a Lukan signature: the shepherd who finds the lost sheep is paired with the woman who finds the lost coin (15:3-10); the persistent widow matches the unjust judge (18:1-8). The kingdom happens in both the garden and the kitchen, through both men's and women's ordinary labor.
[^25]: **TEXT** — *strive (ἀγωνίζεσθε)*: The Greek word agōnizesthe derives from agōn — an athletic contest. The lexicon BDAG glosses this verse specifically: "strain every nerve to enter." This is not casual encouragement ("try your best") but full-body, concentrated, sustained effort. The image is physical: something between a wrestler and a runner — not violence, but intensity.
[^26]: **TEXT** — *narrow door (στενή θύρα)*: Luke uses the word for a house door (thyra), not the word for a city gate (pylē). This is critical. Matthew's parallel (7:13-14) uses "gate" (pylē) — a gate is about difficulty, a door is about timing. Luke's image is of a banquet door in a house: it can be opened, it can be closed from within, and once the host rises and shuts it, latecomers are excluded. The urgency is not "how hard is it to get in?" but "how long will it be open?"
[^27]: **KULTUR** — *I don't know where you come from*: In Mediterranean culture, denying knowledge of a person's origin was a formal act of social repudiation — the equivalent of saying "we have no relationship." This is not casual rudeness but a complete severing of social ties. The phrase is repeated twice (vv.25, 27), emphasizing its finality. The latecomers' appeal — "We ate and drank in your presence! You taught in our streets!" — invokes table fellowship, which in the ancient Mediterranean world created quasi-legal bonds of mutual obligation. Sharing a meal was the strongest social bond short of kinship. Their appeal has genuine weight. And it fails. Proximity is not the same as relationship.
[^28]: **KONTEXT** — *workers of injustice*: This phrase near-quotes Psalm 6:8 (LXX 6:9): "Depart from me, all you workers of iniquity." In its original context, the psalmist drives away persecutors; here the householder drives away those who claim relationship but practiced injustice. The echo connects the parable to the larger biblical tradition of God's judgment distinguishing between those who claim closeness and those who practice righteousness.
[^29]: **TEXT** — *weeping and grinding of teeth (κλαυθμὸς καὶ βρυγμός)*: This phrase appears seven times in the New Testament as a fixed expression for the anguish of exclusion. It is attested in Greek literature from the fifth century BCE (Eupolis). It describes two simultaneous emotional states: intense grief (weeping) and bitter frustration (gnashing/grinding). It is not a description of physical torment in a torture chamber. It is the emotional reality of being shut out — watching the feast through a closed door, seeing the patriarchs inside while you stand in the dark. The anguish is self-knowledge: you had the chance, and you missed it.
[^30]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *the narrow door and exclusion*: This passage has been weaponized across centuries as a claim that God permanently rejected the Jewish people in favor of Gentile Christians — a reading that is exegetically unjustifiable. Jesus speaks here as a Jewish prophet addressing a Jewish audience about the danger of presumption, not about ethnic replacement. The scholar Amy-Jill Levine notes that the text deliberately resists clean identification of "the excluded" with any single group. The people arriving from east, west, north, and south (v.29) are not identified as Gentiles — they may well include diaspora Jews returning from exile, fulfilling Isaiah 49:12. The passage warns against assuming that proximity (to Jesus, to the tradition, to the community) guarantees belonging. This is a critique of presumption, not of a people.
[^31]: **KULTUR** — *recline at the table (ἀνακλιθήσονται)*: Reclining was the bodily posture of honored guests at a formal feast. To recline at a table was to be given the position of honor — welcomed, served, celebrated. The verb is not merely "eat" but "take the honored position at the banquet." The outsiders from all four compass points — arriving from everywhere — are not seated at the back. They are given the seats of honor. The four-direction formula echoes Isaiah 49:12 and Psalm 107:3 — a gathering from the whole world.
[^32]: **KULTUR** — *some Pharisees came to warn him*: Luke's portrait of the Pharisees is more complex than many readers expect. In this scene, Pharisees come not as antagonists but as concerned protectors warning Jesus about a genuine death threat. Luke records Pharisees who invite Jesus to dinner (7:36; 11:37; 14:1), Pharisees who debate him respectfully (13:31), and Pharisees who later become believers (Acts 15:5; 23:6-9). Reducing Pharisees to cardboard villains flattens the text and feeds the anti-Jewish reading that has caused immense historical harm.
[^33]: **TEXT** — *that fox (τῇ ἀλώπεκι ταύτῃ)*: In Jewish tradition, the fox — in contrast to the lion — represents cunning but lesser, scavenging power. This is not a backhanded compliment. It is a dismissive insult: Jesus is calling Herod Antipas a minor, unprincipled schemer who cannot stop what God has set in motion. The demonstrative "that" (\fq ταύτῃ\fq*) adds contempt.
[^34]: **TEXT** — *on the third day my work will be finished (τελειοῦμαι)*: "Today and tomorrow and the third day" is a Semitic idiom meaning "in a short while" — not a literal three-day count and not a veiled resurrection prediction. The verb teleioumai means "I am brought to completion" — the same word used for finishing a course (Acts 20:24) or completing a task (Luke 2:43). Jesus is saying: my mission continues on its appointed course for a short while yet, and then it will be complete. The BDAG lexicon confirms this is idiomatic temporal language.
[^35]: **TEXT** — *a hen (ὄρνις)*: The Greek word ornis here means specifically a domestic hen — not a generic bird or an eagle. Jesus applies to himself the divine image of gathering under wings (Psalm 91:4; Deuteronomy 32:11; Psalm 17:8; 36:7; 57:1; 61:4; 63:7) using a specifically maternal, domestic, vulnerable image. The hen does not overpower the threat to her chicks. She covers them with her own body. This is one of the most striking images in the Gospels: Jesus claiming a divine prerogative through a feminine, self-sacrificing image.
[^36]: **TEXT** — *you were not willing (οὐκ ἠθελήσατε)*: The Greek word thelō means "to will, to want, to be willing." Jerusalem's refusal was a refusal of will, not an inability. This distinction carries enormous theological weight: divine love extended; human agency declined. The Hebrew Bible is full of this pattern — divine invitation met by human refusal (Isaiah 65:2; Hosea 11:1-7). The tragedy is not that Jerusalem could not respond but that it chose not to.
[^37]: **LEBEN** — *the way a hen gathers her chicks*: Jesus reaches for the most vulnerable image available: not an eagle, not a lion, not a warrior — a hen. The hen does not overpower the threat; she covers her chicks with her own body. This is one of the most striking moments in the Gospels: divine protection reimagined as maternal vulnerability, shelter offered through self-exposure rather than counterforce. The image says something about how love actually works — not by defeating the threat from a distance, but by placing oneself between the danger and the ones being protected. And then the devastating final line: "you were not willing." Love that gathers can be refused. Protection that is offered can be declined. The wings stay open. — A question that lands without theological prelude: where in your life have you mistaken protection for performance? The cultural script for safety often runs through power — better locks, better income, better networks, a louder presence. The verse offers a counter-script. The protection Jesus extends comes through proximity and exposure, not dominance. It is the protection of the parent who walks the child home through the dark, of the friend who answers the call at 3am, of the colleague who stands beside the person being scapegoated in the meeting. — For readers inside a tradition the image opens a current the Church has not always known how to receive. Julian of Norwich calls Jesus "our true Mother," not as decoration but as theological substance; the Cistercian Bernard of Clairvaux wrote of Christ's maternal tenderness; the contemporary feminist theology of Elizabeth Johnson and Sarah Coakley recovers this strand against centuries of single-gendered divine imagery. The image is not new — it is biblical and ancient. — Communally and politically: an entire alternative ethics flows from this verse. The protective parent rather than the dominating warrior. The body between the threatened and the threat. — And: "you were not willing." The verse names the heartbreak of love that cannot force itself. It does not give up. The wings remain open. — This week: name one person in your circle who is exposed, and consider what proximity, not performance, would look like. Then move one step closer.
[^38]: **TEXT** — *your house is left to you desolate*: The language echoes Jeremiah 12:7 ("I have forsaken my house, I have abandoned my heritage") and Jeremiah 22:5. This is the vocabulary of divine withdrawal — God's presence departing — not of God's fist destroying. The "house" is the Temple, or at most the city of Jerusalem addressed in its leadership role. This is prophetic lament, not triumphant condemnation: a prophet grieving over what his own people will suffer.
[^39]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *"your house is left desolate" — harm history*: This verse has been used across centuries to claim God's permanent rejection of the Jewish people — one of the most consequential misreadings in the history of biblical interpretation. This reading is exegetically unjustifiable for three reasons. First: Jesus speaks as a prophet from within the Jewish tradition, addressing Jerusalem's leadership in a specific historical moment, not all Jewish people at all times. Second: the "until" clause (\fq ἕως\fq*) explicitly preserves hope — the desolation is provisional, not permanent, conditioned on a future recognition. Third: the quotation "Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord" is Psalm 118:26, sung at Passover, and the crowd will sing these exact words at the Triumphal Entry (19:38). The final word of the chapter is not condemnation but expectation.
[^40]: **KONTEXT** — *Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord*: Psalm 118:26 — a processional psalm sung during pilgrimage festivals, especially at Passover. The crowd will shout these exact words when Jesus enters Jerusalem (Luke 19:38). The word "until" (heōs) is the hinge between lament and hope: the desolation does not end in permanent rejection but in the expectation that the day of recognition is still coming. The last word of the chapter is not grief but an open door.

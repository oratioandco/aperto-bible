## Kapitel 14


### A Sabbath Healing


**1** One Sabbath, Jesus was invited to eat at the home of a prominent Pharisee — one of their leading men. *But the eyes around that table were not hospitable.* They were watching him. Closely.
[^1]
**2** A man was there, right in front of him — *his body swollen with fluid that had nowhere to go.* Dropsy. *The kind of condition that marked you, that people whispered about.*
[^2]

**3** Jesus looked at the legal experts and the Pharisees. *"Tell me,"* he said. "Is it lawful to heal someone on the Sabbath — or isn't it?"
[^3]


**4** He reached out, took hold of the man, and healed him. Then he let him go.
[^4]

**5** He turned back to them. "Suppose your own child fell into a well on the Sabbath. Or your ox. Would you leave them there?"
[^5]

**6** They had nothing to say.

### On Humility and True Hospitality


**7** He had been watching them.*The way the guests jockeyed for the best positions at the table — each one trying to claim the seat closest to their host.* He noticed.
[^6]
[^7]


**8** "When you're invited to a wedding feast, don't head straight for the place of honor. Someone more important may have been invited too.
**9** And then the host comes over and says, 'Give your seat to this person' — and there you are, making the long walk to the back of the room.*In front of everyone.*"

**10** "But if you take the lowest seat? The host will come to you and say: 'Friend — come up higher.' And you will have honor before every person at that table."

**11** "Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled. And everyone who humbles himself will be exalted."
[^8]
[^9]

**12** He turned to the host.


**13** "Instead, when you give a feast, invite the destitute. The maimed. The lame. The blind.
[^10]
[^11]
**14** They cannot repay you.*And that is the point.* You will be repaid — at the resurrection of the righteous."
[^12]

### The Parable of the Great Banquet


**15** One of the guests exclaimed: "What a blessing it will be to eat bread in the kingdom of God!"
[^13]


**16** "A man was giving a great feast. He had invited many guests — *and they had accepted.* When everything was ready, *the food prepared, the house arranged,*
**17** he sent his servant with the message: 'Come now. It's time.'
[^14]

**18** "But they refused. Every one of them. All at once.

**19** *The second:* 'I've just bought five yoke of oxen — *ten animals —* and I need to test them. I'm sorry.'
**20** *The third didn't even bother with the polite formula. He simply said:* 'I just got married. I can't come.'

**21** "*The servant came back with this news.* The master of the house was furious*— the food prepared, the house set, every one of his guests refusing at the last hour.* 'Go out immediately,' he told the servant. 'Into the streets. Into the alleys. Bring in the destitute, the maimed, the blind, the lame.'

**22** "*The servant went and returned:* 'It's done, sir. But there is still room.'

**23** "'Then go further,' the master said. 'Out to the highways. The hedgerows. Urge them to come in — insist — until my house is full.'"
[^15]

**24** *He looked around the room.*

[^16]

### The Cost of Following


**25** Large crowds were traveling with him now. He stopped. He turned around to face them*— all of them, the curious and the committed and those who had not yet decided.*

**26** "If anyone comes to me," he said, "and does not hate his father and mother — his wife — his children — his brothers and sisters — and yes, even his own life — that person cannot be my disciple."
[^17]
[^18]

**27** "Whoever does not carry their own cross and follow me cannot be my disciple."
[^19]


**28** "Which of you, wanting to build a tower, wouldn't sit down first and calculate whether you have enough to finish?
**29** Otherwise — you lay the foundation, the money runs out,
**30** and everyone who passes by laughs: 'This man started building and couldn't finish.'

**31** "Or what king, marching out to war against another king, wouldn't sit down first and ask: can my ten thousand stand against his twenty thousand?
**32** And if the answer is no — he sends a delegation while the enemy is still a long way off, and sues for peace."

**33** *He looked at them.*

[^20]
[^21]

### Salt


**34** "Salt is good. But if salt loses its saltiness — what then? How will you season it? Not for the soil. Not even for the manure pile.
[^22]
[^23]
**35** They throw it out."

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *watching him*: The Greek word \fq παρατηρούμενοι\fq* is surveillance vocabulary — the same root Luke uses in 20:20 for those who "spy" on Jesus. This was not a casual social meal. Sabbath meals at the home of a leading Pharisee were formal occasions with social hierarchy, honor-competition, and public scrutiny. The invitation was an honor — but the watching was hostile. Jesus was being tested.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *dropsy*: The Greek \fq ὑδρωπικός\fq* refers to what was anciently called "dropsy" — severe fluid retention causing visible swelling throughout the body. In the ancient world, this condition carried social stigma beyond the physical suffering. Folk tradition associated it with liver disease, excessive drinking, or divine disfavor. The man was not just ill — he was marked. His presence at a Pharisee's formal meal is unexplained by Luke, which has led some scholars to suggest he was placed there deliberately as a test for Jesus.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *lawful to heal on the Sabbath*: This was a live legal debate within Judaism, not a conflict between Christianity and Judaism. The Damascus Document (a Dead Sea Scrolls text) forbade pulling an animal out of a pit on the Sabbath; the Mishnah (Shabbat 128b) later permitted it in some cases. Jesus' question enters this intra-Jewish conversation on the side of compassion — and his opponents' silence (v. 4) suggests they knew his argument had legal force. The text does not portray Pharisees as heartless; it shows them unable to counter a valid argument.
[^4]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *healed him*: Jesus heals in a hostile room under surveillance — no fanfare, no prayer recorded, just physical contact and release. Three frames. Critical-historical: the healing narrative serves Luke's literary and theological purpose — demonstrating Jesus' authority over the Sabbath debate. Whether the event occurred as described or represents a theological claim framed as narrative is a question the text itself does not settle. Theological-symbolic: the healing enacts what the Sabbath was meant to be — God's restorative work. Jesus does not violate the Sabbath; he fulfills it. The man's release (\fq ἀπέλυσεν\fq*, "he let him go") echoes liberation language throughout Luke. Historical-believing: the healing is an act of divine compassion that exposes the gap between religious debate and human need. The physical contact (\fq ἐπιλαβόμενος\fq*, "taking hold of") in a setting where the man was likely untouched by those around him is itself a statement about the nature of God's kingdom.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *your own child — or your ox*: The earliest manuscripts (NA28 preferred reading) have "son or ox" (\fq υἱὸς ἢ βοῦς\fq*), creating a deliberate escalation from the most precious to the less. Some manuscripts read "donkey or ox" (\fq ὄνος ἢ βοῦς\fq*), harmonizing with Luke 13:15. The "son or ox" reading is stronger — it makes the argument irresistible. This is a standard form of rabbinic reasoning called qal vahomer (from the lighter to the heavier): if you would rescue even an animal on the Sabbath, how much more a human being?
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *the best positions at the table*: Ancient formal meals were eaten reclining on couches arranged in a triclinium — a U-shaped layout with the host at the center. Position mattered: the closer you reclined to the host, the higher your social standing. Everyone in the room could see exactly where everyone was placed. This was not subtle — it was a public, visible ranking system. The Greek \fq πρωτοκλισίας\fq* literally means "first reclining-place." The guests' competition for these positions was an open game of honor-seeking that Jesus turns into a teaching moment.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *take the lowest seat*: Jesus' teaching echoes Proverbs 25:6-7: "Do not put yourself forward in the king's presence or stand in the place of the great, for it is better to be told 'Come up here' than to be put lower in the presence of a noble." The wisdom tradition already knew this. But Jesus transforms social advice into eschatological promise: the divine passives in verse 11 ("will be humbled… will be exalted") signal that God himself reverses the world's rankings. What Proverbs frames as courtly wisdom, Jesus frames as the architecture of the kingdom. The Rule of St. Benedict (6th century) built its entire chapter on humility — the most developed treatment of humility in Western monasticism — on this passage.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *will be humbled... will be exalted*: The Greek uses what scholars call the "divine passive" (passivum divinum) — a grammatical construction that avoids naming God directly but implies God as the agent. "Will be humbled" means "God will humble"; "will be exalted" means "God will exalt." This shifts the saying from social advice to eschatological promise: God himself reverses the world's rankings. This logion appears in four different contexts in the Gospels (Luke 14:11; 18:14; Matthew 23:12; cf. Luke 1:52), signaling its centrality to Jesus' teaching.
[^9]: **LEBEN** — *humbles himself*: The two table-parables before this verse make the point clear: Jesus is not teaching humility as social strategy — "sit in the back so you'll be promoted." That would just be ambition wearing different clothes. The text is about something harder: releasing the ego's grip on rank altogether, trusting that honor is God's to give and not yours to engineer. In a culture that measures worth by visibility, influence, and reach, this is among the most countercultural claims in the Gospels. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where do you spend energy positioning yourself? The list is usually concrete — the meeting where you wait for a strategic moment to speak, the social media post calibrated for response, the conversation steered toward your accomplishments, the small comparisons that flicker through any group photo. None of it is monstrous. Most of it is invisible even to the person doing it. The verse simply asks you to notice. — A low-threshold practice this week: in one meeting, deliberately not be the first or the most articulate voice. Let someone else carry the moment. Watch what happens inside — the urge to clarify, the impulse to correct, the small ache of unrecognized contribution. That ache is the muscle this verse is trying to retrain. — For readers inside the Christian tradition, the lineage is deep. Benedict's Rule (6th century) cites this verse as the foundation for its entire chapter on humility — twelve steps that descend slowly from self-promotion to self-forgetfulness. Thomas Merton diagnosed the same illness in modern dress: the "false self" that needs to be applauded to know it exists. Jean Vanier at L'Arche described daily life with people who could not be impressed as a slow-motion school of this verse. — Vocationally, the verse is also a labor question. Whole industries — consulting, academia, the arts, ministry itself — run on visibility as currency. Trustworthy work often happens beneath that line, where no audience sees it. — This week: do one good thing, deliberately, that no one will know about.
[^10]: **KONTEXT** — *the destitute, the maimed, the lame, the blind*: These four groups appear almost identically in the Qumran community's messianic banquet rules (1QSa 2:5-9), where they are explicitly excluded: "No person smitten in flesh, paralyzed in feet or hands, lame, blind, deaf, dumb or smitten in flesh with a blemish visible to the eyes... shall enter to take a place in the midst of the community." Jesus' guest list is the precise inversion of this exclusion list. Where the holiest community of his day drew a line, Jesus erases it. The kingdom's banquet is defined not by who is kept out but by who is deliberately brought in.
[^11]: **LEBEN** — *stop inviting your friends*: Most hospitality operates on an unspoken ledger: I host you, you host me. Jesus names the transaction and asks for something harder. Generosity that cannot be repaid has no social function — it builds no network, earns no favor, creates no debt. That is precisely what makes it radical: it imitates a God who gives without keeping score. The verse does not condemn friendship. It interrogates the invisible math behind it. — A question that works without theological warmup: who is absent from your table — not because you refused them, but because they were never in the room when you made the guest list? The honest answer often points to people who feel like effort: the elderly neighbor who repeats stories, the colleague no one invites because she's awkward, the unhoused man you pass on the way to the grocery store, the cousin who got divorced and dropped out of family events. None of this is dramatic. It is the structure of ordinary life. — A low-threshold practice this week: the next time you plan a meal, a coffee, a small gathering — leave one seat for someone outside the reciprocal loop. Not as charity, not as project. As a real invitation to a real table. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is deep. Benedict's Rule demands that every guest be received as Christ, without first checking their usefulness. Francis of Assisi ate with lepers when his city was still celebrating his rising status. Dorothy Day's Houses of Hospitality in 1930s New York were built around exactly this verse — a table where the question "what will this cost me, and what will I get back?" was deliberately disabled. — Civically, the verse reads as a quiet protest against networking as a way of life. Most middle-class sociability runs on tacit exchange. Jesus is not telling you to dismantle your friendships — he is telling you that something has to happen at your table that does not fit the spreadsheet. — Perhaps this is where spiritual maturity begins: the day you invite someone who will never invite you back and notice that something in you grows freer, not poorer.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *the resurrection of the righteous*: This phrase reflects a specific strand of Second Temple Jewish belief: that the righteous would be raised first in God's final vindication (see Daniel 12:2; 2 Maccabees 7:9, 14). Luke uses it to ground an ethical demand in eschatological hope — you can afford to be generous without return because the ultimate accounting belongs to God. The promise is not that generosity will be repaid in this life but that God's justice operates on a longer timeline than human reciprocity.
[^13]: **KONTEXT** — *eat bread in the kingdom of God*: The guest is drawing on the Jewish tradition of the eschatological banquet — the great feast God would prepare for all peoples at the end of time (Isaiah 25:6-8): "The LORD of hosts will make for all peoples a feast of rich food, a feast of well-aged wines." The Qumran community developed this into the messianic banquet (1QSa 2:11-22). The guest's exclamation is comfortable piety — easy to say over a good meal. Jesus' parable is about to show what actually happens when that invitation arrives.
[^14]: **KULTUR** — *Come now. It's time.*: Ancient Middle Eastern hospitality operated on a two-stage invitation system. The first stage was the advance invitation — a social commitment that the invitee accepted. The second stage was the day-of summons: "Come, everything is ready." Between the two stages, the host invested heavily — purchasing perishable food, preparing the house, arranging entertainment. To refuse at this second stage was not merely inconvenient; it was a devastating public insult. The host's investment was wasted, and his honor was destroyed in front of the community. All three excuse-givers (vv. 18-20) refuse at this second stage — after already having accepted.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *urge them to come in — insist*: The Greek word \fq ἀνάγκασον\fq* means "urge strongly, press, compel." In context, the servant is addressing people who live on the roads and hedgerows — the absolute margins of society. These are people who would feel profoundly unworthy of entering a wealthy person's formal feast. The servant's insistence is persistent persuasion needed to overcome their shame-based reluctance, not physical force. This must be stated clearly because Augustine of Hippo (by 408 AD) weaponized this verse to justify state coercion of religious dissidents — his doctrine of "coercive love." Augustine's reading powered centuries of forced conversion: Crusade preaching, the Spanish Inquisition's forced conversions, and colonial missions' forced baptisms of indigenous peoples. The Greek text, the social context, and the parabolic frame all resist this coercive reading. Augustine was wrong, and the consequences were catastrophic.
[^16]: **HARM** — *not one of those who were invited*: This verse has been catastrophically misread as an allegory: the original invitees = "the Jews" who rejected Jesus; the replacement guests = Gentiles who accepted. This mapping is exegetically unjustified — the text identifies the excusers as wealthy landowners with economic distractions, not as a religious or ethnic group. It is also historically destructive: this allegorical reading has powered centuries of anti-Jewish theology and violence. The parable critiques social complacency and economic self-satisfaction, not Jewish faith. Note the shift in address: "I tell you" (\fq ὑμῖν\fq*, plural) signals Jesus stepping out of the parable's fictional frame to address his actual audience — the Pharisees at the table. The parable's host speaks to one servant; Jesus speaks to the room. "My feast" (\fq μου τοῦ δείπνου\fq*) makes the claim personal and unmistakable.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *does not hate*: The Greek \fq μισεῖ\fq* ("hate") uses a Semitic comparative idiom that does not mean emotional revulsion. In Hebrew (\fq שָׂנֵא\fq*, Deuteronomy 21:15-17), this word means "hold in disfavor relative to another." Matthew 10:37 gives the interpretive key: "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me." Luke preserves the harder, more Semitic form; Matthew translates the idiom into Greek comparative language. Both mean the same thing: ultimate allegiance to Jesus must take priority over every other loyalty, including family. The Patristic tradition consistently avoided the literal reading — Origen noted that one cannot "hate" one's own life (\fq ψυχήν\fq*) literally without advocating self-destruction, so the meaning must be comparative. This verse has been dangerously misused by high-control religious groups to justify cutting off family members who question or leave the group. That reading inverts the text: Jesus elsewhere cares for others' families (healing Peter's mother-in-law, raising the widow's son). The command is about priority, not destruction of relationships.
[^18]: **LEBEN** — *cannot be my disciple*: Three times in nine verses, Jesus says "cannot be my disciple" (14:26, 27, 33). The repetition is deliberate — this is not casual teaching but a winnowing. The demand is not for dramatic martyrdom but for a settled ordering of loyalties: God first, then everything else in light of that. "Hate" in Semitic idiom does not mean emotional revulsion; it means rank order. When the relativizing call of God comes, every other allegiance — even the most precious — has to move into second position or it stops being love and becomes idolatry. — A question that lands without theological warmup: what would you not be willing to risk, even for something you know is true? The list is usually specific — a family system that punishes deviation, a friendship that cannot tolerate disagreement, a career trajectory that depends on never raising certain questions, a national or political identity that demands silence on uncomfortable matters. The verse is not asking you to throw any of these away. It is asking which of them you have placed above the call to follow honestly. — A small practice this week: name one place in your life where you regularly soften the truth to keep the peace. Not to launch into a crusade — just to notice the muscle. The cross Jesus speaks of was not a metaphor for his audience; it was the most terrifying image in their world. For modern listeners, the equivalent is whatever commitment costs enough to make you hesitate. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is sober. Bonhoeffer wrote The Cost of Discipleship while watching his church bow to a regime — "when Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die." The desert mothers and fathers left honor and family not because they hated either but because both had become substitutes for God. Dorothy Day quoted this verse when explaining why she could not, in conscience, raise her daughter inside the assumptions of American respectability. — The text does not romanticize the cost. It asks you to count it honestly — and then, in spite of the count, to choose.
[^19]: **KULTUR** — *carry their own cross*: In the first century, the cross was not a religious symbol — it was the Roman Empire's instrument of state terror, reserved for political rebels, runaway slaves, and those who challenged imperial authority. Crucifixion was designed to be maximally public and maximally humiliating: the condemned person was often forced to carry the horizontal crossbeam (patibulum, weighing 75-125 pounds) through the streets to the execution site. Anyone living in first-century Palestine would have seen this procession. When Jesus says "carry your cross and follow me," his audience did not hear a metaphor — they heard the most terrifying image in their world.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *give up everything he has*: The Greek \fq ἀποτάσσεται\fq* has a range of meaning from "renounce, give up" to "bid farewell to, take leave of" (BDAG). The two parables that precede this demand (tower-builder, war-king) are about honest assessment, not reckless action. Luke's own narrative shows Jesus' circle including people with property used for the mission (Mary Magdalene, Joanna in 8:1-3; Zacchaeus after his repentance). The demand is for a disposition of total availability — nothing held back in principle — rather than necessarily literal divestiture. Some movements have used this verse to require surrender of all possessions as a condition of membership, sometimes leading to financial exploitation. The rhetorical context argues against this: the parables teach deliberate commitment, not impulsive divestiture.
[^21]: **LEBEN** — *give up everything*: The two parables before this verse are about counting the cost — not about abandoning reason. A builder calculates; a king assesses his forces. The application is not "throw everything away impulsively" but "hold nothing back in principle." The difference matters. Renunciation as disposition means that nothing you own owns you — your security, your identity, your plans remain available to God rather than locked away from the conversation. — A question that lands without theological warmup: what would you not be able to lose without losing yourself? The list is often surprising. For some it is money or a house. For more people it is reputation, a particular relationship, a self-image as competent or independent or good. The verse does not demand poverty as a condition of discipleship; it demands that no possession — material, emotional, professional — become the reason you cannot follow. — A low-threshold practice this week: pick one thing you assume you could not live without and ask honestly whether that is true. Not to throw it away — just to loosen the grip. Often the simple act of admitting that something is not load-bearing changes how you carry it. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is rich. Ignatius's First Principle and Foundation calls it "indifference" — not coldness, but interior freedom: holding all things in such a way that you could let them go if the call became clear. The Cappadocian fathers wrote about "detachment" not as escape from the world but as the precondition for loving it without devouring it. Francis of Assisi gave away his clothes in the public square so that what he owned would no longer own him. — Vocationally and relationally, the test is concrete. The person who cannot leave a job they hate because it defines them; the parent whose identity is fused with their child's success; the artist who cannot make a piece that risks losing their audience — each is being asked the same question this verse asks. — The point is not loss. The point is freedom — the kind that only comes when nothing is locked away from the call.
[^22]: **KULTUR** — *if salt loses its saltiness*: Unlike modern refined salt (pure sodium chloride), Palestinian salt was mined from the Dead Sea region and was mixed with gypsum and other minerals. Under certain conditions — particularly exposure to moisture — the sodium chloride could leach out, leaving a mineral residue that looked like salt but had no flavor. Archaeological chemistry confirms this was a real phenomenon, not a hypothetical impossibility (E. P. Deatrick, Biblical Archaeologist 25, 1962). The residue was useless even as soil amendment — "neither for the soil nor for the manure pile" describes absolute uselessness in agricultural terms.
[^23]: **TEXT** — *loses its saltiness*: The Greek \fq μωρανθῇ\fq* carries a double meaning that English cannot capture: it means both "to become tasteless/insipid" (its primary everyday sense) and "to become foolish" (its primary New Testament usage — see Romans 1:22; 1 Corinthians 1:20). The wordplay connects foolish discipleship to tasteless salt: a follower who does not count the cost (14:28-33) is both foolish and flavorless — useless for the kingdom's purposes.

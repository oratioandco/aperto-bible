## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *tax collectors and sinners*: "Tax collectors and sinners" was a fixed social category in Second Temple Judaism — not a general moral description but a specific label for people who had placed themselves outside the boundaries of covenant observance. Tax collectors (τελῶναι) collected local tolls and customs duties on behalf of Roman-authorized contracts; they were despised as both cheats and collaborators with the occupying empire. "Sinners" (ἁμαρτωλοί) was the broader category for those who failed to observe Torah in ways that marked covenant membership: tithing, purity, Sabbath. Together, these two groups were the socially defined "lost" — the people the three parables are about to address.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *eats with them*: In the ancient Jewish world, eating with someone was not a casual social gesture. It was a covenant declaration. Sharing a table meant vouching for the other person's standing — their purity, their membership in the community, their acceptability before God. The Pharisees' charge against Jesus is precise: he doesn't just teach sinners or tolerate their presence; he sits down and eats with them (συνεσθίει — "eats together with"). In their theological categories, this was not kindness. It was theologically incoherent — blurring the boundaries that defined who was in and who was out of God's covenant people.
[^3]: **KONTEXT** — *grumbled*: The Greek verb (διεγόγγυζον) is the same intensified compound found in the Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) at Numbers 14:36, where the Israelites grumble against Moses after the spies' report — a grumbling that represented a failure to trust God's surprising choices. Luke's choice of this word places the Pharisees in a recognizable pattern: those who resist when God's representatives act in unexpected ways.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *open country*: The Greek word (ἔρημος) is routinely translated "wilderness" or "desert," but in a Galilean context it means open pastureland — scrubby hillside grazing land, not barren wasteland. Shepherds in first-century Palestine worked cooperatively; leaving ninety-nine sheep in the open country wasn't reckless because other shepherds remained with the flock. The parable's premise is realistic, not hyperbolic.
[^5]: **TEXT** — *ninety-nine righteous people who don't think they need to*: Who are the "ninety-nine righteous who need no repentance"? The phrase is almost certainly ironic. Jesus is speaking to Pharisees who consider themselves righteous. The irony cuts: if you think you need no turning back, heaven has less joy over you than over the one person who does. Note also that the sheep in the parable never "repented" — it didn't find its way home; it was found and carried. The word "repentance" (μετάνοια) appears in the application, not in the story.
[^6]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *joy in heaven*: What does it mean to claim that events in heaven respond to events on earth? For modern readers, the idea of a cosmic emotional reaction to one person's change of direction raises real questions. The text works on two levels. In Jewish usage, "heaven" was a standard circumlocution for God — "joy in heaven" means "joy before God." The parallel at 15:10 makes this explicit: "joy before the angels of God." Jesus is claiming that the divine response to a single human return is not cool approval or bureaucratic processing but celebration — extravagant, communal, excessive. Whether one reads this as metaphor for divine disposition or literal cosmic event, the theological claim is unmistakable: what matters most to God is not the maintenance of boundaries but the recovery of the lost.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *the searching shepherd*: Jesus is drawing on a deep prophetic tradition. Ezekiel 34:11–16: "I myself will search for my sheep and seek them out... I will seek the lost and bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured." Isaiah 40:11: "He will tend his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms; he will carry them in his bosom." The image of God as the shepherd who carries the found sheep on his shoulders is not an innovation — it is a vivid, personal enactment of what the prophets promised God would do.
[^8]: **LEBEN** — *you go after the lost one*: The parable's most radical claim is embedded in its premise: the shepherd does not wait for the sheep to find its way home. The sheep does not repent, does not signal, does not deserve rescue. The shepherd simply goes. This image of divine initiative — God searching before being asked — runs counter to transactional religious models where human effort earns divine attention. It also challenges the common secular assumption that spiritual growth is entirely self-directed. The parable suggests that being found may be as important as seeking — that grace sometimes arrives before the person being rescued even recognizes they are lost.
[^9]: **APOLOGETICS** — *told them a story*: Kenneth Bailey spent forty years living in Middle Eastern villages and studying the oral culture of peasant communities. His research demonstrates that Jesus' parables reflect the precise social dynamics, agricultural practices, and honor-shame conventions of first-century Galilee with a specificity that would be difficult to fabricate after the fact. The cooperative shepherding practice (leaving ninety-nine with other shepherds), the stone-floor houses where coins fall into crevices, the village feast for a found animal — these are not generic storytelling but culturally precise narrative art. The 5,800+ Greek New Testament manuscripts, many from within two centuries of the originals, provide textual evidence far exceeding any other ancient document. Bailey's conclusion: "These parables bear the marks of an oral tradition carefully preserved by a community that recognized them as the words of their master."
[^10]: **KULTUR** — *ten silver coins — all her savings*: A drachma was a Greek silver coin worth approximately one day's wages for a laborer. Ten drachmas were almost certainly this woman's dowry — the portion of money she brought into her marriage that remained technically hers. Losing one coin means losing one-tenth of all her liquid savings. This is not a trivial loss; it's economically serious. The typical first-century Palestinian village house had rough stone floors with gaps between the slabs — coins fell into crevices and were lost there. Archaeologists have found ancient coins in exactly these floor cracks at excavated sites.
[^11]: **TEXT** — *the women she knows. Friends. Neighbors*: Both Greek nouns here (τὰς φίλας καὶ γείτονας) are unambiguously feminine plural — "women friends and women neighbors." This is not a mixed gathering but a specifically female celebration community. The detail is theologically loaded: the woman is placed as the exact structural parallel to the male shepherd (15:4–6), and her women's network mirrors his male network of friends and neighbors (15:6). Together these two parables form Luke's most explicit male-female pairing — a shepherd and a woman, both God-figures, both searching, both summoning their communities to celebrate. Heaven, in this parable, is imaged as a circle of women rejoicing.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *makes the angels celebrate*: "Joy before the angels of God" is a Jewish circumlocution for joy in God's own presence — the same reality expressed as "joy in heaven" at 15:7. The variation from "heaven" (15:7) to "the angels of God" (15:10) may intensify the image: the entire heavenly court, not merely a location, is filled with celebration.
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *Give me my share of the estate*: In first-century Palestinian culture, this request was devastating — culturally equivalent to publicly wishing the father dead. Jewish inheritance law (Deuteronomy 21:17) allowed designation of assets before death but not distribution. By requesting his portion now, the son was saying: I want what I'd get when you die, and I want it while you're still alive. The father had every legal and social right to refuse — he could have beaten the son, disinherited him, or publicly denounced him. His compliance is not merely generous; it is shockingly lax by the standards of his audience. He divided his βίον — his "livelihood," the property that sustained the whole family — between them.
[^14]: **TEXT** — *Reckless living*: The Greek word (ἀσώτως) describes financial recklessness — wasteful, irresponsible spending. It is not a euphemism for sexual immorality. The narrator deliberately does not specify what the son did with the money. Later, the elder brother accuses him of spending it "with prostitutes" (15:30), but that is the elder brother's claim — the narrator never confirms it. This distinction matters: the parable's power comes from the son's distance and destitution, not from cataloging his sins.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *pigs*: For a Jewish audience, this detail was the absolute bottom. Pigs were profoundly impure under Torah (Leviticus 11:7–8; Deuteronomy 14:8). Feeding pigs meant not just poverty but ritual defilement — daily, sustained contact with the one animal most viscerally associated with Gentile impurity. The son is as far from his covenant identity as it is possible to be.
[^16]: **KULTUR** — *carob pods*: Carob pods (κεράτια) were standard livestock feed — edible by humans in desperation but associated with extreme destitution. A later Talmudic tradition (b. Berakhot 63a) says explicitly: "The Israelites repent only when they are reduced to eating carob pods." Whether or not Jesus' audience knew this specific saying, the degradation was unmistakable: a son of Israel, reduced to envying the food of unclean animals.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *came to his senses (εἰς ἑαυτὸν ἐλθών)*: A classical Greek idiom for recovering right judgment after a period of distress or delusion — paralleled in Diodorus Siculus (13:95.2) and Epictetus (3:1.15). It describes a cognitive recovery, not a spiritual conversion. The son does not have a religious experience; he does a practical calculation: his father's hired workers eat better than he does. His repentance begins with his stomach, not his conscience. The parable does not require pure motives before the father responds.
[^18]: **LEBEN** — *What brought him back was hunger*: The parable is surprisingly honest about what triggers the son's return: not remorse, not insight, not spiritual longing — hunger. He came to his senses not through prayer but through pain. This unsentimental honesty is characteristic of Jesus' storytelling: he does not idealize the process of return. Crisis, failure, and hitting bottom have been recognized across traditions — from Ignatian spirituality's "desolation" to the twelve-step movement's concept of "rock bottom" — as catalysts for genuine change. The parable neither glorifies suffering nor demands that awakening be pretty. It simply notes that it happened, and that it was enough.
[^19]: **KULTUR** — *He ran*: An elderly man of honor and property did not run in public in the ancient Mediterranean world. Running required pulling up one's outer garment and exposing one's legs — a loss of dignity for a man of standing. Kenneth Bailey argues that the father may have been racing to reach the son before the village could enact a communal shaming ritual. Whether or not that specific theory holds, the run is an unambiguous self-humbling: the father destroys his own dignity to close the distance. He reaches his son before his son reaches him. The initiative — and the cost — is entirely the father's.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *compassion seized him (ἐσπλαγχνίσθη)*: From σπλάγχνα — literally "intestines" or "gut." This is not intellectual sympathy or measured kindness. It is a word for compassion felt in the body before the mind processes it: a gut-punch of love. The same word appears in the Septuagint rendering of Jeremiah 31:20, where God says of wayward Ephraim: "My heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him." Luke uses the word five times, always for Jesus or for God-figures in parables (7:13; 10:33; 15:20). It is the signature emotion of the divine in Luke's Gospel.
[^21]: **LEBEN** — *He ran*: The father's run is not merely affection; it is a strategic act of grace. In an honor-shame culture, the returning son would face a gauntlet of village judgment. The father running — destroying his own dignity — intercepts his son before the community can. Grace, in this image, is not passive acceptance; it is active, costly, and public. It absorbs the shame the son earned so the son does not have to face it alone. This pattern — one person bearing the cost so another does not have to — is central to how Christians understand what happened on the cross. But even without that theological frame, the image carries universal weight: someone running toward you when you expect them to stand back and let you grovel.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *I don't deserve to be called your son*: The son's rehearsed speech (15:18–19) included a final line: "Make me one of your hired workers." At the moment of delivery, that line never comes. The best ancient manuscripts (P75, Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus) end the son's speech here; some later manuscripts add the missing line to harmonize with 15:19. The shorter text is almost certainly original — and the theological point is precisely this: the father interrupts. He does not wait for the son to finish his carefully prepared petition. Grace acts before complete repentance.
[^23]: **KULTUR** — *The best robe ... A ring ... Sandals*: Each restoration gift carries precise social meaning. The "best robe" (στολὴν τὴν πρώτην) was probably the father's own finest garment — a visible, public act of status restoration. The ring (δακτύλιον) was almost certainly a signet ring, a legal instrument bearing the family seal and conferring authority to act on the family's behalf — not jewelry but the reinstatement of legal personhood. Sandals (ὑποδήματα) marked free persons; slaves went barefoot. By giving sandals, the father explicitly refuses the servant-status the son had proposed. The cumulative effect: total, immediate, unconditional restoration to full sonship.
[^24]: **KULTUR** — *the fattened calf*: A grain-fed calf (μόσχος σιτευτός) was reserved for major celebrations and produced enough meat to feed an entire village. This is not a private family dinner but a public, community-wide feast — the father's declaration to the whole village that his son has been restored.
[^25]: **KONTEXT** — *dead — and is alive*: The Greek verb ἀναζάω means "to come back to life" — it is used elsewhere for literal resurrection. This is not metaphorical softness ("he was as good as dead"); it is deliberate death-and-resurrection language. The father's declaration echoes Ezekiel 37 — the valley of dry bones, where God tells exiled Israel: "I will put my Spirit in you, and you shall live." For N.T. Wright, this is the exile-and-return story in miniature: the son who went to the far country is Israel scattered among the nations; his return is the promised restoration; the feast is the celebration that exile is over.
[^26]: **TEXT** — *slaved for you*: The Greek word (δουλεύω) is the standard term for slave-labor — from δοῦλος, "slave." The elder son doesn't say "I served you" or "I worked for you"; he says "I slaved." This is not just a complaint about unfairness; it's a labor-exploitation complaint. It reveals how the elder son experienced his faithfulness — not as joyful sonship but as forced labor under obligation. And yet the father's response (15:31) doesn't dispute the years of service; he simply says: "You are always with me. Everything I have is yours." The elder son had access to everything all along. He just never asked.
[^27]: **LEBEN** — *I have slaved for you. All these years.*: The elder son's complaint reveals something painful about long-term faithfulness: it can curdle into resentment. He stayed. He obeyed. He never left. And he experienced all of it as slavery. The father's response — "You are always with me. Everything I have is yours" — suggests that the elder son had access to everything all along but never claimed it, never celebrated, never invited his friends over for a young goat. He was living as a slave in his own father's house. This is not just a first-century story. It describes a recognizable pattern in relationships, workplaces, and religious communities: the dutiful person who has never learned to receive freely what was freely offered.
[^28]: **TEXT** — *this son of yours ... with prostitutes*: Two details demand attention. First, "this son of yours" (ὁ υἱός σου οὗτος) — the elder son refuses to say "my brother." The relational distancing is deliberate and painful. Second, "with prostitutes" (μετὰ πορνῶν) — this is the elder son's accusation. The narrator never said the younger son spent money on prostitutes. The text at 15:13 says only that he spent "recklessly" (ἀσώτως), a term that describes financial wastefulness, not sexual behavior. The elder son may be inferring, projecting, or repeating village gossip — but the narrator has not confirmed it. Readers should not treat this as established fact.
[^29]: **TEXT** — *My child*: The father addresses the elder son as τέκνον — "child," a term of tender intimacy, more personal than the formal υἱέ ("son"). In the face of the elder son's fury and contempt, the father responds with the most tender word available to him.
[^30]: **KONTEXT** — *Your brother*: The father's "this brother of yours" (ὁ ἀδελφός σου οὗτος) directly corrects the elder son's "this son of yours" (15:30). The father insists on kinship: whatever the younger son has done, he is still your brother. The relational restoration is not optional. And the word ἔδει ("it was fitting" / "it was necessary") frames the celebration as the only appropriate response to death becoming life. This is not a reward for good behavior. It is the fitting response to resurrection.
[^31]: **LEBEN** — *The open ending*: The parable ends here. The elder son never answers. He is standing outside the feast, and we do not know whether he goes in. This silence is not a flaw; it is the theological point. The Pharisees and Torah scholars who grumbled at 15:2 are standing exactly where the elder son stands — outside the celebration, with a legitimate complaint and an open invitation. The parable does not resolve their question for them. It presents the invitation and waits. Every reader, in every generation, who has been faithful and resented someone else's welcome stands in the same place. The question is always the same: Will you go in?

## Kapitel 17


### Stumbling Blocks and Forgiveness


**1** *He turned to his disciples.* "It is impossible," he said, "that causes of stumbling will not come — but woe to the one who sets them.
[^1]
**2** Better to have a heavy millstone hung around your neck and be hurled into the deep than to cause even one of these vulnerable ones to fall.
[^2]
[^3]

**3** Watch yourselves. If your brother sins, rebuke him — and if he repents, forgive him.
[^4]
[^5]
**4** If he sins against you seven times in a single day, and seven times he turns back and says, 'I repent' — *you will* forgive him."
[^6]

### Faith Like a Mustard Seed


**5** The apostles said to the Lord: "Increase our faith."

**6** He said, "If you had faith even the size of a mustard seed, you would say to this mulberry tree, 'Be ripped out by the roots and planted in the sea' — and it would obey you."
[^7]
[^8]

### The Dutiful Slave


**7** "Which of you has a slave who's been out plowing your field or tending your sheep *all day*? When he comes in, would you tell him, 'Come, sit right down to eat'?
**8** *Of course not. You would say,* 'Get my dinner ready. Put on your apron and serve me while I eat and drink — and after that, you can eat and drink yourself.'
**9** Does the master thank the slave for doing what was commanded? *No.*
**10** So it is with you. When you have done everything that was asked of you, say*this*: 'We are unworthy slaves. We have done only what we owed.'"
[^9]

[^11]

### The Ten Lepers


**11** He was headed toward Jerusalem, moving through the border country between Samaria and Galilee*— the kind of place where people who belonged nowhere ended up*.
**12** As he neared a village, ten men called out to him. All of them had a serious skin disease.[^13]
*In that world, it meant exile — from the temple, from work, from everyone they loved.* They stood at a distance.*The law demanded it.*
[^14]

**13** "Jesus! Master!" they shouted. "Have mercy on us!"

**14** He looked*across the gap*. "Go — show yourselves to the priests."
[^15]
[^16]


**15** *Nine kept walking.*

**16** and threw himself face down at Jesus' feet, giving thanks.
[^17]


**17** Jesus said: "Ten were cleansed — were they not?
**18** Then where are the nine? Not one of them came back to give glory to God — no one but this outsider?"
[^18]
[^19]

**19** He looked at the man on the ground. "Get up. Go. Your faith has saved you."
[^20]

### The Kingdom Among You


**20** The Pharisees asked him: "When is the kingdom of God coming?"
[^22]

[^23]
**21** No one will say, 'Look, here it is!' or 'There!' Because the kingdom of God is *already* among you."

### The Day of the Son of Man


**22** He turned to his disciples. "The time is coming," he said, "when you will long to see even one of the days of the Son of Man.
[^25]
You will not see it.
**23** They will tell you, 'Look — over there!' or 'Right here!' Don't go after them. Don't follow.
[^26]
**24** The Son of Man's day will be like lightning — it cracks from one horizon to the other.*You won't need anyone to point it out.*

**25** "But first he must suffer. He must be rejected by this generation.
[^27]

**26** "Remember what it was like in Noah's time. People were eating, drinking, marrying*— all of it ordinary —*
**27** until the day Noah stepped into the ark. Then the flood came. It swept every one of them away.

**28** "The same thing happened with Lot. They were eating and drinking, buying and selling, planting and building*— nothing out of the ordinary —*
**29** until the day Lot walked out of Sodom. Then fire and sulfur came down from the sky and destroyed them all.

**30** "That is how it will be on the day the Son of Man is revealed.
[^31]

**31** "When that day comes, if you are on the rooftop, do not go back inside for your things.
[^32]
If you are in the field, do not turn back for anything.
**32** Remember Lot's wife.
[^33]

**33** "Whoever clutches at their life will lose it. Whoever lets go*of their life* will be given life.
[^34]

**35** Two women will be grinding grain together. One taken. One left."

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *causes of stumbling*: The Greek word (\fq σκάνδαλον\fq*) originally meant the trigger-mechanism of a trap — the stick an animal touches that springs the snare. Extended: an enticement to spiritual or moral ruin. Not merely "offense" or "scandal" in the modern sense, but a mechanism designed to cause someone's destruction.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *a heavy millstone*: The Greek (\fq λίθος μυλικός\fq*) refers to a commercial-scale millstone — the upper stone of a donkey-driven grain mill, weighing 200–400 kg (440–880 lbs). Not the small household quern a woman could operate, but the massive stone that required an animal to turn. The image is deliberately excessive: deep-sea disposal with a weight that ensures no recovery. Jewish people considered Roman punishment by drowning-with-weights barbaric — it was not a Jewish execution form — which makes the hyperbole even more shocking.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *these vulnerable ones*: "These little ones" (\fq τῶν μικρῶν τούτων\fq*) in Luke's social vocabulary denotes the powerless and marginalized — not only children (though children are included) but all those at the bottom of the social hierarchy: clients without patrons, the economically marginal, those with no recourse.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *if he repents, forgive him*: The conditional structure (Greek: \fq ἐὰν μετανοήσῃ\fq* — "if he repents") is not optional. Forgiveness in this passage is explicitly conditional on genuine repentance. This is not unconditional forgiveness — a concept deeply embedded in modern Western culture but not what the text says. The sequence is precise: first rebuke (active confrontation, not passive avoidance), then — if and only if repentance occurs — forgiveness. This ordering protects the community's vulnerable members.
[^5]: **KONTEXT** — *rebuke him*: The command to rebuke (\fq ἐπιτίμησον\fq*) before forgiving is rooted in Leviticus 19:17: "Rebuke your neighbor frankly so you will not share in their guilt." The fraternal correction tradition was active in first-century Judaism: the Damascus Document at Qumran specifies reproof procedures (CD 9:2–8). Jesus stands fully within this tradition while pushing the community's forgiveness capacity to an extreme.
[^6]: **LEBEN** — *seven times in a single day*: Seven times in one day is a theoretical extreme — a limit case designed to test the outer boundary of forgiveness. The number seven signals completeness. Even in this scenario, each instance still requires genuine repentance (\fq μετανοῶ\fq* — "I repent," "I am turning"). The repetition does not eliminate the conditionality; it expands the patience of the person who forgives. Two things this verse is not: it is not a command to maintain proximity with someone who keeps harming you; and it is not a guarantee that you must feel warm again every time. It is a refusal to let your interior life be permanently organized around someone else's failure. — A question that lands without theological warmup: who do you keep score on, even when you have officially forgiven them? An ex-partner whose name still lights up something acidic. A parent whose old patterns continue under new disguises. A colleague who keeps using and apologizing. The verse does not require you to pretend the pattern is not there. It requires you to notice that holding the ledger has begun to organize your inner life around them, and to wonder if you are ready to lay it down — again, and probably again. — A low-threshold practice this week: when you notice the old grievance running automatically through your head — at the gym, in traffic, at 2 a.m. — say once, internally, "I do not have to carry this today." Not as denial. As a small interruption of the rehearsal loop. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is careful. Augustine distinguished between forgiveness and reconciliation — the first is your work, the second requires the other's turning. Miroslav Volf, who lost family to the Yugoslav wars, wrote in Exclusion and Embrace that forgiveness without truth-telling is cheap, but truth-telling without forgiveness is permanent war. Twelve-step recovery teaches the same: resentment is the alcoholic's number one offender. — Pastorally, the verse has often been weaponized against abuse survivors. It should not be. The text presupposes a community where confrontation is real and repentance is named. Where those are missing, the verse does not yet apply. — This week: lay down one ledger, even just for an afternoon.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *Increase our faith*: The apostles' request treats faith as a quantifiable substance that can be incremented — like adding money to an account. Jesus' response demolishes this assumption. The Greek grammar is a contrary-to-fact conditional (\fq εἰ ἔχετε\fq* ... \fq ἐλέγετε ἂν\fq* ... \fq ὑπήκουσεν ἄν\fq*): "If you had faith ... you would say ... it would have obeyed." This is NOT a promise that faith moves trees. It is a deconstruction of the quantity question: even the smallest conceivable faith (the mustard seed was proverbially the smallest seed in Jewish tradition) would be sufficient — so "more faith" is not what you need. The problem is not the quantity of your faith but the category error about what faith is. Faith is not a spiritual currency to accumulate; it is a relational trust to enact.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *this mulberry tree*: The black mulberry (Morus nigra), not the sycamore-fig of Luke 19:4. The mulberry was renowned in the ancient world for its exceptionally deep, spreading root system — among the most difficult trees to uproot in the Mediterranean basin (Theophrastus, History of Plants 4.2). The image is chosen for its absurdity: uprooting the un-uproottable and planting it in the sea (where trees cannot grow). The hyperbole IS the point.
[^9]: **TEXT** — *slave*: The Greek \fq δοῦλος\fq* means "slave" — a person held in bondage, without legal freedom or social recognition. Many translations soften this to "servant," but the leading Greek-English lexicon (BDAG) explicitly notes that "servant" obscures the social reality of slavery. The parable's theological force depends on the full weight of the slave's position: someone with zero social standing generates no credit for doing their duty. If even the slave cannot place the master in their debt, then no disciple can place God in theirs.
[^10]: **KULTUR** — *unworthy slaves*: The word \fq ἀχρεῖοι\fq* ("unworthy, unprofitable") specifically negates the accumulation of merit or credit. The parable is not a counsel of despair or a statement about the disciple's worthlessness — it dismantles the transactional model of religious obedience. Service is what is owed, not a deposit in a spiritual savings account. The Reformation's foundational anti-merit theology drew heavily on this passage.
[^11]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *slavery language in a teaching of Jesus*: This parable uses the most extreme social image available in the ancient world — enslaved labor, social non-personhood — to make a theological point about grace. It is not a legitimization of slavery. In communities shaped by the history of chattel slavery, the passage has been misused to enforce compliance and to theologically justify bondage. The exegetical counter-reading: Jesus deploys the lowest-status self-designation available to puncture hierarchical spiritual pride among his own followers. The slave's absolute inability to accumulate merit models the disciple's relationship with God — not because slavery is good, but because the spiritual arithmetic of debt and credit does not apply.
[^12]: **LEBEN** — *We have done only what we owed*: Performance culture trains people to expect recognition for effort — bonuses, promotions, gratitude. This parable dismantles that expectation in the spiritual life. The point is not that effort is worthless but that no amount of obedience creates a balance in your favor with God. The relationship is not transactional. For anyone who has ever felt they have "earned" God's blessing — or, conversely, that God "owes" them something after sustained faithfulness — the parable offers a disorienting but liberating correction: grace is always gift, never payment. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where have you been keeping score with God? The honest answer often emerges in suffering. "I have tithed for twenty years and my marriage is still falling apart." "I raised my children in the church and they no longer believe." "I served and prayed and now I have cancer." The grief is real and the verse does not deny it. What it denies is the architecture: that obedience builds up credit which suffering then unfairly withdraws. The Christian life is not a savings account. It is a friendship. — A low-threshold practice this week: notice one place where you are working for credit rather than working from love. The volunteer role you've kept because leaving would feel like failure. The relational gesture you make and then quietly resent when it is not returned. The faithfulness you perform with a half-conscious expectation that it will be repaid. Not to stop being faithful — to stop being a creditor. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is consoling. Martin Luther rediscovered this verse with violent relief — that nothing about his salvation depended on the ledger he had been keeping. Thérèse of Lisieux's "little way" rests on this same truth: nothing she did earned God's love; everything she did was the response to it. Twelve-step recovery captures it crisply: "we do not earn our chips." — Vocationally, the verse is also a labor question. Whole industries — service work, ministry, caregiving — wear people out by demanding gratitude they may never receive. The verse offers a different motor: not credit, but participation. — This week: do one good thing and do not tell anyone.
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *a serious skin disease*: The Greek \fq λέπρα\fq* covers a broad range of skin conditions causing ritual and social exclusion under Leviticus 13–14. It is not the same as modern leprosy (Hansen's disease, caused by Mycobacterium leprae). Under Levitical law, a person with \fq λέπρα\fq* was barred from inhabited areas, from the temple, from work, and from community life. The disease was a sentence of total social exile.
[^14]: **KONTEXT** — *stood at a distance*: Levitical law (Lev 13:46; Num 5:2) required persons with skin diseases to remain outside inhabited areas. The distance was not chosen but mandated — a legally precise detail. Their shouting (v. 13) reflects the required gap: to be heard, they had to raise their voices across it.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *show yourselves to the priests*: Under Levitical law, only a priest could certify that a skin disease had healed and readmit the person to community life (Lev 14:1–32). Jesus directs them into the established process, not around it. The healing occurs in process of obedience — "as they were going" (\fq ἐν τῷ ὑπάγειν\fq*) — before they reach any priest. They trust his word before seeing results.
[^16]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *their skin cleared*: Ten people with serious skin diseases are healed simultaneously, without physical contact or visible intervention, while walking down a road. For modern readers, this is a miracle claim that demands honest engagement. Three frameworks: (1) Naturalistic — psychosomatic conditions can remit under strong expectation and social support; some skin disorders are stress-responsive. (2) Symbolic — Luke uses the healing as a literary vehicle for the theological point about gratitude, exclusion, and salvation; the physical details serve the narrative's larger meaning. (3) Historical-faith — Luke presents this as a real event involving divine power mediated through Jesus' word. The text does not explain the mechanism; it describes the result: "as they were going, they were cleansed." What all frameworks share: the ten acted on trust before seeing results — they went to show themselves to priests while still sick. The healing occurred in the space between obedience and arrival.
[^17]: **KONTEXT** — *giving thanks*: The Greek \fq εὐχαριστῶν\fq* ("giving thanks") shares the root of "Eucharist." Luke uses this word in narrative context only twice: here, and at the Last Supper (22:17, 19). The Samaritan's thanksgiving anticipates the eucharistic action — the return of the healed to the healer in gratitude. His prostration (falling on his face) is the Old Testament gesture of encounter with divine presence (Ezek 1:28; Dan 8:17; Gen 17:3).
[^18]: **KULTUR** — *this outsider*: The Greek \fq ἀλλογενής\fq* ("foreigner, one of another race") is the precise word inscribed on stone barriers in the Jerusalem temple, warning foreigners that crossing meant death. An actual inscription discovered in 1871 (now in the Istanbul Archaeological Museum) reads: "No foreigner (\fq ἀλλογενής\fq*) is to enter within the balustrade around the sacred precinct. Whoever is caught will have himself to blame for his subsequent death." Jesus uses the temple's own exclusion terminology — and the one excluded by that terminology is the only one who returns.
[^19]: **KONTEXT** — *the Samaritan's return*: The healing echoes 2 Kings 5:1–14, where Elisha heals Naaman the Aramean (a Syrian outsider) of a skin disease. Luke 4:27 has already cited this story in Jesus' Nazareth sermon. The outsider healed of skin disease is a deliberate Lukan pattern. The Samaritan's return to Jesus is also practical necessity: the Levitical purification ritual (Lev 14:1–32) requires presentation to priests in Jerusalem — which the Samaritan, as an \fq ἀλλογενής\fq*, cannot access. His return is not only gratitude but the recognition that his route to restoration runs through Jesus, not the institution that bars him.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *has saved you*: The nine were "cleansed" (v. 17, Greek \fq ἐκαθαρίσθησαν\fq* — ritual purification). The Samaritan is told his faith has "saved" him (Greek \fq σέσωκέν σε\fq* — the verb \fq σώζω\fq* meaning wholeness, salvation, full restoration). The distinction is deliberate and central: all ten received physical healing; only the one who returned received something qualitatively more — not merely cleansing but salvation, not merely the removal of disease but the restoration of relationship.
[^21]: **LEBEN** — *Your faith has saved you*: The nine did nothing wrong. They followed instructions. They went to the priests. They were healed. But only the one who stopped, turned around, and came back received the word "saved." The difference between the nine and the one is not obedience — all ten obeyed. It is attention. The Samaritan noticed what had happened to him and let it change his direction. Gratitude here is not a feeling but a reorientation — a physical turn, a return. In a culture that rewards forward motion and productivity, the Samaritan models something countercultural: sometimes the most important thing to do is stop, go back, and say thank you. — A question that lands without theological warmup: what gifts have you received and never gone back to acknowledge? Not just from God — from people. The teacher in seventh grade who actually saw you. The friend who covered for you during the worst year. The boss who took a risk on you when your resume did not justify it. The doctor who caught something early. Gratitude that stays inside the head is not yet gratitude. The Samaritan ran back. — A low-threshold practice this week: pick one person who shaped your life and never knew it. Write to them. A text, an email, a card. Two sentences is enough. Say what they did. Say thank you. Send it. Notice what happens in you while you do it. The act is at least as transformative for the sender as for the recipient. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is rich. Ignatius placed the "Contemplation to Attain Love" at the climax of the Spiritual Exercises — an extended exercise in recognizing the gifts already received as the foundation of any deeper response to God. Dietrich Bonhoeffer in Letters and Papers from Prison kept writing thank-you notes from his cell — to friends, to his fiancée, to those who smuggled in cigarettes — as a discipline of refusing to let captivity organize his interior life around lack. — Civically, the verse is also a quiet politics. Societies that lose the capacity to say thank you become brittle, suspicious, transactional. Gratitude is a public good. — This week: turn around once, deliberately, and go back.
[^22]: **KULTUR** — *the Pharisees asked*: The Pharisees here are legitimate theological questioners, not hostile opponents. Their question reflects the widespread first-century Jewish expectation that God's kingdom would arrive at a specific future moment, identifiable by observable signs — an expectation shared across many streams of Second Temple Judaism (Psalms of Solomon 17–18; the Qumran War Scroll; 4 Ezra).
[^23]: **TEXT** — *observable signs*: The Greek \fq παρατηρήσεως\fq* means "careful observation, sign-watching" — scanning for celestial or political phenomena that would signal God's eschatological intervention. Jesus rejects the sign-watching method itself, not merely specific signs.
[^24]: **TEXT** — *among you*: The Greek \fq ἐντὸς ὑμῶν\fq* can mean either "within you" (interior) or "among you" (in your midst). The older translation "within you" (KJV) has deeply influenced pietist and mystical traditions. However, post-2010 scholarship decisively favors "among you" for three reasons: (1) Jesus is speaking to the Pharisees — telling hostile questioners the kingdom is "within" them is contextually incoherent; (2) Luke's kingdom theology is consistently social and enacted, not interior; (3) the closest lexical parallel (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.3.19) uses \fq ἐντός\fq* to mean "in the midst of." The kingdom is not a private inner experience but a present reality enacted in Jesus' ministry — in his healings, his meals with outsiders, his restoration of the marginalized.
[^25]: **TEXT** — *the Son of Man*: A title drawn from Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" receives cosmic authority and dominion over all peoples. Jesus uses it as his primary self-designation in eschatological contexts.
[^26]: **KULTUR** — *Don't go after them*: In the first century, multiple messianic claimants appeared claiming to lead movements of liberation (documented in Josephus, Antiquities 17.278–285; 20.97–99, 167–172). Jesus' warning is historically concrete: do not follow localized messianic claims. Any genuine revelation of the Son of Man will not be localized — it will be universal (v. 24).
[^27]: **KONTEXT** — *first he must suffer*: The cross interrupts the eschatological sequence — it is the prerequisite, not an obstacle. This verse anchors eschatological hope in the Passion: the consummation cannot be understood apart from the suffering that precedes it.
[^28]: **TEXT** — *swept every one of them away*: The Greek verb \fq ἀπώλεσεν\fq* means "destroyed/took away all." This is the interpretive key for vv. 34–35: in the Noah analogy, those "taken" by the flood were destroyed, while Noah was preserved ("left"). See note at v. 34.
[^29]: **KULTUR** — *eating, drinking, marrying*: None of the activities listed is sinful. The point is not that Noah's contemporaries were wicked but that they were oblivious — going about ordinary life, unaware of the urgency of the moment. The judgment catches people in the routines of normal existence, not in spectacular sin.
[^30]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *fire and sulfur from the sky*: Luke specifies no sin for Sodom's residents in this passage. The activities listed are entirely ordinary. The text is about the surprise of judgment within everyday life, not about any specific moral failing. The common reduction of Sodom's significance to sexual sin is not supported by this Lukan text. Ezekiel 16:49 describes Sodom's sin as "arrogance, excess of food, and prosperous ease" combined with failure to help the poor and needy — a social-justice reading that the early church fathers also recognized.
[^31]: **TEXT** — *is revealed*: Luke deliberately avoids the word \fq παρουσία\fq* ("coming/return") that Matthew and Paul use. Instead he says the Son of Man "is revealed" (Greek \fq ἀποκαλύπτεται\fq* — unveiled, disclosed). This suggests not arrival from elsewhere but the uncovering of what is already true.
[^32]: **KULTUR** — *on the rooftop*: First-century Palestinian houses had flat roofs accessible by outside stairs. A person on the roof could flee directly without re-entering the house — but only if they did not go back for possessions. The image conveys extreme urgency.
[^33]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Lot's wife*: Six Greek words with enormous interpretive weight. Genesis 19:26 does not say why Lot's wife looked back. The long tradition that reads her looking back as "feminine weakness," curiosity, or disobedience projects onto the text's silence. A more compassionate and textually honest reading: she looked back from grief — leaving behind a life, a community, daughters she loved. The text describes her fate without explaining her motive. What Jesus draws from the story is not a judgment on her character but a warning about the danger of backward orientation when the moment demands forward movement.
[^34]: **TEXT** — *will be given life*: The Greek verb \fq ζῳογονήσει\fq* is rare — it means "to cause to live, to actively give life." It emphasizes that life is a gift actively bestowed, not merely a survival outcome. This is one of the most repeated sayings in the Synoptic tradition (Mark 8:35; Matt 10:39; 16:25; Luke 9:24; John 12:25).
[^35]: **LEBEN** — *Whoever clutches at their life*: The verb "clutches" captures the Greek \fq ζητήσῃ περιποιήσασθαι\fq* — actively seeking to secure, preserve, and protect one's life. The image is physical: hands gripping. The paradox cuts against every survival instinct: the tighter you hold, the more slips through your fingers. This is not a call to recklessness or self-destruction — it is a diagnosis of the anxiety that turns life into a possession to be defended rather than a gift to be lived. Career, reputation, security, relationships — anything clutched too tightly becomes a cage. Letting go is not loss; it is the precondition for receiving life as gift rather than grasping it as commodity. — A question that lands without theological warmup: what are you holding so tightly right now that you can no longer actually live it? The list is usually concrete — the apartment you have decorated against any future change; the relationship you can no longer move inside of, for fear of losing it; the career that defines you so completely that any pivot feels like identity death; the self-image as competent, independent, good. Clutching is not love — it is fear that has learned to look like love. — A low-threshold practice this week: name one thing you are clutching. Do not throw it away. Just admit, out loud or on paper, that you are holding it. Sometimes the grip loosens just by being seen. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is deep. Meister Eckhart made Gelassenheit — releasement, letting-be — the core of his spiritual path: not indifference, but the capacity to hold everything without strangling it. John of the Cross described the same process in The Dark Night as purification — the slow loosening of the soul's grip on lesser goods so that it can receive the greater. Etty Hillesum, the Dutch Jewish writer who died in Auschwitz in 1943, wrote in her diaries that she loved life more, not less, as death approached — and that the loving was different because the gripping was gone. — Vocationally and relationally, the test is concrete. The person who cannot leave a job that is killing them; the parent whose identity has fused with the child's success; the artist who cannot risk losing an audience. Each hears the same word. — This week: loosen one grip without letting go. That is enough for a start.
[^36]: **HARM** — *one will be taken... one left*: In context, "taken" means taken away to judgment — as the flood "took away" (destroyed) all of Noah's contemporaries (v. 27) while Noah was preserved. "Left" means left alone, preserved from judgment. The popular "Rapture" interpretation — where "taken" means believers rescued to heaven and "left" means abandoned to tribulation — inverts the text's own logic. No ancient, medieval, or Reformation-era commentator read the text this way. The Rapture reading originates with John Nelson Darby in the 19th century and has no support in the pre-modern interpretive tradition. When the disciples ask "Where, Lord?" (v. 37), Jesus answers with vultures at a corpse — pointing to the site of judgment, not a heavenly destination.
[^37]: **TEXT** — *[Verse 36 omitted]*: Verse 36 ("Two men will be in the field; one will be taken, the other left"), found in some older translations including the King James Version, is absent from the earliest and most reliable manuscripts (including Papyrus 75, Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, and Codex Alexandrinus). It was copied here from Matthew 24:40 by a later scribe and does not belong to Luke's text.
[^38]: **TEXT** — *vultures*: The Greek \fq ἀετοί\fq* can mean either "eagles" or "vultures." Since the context is a body (\fq σῶμα\fq*) attracting scavenging birds, vultures are required — eagles do not eat carrion. The image is grim and precise: judgment finds its objects the way vultures find a carcass. The question "where?" receives not a geographical answer but a natural-law metaphor: wherever there is something dead, the agents of reckoning will gather.

## Kapitel 18


### The Persistent Widow and the Unjust Judge


**1** Jesus told them a parable about why they should always pray and never give up.
[^1]

**2** "There was a judge in a certain city. He feared neither God nor the shame of his neighbors*— answerable to no one, accountable to nothing*.
[^2]
**3** And in that city, a widow.*No husband. No patron. No one to press her case or open doors. She had only herself, and a legal claim no one was interested in hearing.*
[^3]


**4** He refused.



**5** Finally the judge said to himself: 'I don't fear God, and I don't care what anyone thinks. But this woman will not stop. She is going to damage my name if she keeps showing up in my court, making her demands where everyone can see. I'll grant her what she wants*— for my own sake, not hers*.'
[^4]

**6** Then the Lord said: 'You heard the unjust judge.
**7** Now think about God. If even a man like that eventually grants justice*— a man with no conscience and no shame —* how much more will God bring about justice for his chosen ones, who cry out to him day and night? Will he delay?
[^5]

**8** I tell you: he will bring justice. And swiftly.
[^6]

[^7]

### The Pharisee and the Tax Collector


**9** He told this next parable to people who had settled into trusting their own righteousness — and who looked down on everyone else.

**10** "Two men went up to the Temple to pray. One was a Pharisee. The other was a tax collector.
[^8]

**11** The Pharisee stood and prayed: 'God, I thank you that I am not like other people — swindlers, cheaters, adulterers. I am certainly not like this tax collector.  **12** I fast twice a week. I give a tenth of everything I earn*— even what the law doesn't require*.'
[^9]

[^10]

**13** The tax collector stood far off. He would not lift his eyes. He kept striking his chest — again and again — and all he said was this: 'God, have mercy on me — the sinner.'
[^11]
[^12]
[^13]


**14** Jesus said: 'I tell you, this man went home declared righteous — rather than the other. Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled. And whoever humbles himself will be exalted.'"
[^14]
[^15]

### Children and the Kingdom


**15** People were bringing their infants to Jesus, wanting him to touch them. The disciples rebuked them*— a teacher's time was not for this*.
[^16]
[^17]

**16** But Jesus called the children over. "Let them come to me," he said. "Don't stop them. The kingdom of God belongs to people like these."

**17** "Listen carefully: whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child will never enter it."
[^18]


### The Rich Ruler


**18** A ruler approached him and asked: "Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?"
[^19]

**19** Jesus said: "Why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone.
**20** You know the commandments: don't commit adultery, don't murder, don't steal, don't bear false witness, honor your father and mother."

**21** "All of these," the man said, "I have kept since I was young."


**22** Jesus looked at him and said: "There is still one thing you lack. Sell everything you have. Give it to the poor. You will have treasure in heaven. Then come — follow me."
[^20]

**23** When the man heard this, a deep sadness came over him. He was extremely wealthy.
[^21]

**24** Jesus, watching him, said to those around him: "How hard it is for the wealthy to enter the kingdom of God.
**25** It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter God's kingdom."
[^22]
[^23]

**26** The people who heard it said: "Then who can possibly be saved?"

**27** "What is impossible for people," Jesus said, "is possible for God."

**28** Peter spoke up: "Look — we left everything and followed you."

**29** Jesus said: "I tell you the truth: everyone who has left home, or wife, or brothers, or parents, or children for the sake of the kingdom of God  **30** will receive far more in this present life — and in the age to come, eternal life."
[^24]

### The Third Passion Prediction


**31** Jesus took the Twelve aside*— away from the crowd, away from everyone —* and said: "We are going up to Jerusalem. Everything written by the prophets about the Son of Man will be fulfilled there.
[^25]
**32** He will be handed over to the Gentiles. Mocked. Insulted. Spit on. Flogged.
**33** And killed."
[^26]



**34** They understood none of it. The meaning was hidden from them*, as if sealed shut,* and they could not grasp what he was saying.
[^27]
[^28]

### The Healing of the Blind Beggar near Jericho


**35** As Jesus came near Jericho, a blind man sat beside the road, begging. *This was his life — the road, the dust, the sound of passing feet, the coins that sometimes fell.*
[^29]
**36** He heard a crowd moving past, larger than usual, and asked what was going on.

**37** "Jesus of Nazareth," they told him. "He's passing through."

**38** The man cried out*— not a request but a shout*: "Jesus! Son of David, have mercy on me!"
[^30]

**39** The crowd told him to be quiet.*A blind beggar had no business shouting at a rabbi — certainly not with a title like that.*


**40** Jesus stopped.


**41** When the man stood before him, Jesus asked: "What do you want me to do for you?"


**42** "Receive your sight," Jesus told him. "Your faith has saved you."
[^31]
[^32]
[^33]

**43** Immediately, he could see. *And he did not go home.* He followed Jesus*— kept following, on the road toward Jerusalem —* praising God. And all the people who saw it gave praise to God.
[^34]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KONTEXT** — *always pray and never give up*: Luke states the parable's purpose before telling it — an unusual move. Most parables reveal their meaning afterward. By front-loading the moral ("always pray and never give up"), Luke signals that the story is not a puzzle to solve but a question to sit with. The question comes at the end (18:8), and it is aimed at the reader.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *feared neither God nor the shame*: In Roman-period Palestine, local judges obtained their positions through patronage networks. A judge who "feared neither God nor people" existed outside both divine accountability (covenant law obligated judges to defend widows — Deuteronomy 27:19; Isaiah 1:17) and social accountability (the community honor-obligations that normally enforced minimal justice). He is structurally unchecked. And yet the widow still wins.
[^3]: **KULTUR** — *a widow*: Widows without adult sons occupied the lowest rung of the social-legal hierarchy. Roman law placed women under male guardianship (tutela mulierium). A poor widow without a male advocate pressing her case in a local court faced structural barriers at every stage — no money for bribes, no patron, no social capital. Her repeated appearances in a male-coded public court were acts of social transgression that violated both gender and class norms.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *damage my name*: The Greek word \fq ὑπωπιάζῃ\fq* literally means "to strike under the eye" or "give a black eye to" (Aristotle, Rhetoric 3.11.15). Most translations soften this to "wear me out," but the original image is of physical violence or — more likely in context — public reputational damage. The widow's persistence threatens to shame the judge before the community. She is not a nuisance; she is a genuine social threat to his honor.
[^5]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *persistent prayer*: Does persistent prayer "work"? Three perspectives: (1) The skeptic reads this as magical thinking — repeating requests doesn't change cosmic outcomes. (2) The theological reader sees the parable's argument: the contrast is between the unjust judge (who acts from self-interest after nagging) and God (who needs no convincing and will act swiftly). The point is not that more prayer causes God to act, but that God's character guarantees action. (3) The formation reader understands persistent prayer as the practice of maintaining faith through apparent divine silence — prayer changes the pray-er, not God's mind. Jesus himself seems to hold the third view: the real question is not "Will God act?" but "Will faith survive the waiting?"
[^6]: **TEXT** — *swiftly*: The Greek \fq ἐν τάχει\fq* means "swiftly, without delay" — emphasizing the speed of God's action when it comes, not the timeline until it comes. "Soon" (a common translation) introduces a false ambiguity. The emphasis is on divine decisiveness, not divine punctuality.
[^7]: **LEBEN** — *will he actually find faith*: The question lands flat at the end of the parable: when the Son of Man comes, will he actually find faith on the earth? Luke does not soften it. Jesus is not predicting that faith will vanish — he is warning that the long stretch of apparent divine silence is the most demanding part of belief, and that the test is real. Faith here is not a feeling; it is the question of whether anyone is still showing up. — A question worth holding without immediate answer: where in your life is there something you stopped asking for because you stopped expecting it? Not because the asking failed — because you outgrew, or grew tired of, hoping at all. Most adults have an inventory of these quiet abandonments; faith is, among other things, the refusal to keep that list closed. — For readers inside a faith, the parable points to a specific practice. The Daily Office of the Western and Eastern churches — fixed hours of prayer, morning and evening, kept whether you feel like it or not — is a structural answer to this verse. Not because the prayer changes circumstances on demand, but because the showing up itself is the faith Jesus is asking after. Benedictine and Anglican formation know this. So do Jewish daily \it Shacharit\it* and the Muslim five-times-daily \it salah\it*. The traditions are different; the architecture is the same: keep arriving. — The verse has a communal echo. Western Christianity is in the middle of a generational reckoning with church attrition, abuse revelations, and the collapse of denominational certainties. Many readers will know — sometimes painfully — what it is to stay in a tradition through its worst chapter. The parable does not promise quick vindication. It commends a long faithfulness that may look, from the outside, like simple stubbornness. — And there is a civic edge: the widow in the parable is the figure of structural powerlessness petitioning a corrupt court. Faith in this passage is not pious resignation. It is the refusal to stop pressing — for justice, for an answer, for an acknowledgement. Sojourners in Washington, the Catholic Worker houses, Black church organizing traditions have all read this parable as the basic posture of justice work: outlast the indifference. — This week: one practice you used to keep and have stopped — return to it once. Not to resume — just once.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *the Temple to pray*: The Temple prayer context was formalized: daily morning and afternoon offerings were accompanied by communal prayer, and Jews throughout the land aligned their prayers with these moments. To pray in the Temple was to participate in the most sacred locus of Israel's relationship with God. Both men are using recognized, legitimate prayer forms — the Pharisee's thanksgiving (todah) and the tax collector's penitential cry (cf. Psalm 51). The parable's surprise is not about prayer forms but about what lies behind them.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *I fast twice a week*: The Pharisee is doing everything right by conventional standards. He fasts twice a week (Monday and Thursday — the standard piety-fasting days). He tithes on everything, going beyond strict legal requirements. He thanks God — giving credit to God, not claiming it for himself. First hearers would not think of him as boastful; they would see a model of piety. The parable's shock is that this exemplary practice has been corrupted by contempt for others — the one flaw that undoes everything else.
[^10]: **HARM** — *I am not like other people*: This parable has been systematically misread in Christian history as an attack on "Jewish pride" or "works-righteousness Judaism." This is both historically inaccurate and causally connected to anti-Semitic theological teaching. The Pharisee is not "Judaism." He is doing Judaism correctly. Amy-Jill Levine (Short Stories by Jesus, 2014) argues persuasively that the parable attacks the posture of self-comparison and contempt that can corrupt any religious community — including the Christian communities Luke was writing to.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *tax collector*: Tax collectors (\fq τελῶναι\fq*) were local Jews who contracted with the Roman fiscal system to collect taxes, keeping whatever surplus they could extract. They were classified with murderers and robbers in rabbinic literature and disqualified as witnesses in Jewish courts. When this man calls himself "the sinner" (the emphatic article \fq τῷ\fq* makes him THE sinner — not false modesty but accurate self-description), he names what his community already knows. His prayer for mercy is the most radical act in the chapter precisely because he has objective grounds for his self-identification.
[^12]: **KONTEXT** — *God, have mercy on me*: The tax collector's prayer — "God, have mercy on me, the sinner" — is the direct scriptural foundation of the Eastern Orthodox Jesus Prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." The Desert Father Ammonas instructed monks: "Always keep in your heart the words of the Publican." The Philokalia (compiled 1782) is built on this verse. Every time a congregation sings the Kyrie eleison — "Lord, have mercy" — they echo this tax collector and the blind man of 18:38.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *have mercy on me*: The Greek \fq ἱλάσθητί\fq* is priestly atonement language — drawn from the Septuagint vocabulary of the mercy seat (kapporeth) and the Day of Atonement ritual (Leviticus 16). In a Temple prayer setting, this resonance is intentional. The tax collector is not expressing emotional self-deprecation; he is invoking the divine mercy mechanism, the Temple's deepest theological function.
[^14]: **TEXT** — *declared righteous*: The Greek \fq δεδικαιωμένος\fq* is a perfect passive participle — meaning the verdict has been rendered and stands. "Declared righteous" captures both the completed action (God's verdict is given) and the ongoing state (he goes home in this condition). This single Greek form was at the center of the Reformation's most consequential theological debate. The 1999 Joint Declaration on Justification (Lutheran World Federation and Vatican) acknowledged that both forensic justification (God declares the sinner righteous) and transformational justification (God makes the sinner righteous) find common ground in this passage — both affirming that justification originates entirely in God's grace.
[^15]: **LEBEN** — *exalts himself will be humbled*: The parable does not reverse two equally arbitrary outcomes. The Pharisee's practice was real — the fasting, the tithing, the discipline. None of it was fake. What corrupted him was not what he did but what he did with it: he used his virtue as a measuring stick against the tax collector standing nearby. The text does not condemn the practice. It condemns the comparison. — A question that lands without theological warmup: think back across the last week and notice when you were most aware of someone else's failing. Not a passing observation — the kind of awareness that made you feel a little taller. The colleague whose work is sloppier than yours. The parent at the school gate who clearly has less of it together. The sibling whose life is more chaotic. The point is not that the observation is wrong. The point is what it does inside. Comparison is the engine. The Pharisee's prayer is not a religious problem; it is the universal human reflex of using someone else's smaller standing to confirm your own. — A low-threshold practice this week: when you catch yourself in the comparison move — the small lift that comes from someone else's diminishment — name it without judgment. Just notice. The reflex is automatic; the awareness is not. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is long. Augustine's Confessions traces the entire spiritual journey as the slow undoing of comparative pride. The Desert Fathers warned that the demon defeated by sin returns dressed as virtue — and the second visit is harder to dislodge. Thérèse of Lisieux's "little way" is the deliberate inverse: a spirituality that refuses every metric of superiority. — The parable's twist is not that the tax collector earned justification by being humble; it is that justification arrived precisely where comparison stopped. — This week: catch one comparison reflex and let it pass without feeding it.
[^16]: **KULTUR** — *their infants*: Luke uses \fq βρέφη\fq* (infants — pre-verbal, must be carried) rather than the more general "children." In the ancient world, children had no legal standing — they were not legal persons, could not own property, testify, or participate in civic life. Bringing infants to a rabbi was, in the disciples' view, wasting his most valuable resource: time and attention. Their rebuke was socially reasonable. Jesus's response overrules it.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *rebuked*: The Greek \fq ἐπετίμων\fq* is the same verb used for the crowd silencing the blind man in 18:39. Luke creates a structural pattern: social gatekeeping — controlling who has access to Jesus — repeatedly overridden by Jesus himself.
[^18]: **TEXT** — *like a child*: "Like a child" does not primarily mean with innocence, trust, or simplicity — that is the sentimental Western reading. It means: like one who has no claim, no standing, no leverage, no ability to negotiate terms. A child cannot earn the kingdom, demand the kingdom, or qualify for the kingdom. A child can only receive what is given. The emphatic double negative in Greek (\fq οὐ μή\fq*) means "will absolutely never enter."
[^19]: **TEXT** — *a ruler*: Luke identifies this man as an \fq ἄρχων\fq* — a ruler or leader with governing authority. He is not "a young man" (that is Matthew's word, \fq νεανίσκος\fq*, in Matthew 19:20). The common designation "rich young ruler" fuses all three Synoptic accounts. Luke's choice intensifies the encounter: this is an established leader, not a youthful idealist. His question is sincere, and his compliance with the commandments is plausible — most well-raised Jewish people could genuinely claim this (Keener).
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *Sell everything you have*: Judaism stressed charity heavily, but no other Jewish teacher required a prospective disciple to divest himself entirely of funds (Keener). This demand is specific and extreme. The command has three parts: (1) sell everything, (2) distribute to the poor, (3) come, follow me. The third is the key — this is a discipleship call, not merely an ethical demand. Following Jesus requires releasing what holds you.
[^21]: **LEBEN** — *a deep sadness*: The Greek \fq περίλυπος\fq* — deeply grieved, the same word Jesus uses of himself in Gethsemane — does serious work here. The rich ruler is not a villain refusing to do good. He is a man who genuinely wants eternal life and who finds, when the question comes specific, that he cannot release what holds him. Luke does not give us his name. He gives us the look on his face. The passage does not condemn him; it grieves with him. — A question that lands without theological warmup: what is the one thing in your life that, if asked to release it, would leave you περίλυπος — not annoyed, not inconvenienced, but deeply grieved? The honest answer is rarely the obvious one. For some it is money, but more often it is what money stands in for: a sense of control, a self-image as competent, a future already half-built. For others it is reputation, a particular relationship, an identity as the responsible one, the good one, the indispensable one. The passage is not moralizing; it is diagnostic. It asks where you find yourself when the call gets specific. — A low-threshold practice this week: identify the one item in your life that you assume is non-negotiable and ask honestly whether it owns you or you own it. Not to give it away — just to test the grip. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is long. The Cloud of Unknowing (14th century) speaks of a "naked intent" toward God that must release every attachment, however good. Meister Eckhart called this Gelassenheit — a letting-be that is neither apathy nor abandonment, but the opening of the hand. Ignatius named the same posture indifference. — Pastorally, the verse is gentle. The text does not say the ruler walked away from salvation forever. It says he walked away sad. Sadness is data. Sometimes it is the first move in a longer return. — This week: name your περίλυπος. Sit with it long enough to learn what it is teaching.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *camel... eye of a needle*: The earliest and most reliable manuscripts (P75, Codex Vaticanus, Codex Sinaiticus) all read "camel" (\fq κάμηλον\fq*). The variant "rope" (\fq κάμιλον\fq*) appears only in late Byzantine manuscripts and is a scribal attempt to soften the hyperbole. The "needle's eye gate in Jerusalem" theory — that Jesus referred to a small gate requiring camels to kneel — has no ancient support; the alleged gate was built in medieval times (Keener). The image is meant to be impossible. The disciples understand it that way (18:26), and Jesus confirms it (18:27).
[^23]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *camel through the eye of a needle*: Does Jesus really mean wealth makes salvation impossible? Three perspectives: (1) The social critic reads this as structural analysis — wealth creates dependencies, obligations, and identities that are structurally incompatible with radical kingdom dependence. (2) The theological reader notes that Jesus does not leave it at "impossible" — he immediately adds "what is impossible for people is possible for God" (18:27). Salvation of the wealthy is not difficult but structurally impossible by human effort; it requires divine miracle. (3) The prosperity gospel reading, which treats wealth as a sign of divine blessing, directly contradicts this passage. Luke's consistent wealth theology (6:24; 12:15–21; 16:13, 19–31) treats wealth as structural danger, not divine reward.
[^24]: **KONTEXT** — *far more in this present life*: The "far more in this present time" points to the community of disciples sharing possessions — realized in Acts 2:44–45, where the early church held everything in common. The promise is both present (community provision) and eschatological (eternal life). Peter's sacrifice of "everything" is acknowledged and affirmed, but the text's logic makes clear: even the disciples' sacrifice is only possible because God enables it.
[^25]: **KONTEXT** — *Everything written by the prophets*: This is the third and most detailed of Luke's passion predictions (cf. 9:22; 9:44). "Everything written by the prophets" points to Isaiah 53 (the Servant's suffering), Psalm 22 (mockery and humiliation), and Psalm 31 (trust in God's vindication). Luke's passion theology is explicitly scriptural: the suffering and death are not accidents of history but the fulfillment of prophetic testimony. Luke 24:25–27 will make this connection fully explicit.
[^26]: **TEXT** — *the Gentiles*: Luke explicitly names Gentile (Roman) agency in the passion — a detail absent from the earlier predictions. This is historically and theologically significant: the crucifixion was a Roman execution, carried out by Gentile authorities. Any reading that places sole responsibility on "the Jews" contradicts Luke's own text.
[^27]: **TEXT** — *hidden from them*: The Greek \fq κεκρυμμένον\fq* is a perfect passive participle — the hiddenness is a state that has been imposed and persists. This is not mere intellectual slowness; it is divinely imposed incomprehension that will be lifted only after the resurrection, when Jesus "opened their minds to understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:45). The triple statement of incomprehension — they understood nothing, the meaning was hidden, they could not grasp — emphasizes that the failure is total and structural.
[^28]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *understood none of it*: Why couldn't the disciples understand plain speech about death and resurrection? Three perspectives: (1) The psychological reader notes that people routinely fail to process information that contradicts their deepest expectations — the disciples expected a triumphant Messiah, not a crucified one. (2) The theological reader sees divine purpose: understanding must await the event itself and the Spirit's illumination. (3) The literary reader recognizes dramatic irony: Luke's post-Easter audience understands what the characters cannot, creating the aching tension of watching people walk toward a catastrophe they cannot see.
[^29]: **KULTUR** — *a blind man sat beside the road*: Blindness in the ancient world typically resulted in permanent economic dependency. Begging was not an embarrassment but the primary means of survival. Jericho was a prosperous administrative center with significant pilgrim traffic (Jerusalem being 27 km away), making the roadside a viable begging position. The blind man's "faith" (18:42) involves a concrete risk: leaving this position of economic security to follow a traveling teacher.
[^30]: **TEXT** — *Son of David*: "Son of David" is not a polite address — it is a Messianic confession. In Jewish prophetic expectation, the Davidic Messiah would heal the blind (Isaiah 29:18; 35:5; 61:1). The blind man is not merely shouting for help; he is declaring Jesus's Messianic identity. His "faith" (18:42) is inseparable from this theological recognition. That he makes this confession while blind — while those who can see remain silent — is Luke's sharpest irony.
[^31]: **TEXT** — *has saved you*: The Greek \fq σέσωκέν σε\fq* (perfect tense of \fq σῴζω\fq*) means "has saved you" — not merely "has healed you." The same formula appears in Luke 7:50 (the forgiven woman), 8:48 (the hemorrhaging woman), and 17:19 (the Samaritan leper). In every case, \fq σῴζω\fq* encompasses both physical restoration and salvific rescue. The translation "has healed you" (common in many English versions) severs what Luke deliberately holds together. That the man immediately follows Jesus (18:43) — discipleship language — confirms that something far more than a medical procedure has occurred.
[^32]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Receive your sight*: Can a word restore a blind man's sight? Three perspectives: (1) The naturalist reads this as literary construction — a symbolic healing that communicates a theological point about spiritual blindness. (2) The medical reader notes that some ancient "blindness" was treatable (cataracts, trachoma), but the text presents an instantaneous, complete restoration triggered by a verbal command, not a medical intervention. (3) The faith reader takes the narrative at face value: Jesus heals because he is who the blind man declares him to be — the Son of David, the Messianic healer prophesied in Isaiah 35:5 ("Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened"). Luke records multiple healings with the same structure — a cry for mercy, Jesus's response, immediate restoration (7:21; 13:13; 17:14). The pattern is consistent: faith recognizes what sight cannot see, and Jesus responds to that recognition with restoration.
[^33]: **KONTEXT** — *Your faith has saved you*: This scene answers the question posed at the chapter's opening: "When the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?" (18:8). Yes — here, in this blind beggar, crying out "Son of David, have mercy" despite the crowd's silencing. The disciples failed to understand (18:34). The rich ruler walked away (18:23). But this man, who cannot even see Jesus, recognizes him, cries for mercy, and follows. He is the faith the Son of Man finds.
[^34]: **TEXT** — *followed*: The Greek \fq ἠκολούθει\fq* is imperfect tense — he kept following, continuously. This is discipleship language (cf. 5:27–28; 9:23), not momentary gratitude. He joins those walking toward Jerusalem — the city where everything written by the prophets about the Son of Man will be fulfilled (18:31). His following is the chapter's final image: a man who was blind, now seeing, walking with Jesus toward the cross.

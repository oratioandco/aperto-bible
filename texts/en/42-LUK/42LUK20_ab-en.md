## Kapitel 20


### The Question about Jesus' Authority


**1** He had been teaching in the temple courts every day that week, and every day the crowds grew. He was not just teaching — he was proclaiming*, and the people pressed close to hear it*.

**2** Then the delegation arrived. *Chief priests. Legal scholars. Elders. Together, they formed the highest authority in the land, and they came with a single demand.*

[^1]

**3** "I have a question for you first," Jesus said. "John's baptism. Was it from heaven, or from a human source?"
[^2]

**4** They pulled aside*. But this was not a theological discussion. It was arithmetic.*
**5** If they acknowledged John's authority as divine, Jesus would ask why they had refused to follow him. If they dismissed it as merely human, the crowd — who still believed John was a prophet*, who still mourned him* — would stone them.
[^3]
[^4]

**7** "We can't say," they answered.

**8** "Then I can't say either," said Jesus.

### Parable of the Wicked Tenants


**9** Then he turned from the leaders and spoke directly to the crowd.

[^5]
[^6]

**10** "They beat the servant. Sent him back with nothing.

**11** "He sent a second servant. They beat him too, shamed him, and sent him back empty-handed.

**12** "A third. They wounded him and threw him out.

**13** "The owner thought, 'What can I do now?' And then: 'I will send the son I love. Perhaps they will respect him.'
[^7]
[^8]

**14** "But the tenants recognized the son. They saw the heir, and they saw their chance. 'If we kill him,' they said, 'the estate becomes ours.'
**15** They dragged him outside the vineyard. And killed him.

**16** "So what will the owner of the vineyard do? He will come back. He will destroy those tenants. And he will give the vineyard to others."
[^9]

[^10]

**17** Jesus looked straight at them and said, "Then what is this that was written — \qt The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone?"
[^11]

**18** He let that sit. Then: "Everyone who falls on that stone will be shattered. And when that stone falls on someone — it will crush them to dust."
[^12]

**19** The chief priests and the legal scholars wanted to arrest him on the spot. They knew exactly who the parable was about. But they were afraid of the crowd.

### Taxes to Caesar


**20** After that, they watched him and waited. Then they sent in spies — men pretending to be sincere — with a single purpose: to catch Jesus in a statement that would justify handing him over to the Roman governor.
[^13]

**21** The spies approached*with flattery*. "Teacher, we know you speak and teach what is right. You don't defer to anyone. You teach the way of God honestly. So — is it lawful for us to pay the tax to Caesar, or not?"
[^14]

**22** Jesus saw through them immediately.
**23** "Show me a denarius."

[^15]

**24** "Whose image is this? And whose inscription?"
[^16]

**25** "Caesar's."

[^17]
[^18]
[^19]

**26** They fell silent. *They couldn't find a single word to use against him.* They just stood there, amazed.

### The Question about the Resurrection


**27** Then the Sadducees came forward*— the priestly aristocracy who denied that the dead would ever rise*. *If God's justice was exhausted in the present arrangement, then the present arrangement could not be challenged, and the families who profited from it were safe.* They had a question for him.
[^20]

**28** "Teacher," they said, "Moses wrote that if a man dies without children, his brother must marry the widow and raise up offspring for the dead man.
[^21]
**29** Now there were seven brothers. The first married a woman and died childless. The second married her. Then the third. One after another, all seven married her and died without children.
**30** Finally the woman herself died.
**31** So — in the resurrection — whose wife does she become? All seven had her."

**32** *Seven marriages. Seven brothers. And at the center of their puzzle, one woman — unnamed, unasked, passed from household to household like a deed of land.*

**33** Jesus said, "People of this age marry and are given in marriage.
**34** But those counted worthy to share in that age and in the resurrection from the dead neither marry nor are given in marriage.
**35** They cannot die anymore. They are like the angels. They are God's children — because they share in the resurrection."
[^22]
[^23]

**36** *He paused.* Then he went further — *straight to Moses, the only authority they accepted.*
[^24]

**37** "Even Moses showed this, at the burning bush, when he calls the Lord 'the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.'
**38** He does not say 'I was their God.' He says 'I am.' God is not God of the dead. He is God of the living. To God, all are alive."
[^25]
[^26]

**39** *Silence.*


**40** After that, no one dared ask him anything.

### The Question about David's Son


**41** Then Jesus put a question to them.

[^27]
**42** "David himself wrote in the Book of Psalms:
> 'The LORD said to my Lord:
> Sit at my right hand
> until I make your enemies
> a footstool for your feet.'
[^28]
[^29]

**43** "David calls him 'Lord.' So how can the Messiah be his son?"

**44** *Everyone agreed the Messiah would come from David's line — a son. But in their world, a father did not call his own son "Lord." Yet that is what David did.*



### Warning against the Scribes


**45** Then Jesus turned to his disciples*— though he made no effort to lower his voice*.

**46** "Watch out for the legal scholars," he said. "The ones who love their long robes, who love being greeted with deference in the marketplace, who claim the best seats in the synagogue and the best places at every banquet."


**47** "They devour widows' estates, and they do it under cover of long prayers."
[^30]
[^31]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **TEXT** — *authority (ἐξουσία)*: The leaders ask two distinct questions: what KIND of authority (institutional? prophetic? divine?) and WHO authorized it. In the ancient world, legitimate authority required institutional delegation — ordination, appointment, lineage. Jesus operated entirely outside these channels. His authority claim was prophetic and messianic, categories the priestly aristocracy had every institutional reason to resist.
[^2]: **KULTUR** — *a question for you first*: Jesus' counter-question was a recognized rabbinic technique — meeting a challenge with a counter-question that exposes the questioner's position. This was not evasion. It was an argument: if you cannot evaluate the evident credentials of John — a man whose ministry was public, whose fruits were obvious — you have demonstrated that you are not asking mine in good faith.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *they calculated (συνελογίσαντο)*: Luke's unique word — a hapax legomenon — reveals political arithmetic, not theological inquiry. The leaders are not seeking truth; they are managing outcomes. Each option is weighed for its political consequence, not its truthfulness.
[^4]: **TEXT** — *stone us (καταλιθάσει)*: Luke alone sharpens the crowd's potential response to stoning — a lynching threat. Matthew and Mark say only "we fear the crowd." Luke names the physical danger, raising the stakes of the leaders' cowardice.
[^5]: **KONTEXT** — *a vineyard*: The moment Jesus says "vineyard," any biblically literate listener hears Isaiah 5:1-7 — the Song of the Vineyard, Israel's most famous judgment poem: "The vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel." But Jesus shifts the allegory. In Isaiah, the vineyard itself is unfaithful. In Jesus' parable, the vineyard is not the problem — the tenants are. The critique targets specific stewards of God's trust, not the people as a whole.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *tenant farmers (γεωργοί)*: Not a fantasy scenario. Herodian land concentration had created a class of absentee landlords extracting from peasant tenant farmers across Palestine. Rent strikes and violence against owners' agents were real. The parable's economic realism grounds its theological freight: the listeners knew this world.
[^7]: **TEXT** — *the son I love (τὸν υἱόν μου τὸν ἀγαπητόν)*: The word \fq ἀγαπητόν\fq* ("beloved") is the same word used at Jesus' baptism: "You are my beloved Son" (Luke 3:22). The christological identification is embedded in the vocabulary. The owner's vulnerable hope — "perhaps they will respect him" — is presented as almost foolishly generous, the extravagance of divine patience extending beyond rational expectation.
[^8]: **LEBEN** — *perhaps they will respect him*: The word \fq ἴσως\fq* — "perhaps" — is the heart of the parable. The owner has every reason for force. Three servants beaten, wounded, thrown out. The rational, even responsible response is escalation. Instead he sends the one person he cannot afford to lose, on the strength of a "perhaps." This is not the calculation of a strategist. It is the logic of love operating after every reasonable hope has been exhausted. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where in your life are you tempted to harden because the soft response has not worked yet? The relative who keeps hurting people in the same way each time. The colleague whose pattern has not shifted across years. The friend whose addiction has burned through every careful intervention. The verse does not say the soft response is always right. It names what it costs to keep trying. The owner sends his son knowing he may lose him. He is not naive; he is choosing. — A low-threshold practice this week: one relationship where you have begun to harden — not because hardening is wrong, but to test what it is doing in you — speak one sentence that you would have spoken six months ago, before the wall went up. Just one. Watch what moves. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage runs through every story of God refusing force as the final word. The book of Hosea is the same scandal: a prophet told to love a person who keeps leaving, because God will not abandon a covenant the people have already abandoned. Bonhoeffer at Tegel wrote letters to a fiancée and a Germany that could not hear them. Etty Hillesum kept writing about love in a camp designed to destroy the people who could practice it. — The parable does not promise the soft response will be received. It tells the truth about what kind of God sends a son anyway. — This week: notice one place where your hardening serves you, and one where it has begun to cost more than the wound it was meant to protect.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *give the vineyard to others*: CRITICAL: "Others" (\fq ἄλλοις\fq*) is deliberately unidentified in Luke's text. It does NOT mean "the Gentiles" or "the church." The vineyard (God's covenant community) is not destroyed — only the unfaithful tenants are replaced. The 2,000-year history of reading this parable as God's rejection of the Jewish people in favor of the Gentile church is a hermeneutical disaster: Luke 20:19 explicitly identifies the parable's targets as the chief priests and scribes — the Jerusalem leadership, not the Jewish people.
[^10]: **TEXT** — *God forbid! (Μὴ γένοιτο)*: The strongest possible rejection in Greek — an idiom characteristic of Paul (Romans 3:4, 6, 31; Galatians 2:17) appearing uniquely here in Gospel narrative. The crowd hears the parable's implication — judgment on them — and recoils with visceral horror. This is not a polite objection. It is a cry of refusal.
[^11]: **KONTEXT** — *Psalm 118:22*: The crowd had just sung this very psalm at Jesus' triumphal entry days earlier (Luke 19:38 quotes Psalm 118:26). Now Jesus turns their own song back on them: the stone the builders rejected — after inspection, after deliberate evaluation — has become the structural foundation of the entire building. The reversal is architectural: from waste to cornerstone.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *shattered ... crushed to dust*: Two distinct fates using two different Greek verbs. The first (\fq συνθλασθήσεται\fq*, "shattered") describes stumbling against the stone — resistance that breaks the resistor. The second (\fq λικμήσει\fq*, "winnowed/scattered like chaff") draws on Daniel 2:34 — the divine stone that pulverizes the statue of empires. Two degrees of judgment: those who oppose the stone are broken; those whom the stone actively falls upon are annihilated.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *spies (ἐγκαθέτους)*: A New Testament hapax — "people planted secretly in an audience." Luke uniquely names this as a covert entrapment operation, not sincere inquiry. The spies' mandate was explicitly legal: to catch a statement that could be reported to the Roman governor (\fq ἡγεμών\fq*) as sedition.
[^14]: **KULTUR** — *the tax (φόρον)*: Specifically the Roman poll tax (tributum capitis) — a flat per-person levy on all provincial subjects as a mark of subjugation. Judas the Galilean had launched a violent revolt over this tax a generation earlier (6 CE; Acts 5:37). Asking "is it lawful to pay?" was not theology — it was a politically lethal binary. Say yes: you validate Roman occupation, lose the crowd. Say no: you commit sedition, Rome arrests you.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *a denarius (δηνάριον)*: The Tiberian denarius bore the inscription Ti. Caesar Divi Aug. F. Augustus ("Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus") with the emperor's portrait, and on the reverse Pontif. Maxim. ("chief priest") with a seated figure. The coin made three theological claims — Caesar was emperor, son of a god, and supreme priest. Carrying it into the temple precincts was itself theologically fraught. Jesus did not carry one. The spies produced one from their own pockets — a self-indictment.
[^16]: **TEXT** — *image (εἰκών)*: THE KEY WORD. The Greek \fq εἰκών\fq* is the same word used in the Septuagint of Genesis 1:26-27: \fq κατ' εἰκόνα θεοῦ\fq* — "in the image of God." The coin bears Caesar's image; give it back. Human beings bear God's image; give yourself back. As Tertullian wrote around 200 CE: "Render the image of Caesar, which is the coin, to Caesar; and the image of God, which is the human person, to God." Caesar gets a coin. God gets everything that bears God's image.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *give back (ἀπόδοτε)*: CRITICAL DECISION: Not "give" (neutral transfer) or "render" (KJV archaism). The Greek \fq ἀποδίδωμι\fq* means "return to the rightful owner." The coin belongs to Caesar — give it back. What bears God's image belongs to God — give that back. The asymmetry is staggering: Caesar's domain is a metal coin; God's domain is every human being.
[^18]: **LEBEN** — *give back to God what belongs to God*: The asymmetry is the entire argument. Caesar's domain is the metal he stamped his face onto — a coin, a denarius, a unit of taxation. God's domain is every human being who bears God's image, which is to say: every human being. The text does not partition life into a sacred zone (church, prayer, religion) and a secular zone (work, money, politics) where different rules apply. It does the opposite. The secular zone shrinks to a coin. Everything else is already God's. — A question that lands without theological warmup: which parts of your life have you implicitly classified as "mine to manage, religion not relevant here"? The financial decisions you make alone. The way you talk to people who report to you. The ambitions you do not say out loud even to your closest friends. The rest you take or do not take. The verse is not asking for more religious activity. It is asking whether the parts of life you guard as private are quietly being shaped by the wrong measure. — A low-threshold practice this week: pick one compartment you have been treating as outside the question — work hours, money, leisure, ambition — and just notice for seven days what would change if the question "what does it mean that this belongs to God?" were sitting open in the room. No conclusions. Just the question, present. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage runs through the Hebrew prophets and into the early church's refusal to burn incense to the emperor. The denarius bore Tiberius's image and the inscription "son of the divine Augustus." Carrying it was already complicated. The Anabaptists in the sixteenth century, Bonhoeffer in the twentieth, Romero in the eighties — every figure who refused to let civic loyalty consume the deeper loyalty is reading this verse. — The instruction is not "give Caesar his due and God his." It is: notice which is the coin and which is the image. — This week: name one compartment, and let the question sit there.
[^19]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *give back to Caesar*: Does this text support paying taxes to oppressive regimes? Three readings: (1) The political quietism reading — "Two legitimate spheres; pay your taxes" — was used to justify compliance with Nazi Germany (by the Deutsche Christen), apartheid South Africa (by white Dutch Reformed theology), and colonial extraction globally. The 1985 South African Kairos Document explicitly named this as heresy. (2) The subversive reading (N.T. Wright, Warren Carter, Richard Horsley): if you give God everything that bears God's image, Caesar gets very little — just the coins. This is a restriction on imperial claims, not an endorsement. (3) The practical reading: Jesus refuses the binary (collaborate or revolt) and offers a third way — a statement about ultimate belonging that neither validates nor incites. The text does not settle church-state relations. It exposes a trap and points to a claim that dwarfs any empire.
[^20]: **KULTUR** — *Sadducees (Σαδδουκαῖοι)*: The party of the priestly aristocracy — high-priestly families, wealthy merchants, and landowners who collaborated with Rome. They accepted only the Torah (Pentateuch) as authoritative Scripture and rejected the oral tradition. As N.T. Wright argues, their denial of resurrection was politically functional: people who believe God will raise the dead and vindicate the faithful are far more dangerous to a political establishment than those who believe the present order is final.
[^21]: **KULTUR** — *brother must marry the widow*: The levirate obligation (from Latin levir, "husband's brother") — Deuteronomy 25:5-10 required a surviving brother to marry his deceased brother's childless widow, preserving the dead man's lineage and keeping property within the family. This was compulsory, not voluntary. The Mishnah tractate Yevamot devotes extensive discussion to its application. The institution treated women as instruments of male lineage preservation.
[^22]: **TEXT** — *like the angels (ἰσάγγελοι)*: A New Testament hapax: "equal to angels" — describing participation in deathless divine existence, not disembodiment or celibacy. The point is about mortality: in the resurrection age, death no longer occurs, so the lineage-preservation function of marriage is moot. Women are no longer "given in marriage" (passive voice: transferred as property). The passage is a resurrection text, not a sexuality text — the patristic reading that elevated present-age celibacy from this verse misapplies it.
[^23]: **LEBEN** — *neither marry nor are given in marriage*: The Sadducees built a trick question on a real institution. Levirate marriage (Deut 25) required a man's brother to marry his widow to produce an heir in the dead man's name — a system designed to protect bloodlines and property, with the woman as the carrier. In their puzzle she is passed through seven brothers without a name, a preference, a refusal. Jesus does not solve the puzzle on its terms. He removes the terms. In the resurrection age, the passive voice — "given in marriage" — vanishes. No one is transferred. No one is property. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where in your own world are people treated as means to someone else's ends? The intern whose only function is to absorb the workload no one else wants. The aging parent whose preferences keep being overridden "for their own good." The partner whose career consistently flexes around the other's. The point is not that all such arrangements are exploitative. The point is that the resurrection horizon Jesus opens up reads every present arrangement against a future in which no one is given. — A low-threshold practice this week: notice one person in your immediate circle who has, without meaning to, become more function than person to you — and spend one ten-minute conversation asking them what they want, with no agenda of your own. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage runs through every refusal to let religion baptize property arrangements as sacred. Paul's instruction that there is "neither male nor female" in Christ (Gal 3:28) is the same vision. The medieval women's movement — Hildegard, the Beguines, Catherine of Siena — claimed it against the marriage market of their century. Sojourner Truth's "Ain't I a Woman?" speech is the verse spoken into the slave economy. — Jesus' answer is not "there's no marriage in heaven." It is: in the world that is coming, no one will ever be given. — This week: one conversation in which the other person is the whole point.
[^24]: **KONTEXT** — *Moses showed this, at the bush*: Jesus defeats the Sadducees on their own canonical territory. He does not appeal to Isaiah or Daniel, where resurrection hope is more explicit. He finds resurrection implied in the Pentateuch — the Torah, the only Scripture the Sadducees accepted. The argument from Exodus 3:6 depends on God's present-tense self-identification: "I AM the God of Abraham" — not "I was." If God currently identifies with the patriarchs, they must currently exist. God's ongoing relationship with them implies their ongoing life.
[^25]: **TEXT** — *to God, all are alive (πάντες γὰρ αὐτῷ ζῶσιν)*: The dative \fq αὐτῷ\fq* is not "from God's perspective, everyone looks alive." It is a dative of relation: all live in relationship to God. The patriarchs' life consists in their ongoing relational existence with God. Resurrection is not resuscitation — returning a dead body to biological function — but participation in the life of the living God. This is the deepest New Testament statement about the nature of resurrection.
[^26]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *to God, all are alive*: Resurrection is among the most difficult claims for modern readers. Three frameworks: (1) The materialist view holds that consciousness ends at death — resurrection is wishful thinking. (2) The symbolic reading sees resurrection as a metaphor for renewed purpose or social transformation, not literal survival. (3) The relational reading (N.T. Wright, Wolfhart Pannenberg) argues that resurrection is grounded not in human capacity for survival but in God's faithfulness — if God's covenant relationship with Abraham is real, it cannot be annulled by death, because God is not diminished by death. Jesus' argument here does not appeal to philosophy or near-death experiences. It appeals to the character of God: a God who says "I am" — not "I was" — the God of Abraham. The claim is that death does not have the final word because God does.
[^27]: **TEXT** — *the Messiah (τὸν χριστόν)*: "Christ" has become a surname to modern ears — "Jesus Christ" sounds like a first and last name. But \fq χριστός\fq* is a title meaning "the anointed one" (Greek translation of Hebrew mashiach). The popular expectation was specific: a Davidic warrior-king who would drive out Rome and restore Israel's sovereignty. Jesus does not deny Davidic descent — Luke's genealogy (3:23-38) affirms it. He complicates it: the Messiah is David's son AND David's Lord.
[^28]: **KONTEXT** — *Psalm 110:1*: The most quoted Old Testament verse in the New Testament — cited or alluded to over twenty times (Acts 2:34-35; Romans 8:34; 1 Corinthians 15:25; Ephesians 1:20; Colossians 3:1; Hebrews 1:3, 13; 8:1; 10:12-13; 12:2). The early church's use of this psalm to articulate Jesus' identity — exalted to God's right hand, ruling until all opposition is subdued — became the foundational christological proof text. Jesus' riddle here plants the seed that the entire apostolic movement would cultivate.
[^29]: **TEXT** — *The LORD said to my Lord*: Psalm 110:1 — the double use of "Lord" is the riddle. The first "LORD" (\fq κύριος\fq* without article) represents YHWH, the divine name. The second "my Lord" (\fq τῷ κυρίῳ μου\fq*) is the one David addresses — whom the early church identified as the Messiah. In the ancient honor system, an ancestor is always superior to a descendant. David calling his own offspring "Lord" violates the deepest social hierarchy. The paradox is deliberate: the Messiah must be both human (from David's line) and something more (worthy of David's worship).
[^30]: **KULTUR** — *devour widows' estates (κατεσθίουσιν τὰς οἰκίας τῶν χηρῶν)*: Not generic greed — a specific mechanism of structural exploitation. The scribal class served as legal interpreters, estate trustees, and inheritance administrators. Widows, lacking direct access to legal protection and property rights, depended on exactly these men. The authority that made scribes essential to widows was the same authority that gave them access to widows' wealth. The Greek verb \fq κατεσθίω\fq* (devour, eat up entirely) names consumption, not theft — a slower, more devastating process.
[^31]: **TEXT** — *under cover of long prayers (προφάσει μακρὰ προσεύχονται)*: The key word is \fq πρόφασις\fq* — "pretext, cover." The long prayers are not a separate charge alongside the exploitation. They ARE the mechanism: visible piety that makes invisible extraction unchallengeable. Religion as economic cover — this is the prophetic tradition's oldest and deepest complaint (cf. Isaiah 1:10-17; Amos 5:21-24; Micah 6:6-8).
[^32]: **LEBEN** — *the heaviest sentence (περισσότερον κρίμα)*: The comparative \fq περισσότερον\fq* — "more, heavier" — does work many religious readers would rather skip. The sentence is heavier not in spite of the long prayers but because of them. Piety, in this verse, is not a mitigating circumstance. It is the aggravating one. Religious cover turns ordinary harm into something worse: it adds the violation of trust to the original wound, and it teaches the harmed to mistake the harm for the will of God. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where have you watched religious or institutional authority provide cover for harm? Not in the abstract — the specific instance. The pastor whose tone with his wife shifted at the front door. The teacher whose discipline was always heavier on certain children. The mentor whose generosity came with a price you did not understand until later. The verse is uncomfortable because it asks where the long prayers were happening over the houses. — A low-threshold practice this week: one place where you carry confused gratitude — a relationship that gave you something and also cost something it had no right to cost — name both halves. Not to demolish what was good; to stop letting the good launder the harm. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage runs through every prophet who criticized his own house. Isaiah on the temple liturgy (1:13 — "I cannot endure your solemn assemblies"). Amos on the festivals (5:21). Jeremiah on the prayer that wallpapers over injustice (7:9-11, the original "den of robbers" Jesus would later quote in this same temple). The fiercest criticisms of Christian institutions in the last fifty years — the abuse audits, the truth-and-reconciliation processes, the survivor networks — are this verse becoming public record. — Jesus speaks here as a faithful insider. The criticism is not anti-religious. It is what religion sounds like when it is told the truth. — This week: name one good thing and one bad thing about a place that gave you both, and refuse to collapse them into a single feeling.

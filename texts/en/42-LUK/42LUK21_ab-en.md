## Kapitel 21


### The Widow's Offering


**1** He looked up. *Wealthy donors were filing past the collection chests, dropping in their gifts — the kind that rang against the metal.*
**2** Then he saw a widow. *Destitute.* She dropped in two tiny copper coins.
[^1]

**3** "Listen," he said *to his disciples*. "This woman put in more than all of them combined.
**4** They all gave out of their overflow*— what they had left over*. She gave out of *what she lacked —*everything she had to live on."
[^2]
[^3]
[^4]
[^5]

### Temple Destruction Foretold


**5** Some people were admiring the Temple — *the fine stones, the gifts that generations had dedicated to God*. He said, "These things you're gazing at?
**6** Days are coming when not one stone will be left on another. All of it — torn down."
[^6]
[^7]

**7** "Teacher," they asked, "when? And what will the sign be?"

**8** "Watch that no one leads you astray," he said. "Many will come in my name claiming, 'I am the one!' and 'The time has come!' Do not follow them.
[^8]
**9** And when you hear of wars and revolts — do not be terrified. All that has to happen first. But it's not the end."
[^9]

### Signs, Persecution, and Testimony


**10** Then he said to them, "Nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom.
**11** There will be earthquakes — famines in one place, plagues in another — terrors, and great signs blazing from the sky.

**12** But before any of this — they will seize you and persecute you, handing you over to synagogues and prisons, dragging you before kings and governors because of my name.
**13** This will turn into your chance to testify.
**14** So settle it in your hearts: do not rehearse your defense ahead of time.
**15** I myself will give you words and wisdom that none of your opponents will be able to resist or refute.
[^10]

**16** You will be handed over even by your own parents, your brothers, your relatives, your friends. They will put some of you to death.
**17** Everyone will hate you because of my name.

**18** And yet — not a hair of your head will be lost.

**19** By your endurance you will win yourselves back."
[^11]
[^12]

### Jerusalem Surrounded


**20** "When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, know this: her devastation has drawn near.
[^13]
**21** Then let those in Judea flee to the mountains. Those inside the city — get out. Those in the countryside — do not go in.
**22** Because these are days of judgment — *fulfilling everything written by the prophets*.
[^14]

**23** And the pregnant women — the mothers still nursing — God help them in those days. There will be terrible distress across the land, and wrath against this people.
**24** They will fall by the edge of the sword. They will be dragged off as prisoners to every nation. And Jerusalem will be trampled by the nations — until the times of the Gentiles have run their course."
[^15]
[^16]

### The Coming of the Son of Man


**25** "There will be signs — in the sun, the moon, the stars. On earth, nations in anguish, bewildered by the roaring and surging of the sea.
[^17]
**26** People collapsing with dread at what is bearing down on the world — the very powers of the heavens shaken loose.
[^18]

**27** And then they will see the Son of Man coming in a cloud, with power and great glory.
[^19]

**28** When these things begin to happen — stand tall. Lift your heads. Your liberation is drawing near."
[^20]

### The Parable of the Fig Tree


**29** *He told them a parable:* "Look at the fig tree — *any tree*.
**30** When fresh leaves appear, you know without being told: summer is coming.
**31** In the same way, when you see these things taking shape, know this — the kingdom of God is close.

**32** Truly I tell you: this generation will not pass away until all of it has taken place.
[^21]
[^22]

**33** Heaven and earth will pass away. But my words — never."
[^23]

### Watchfulness


**34** "Guard your hearts. Don't let them get heavy with overindulgence and drunkenness and the anxieties of ordinary life —
**35** or that day will close on you like a trap*, every last person on earth*.
[^24]

**36** Stay alert. Pray at every turn — for the strength to escape what is coming, and to stand before the Son of Man."
[^25]

**37** *During the days,* he taught in the Temple. At night he went out and stayed on the hill called the Mount of Olives.
**38** And *the people — all of them —* would rise early every morning and come to the Temple to listen.

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *two tiny copper coins*: Two \fq λεπτά\fq* (lepta) — the smallest coins in circulation, each worth 1/128 of a denarius. Together they equaled one quadrans: not enough to buy a single loaf of bread. The treasury was in the Court of Women, where thirteen trumpet-shaped (shofar) collection chests amplified the sound of coins falling in. The wealthy made the treasury ring; her two coins barely registered.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *everything she had to live on*: The Greek \fq βίος\fq* means "livelihood" or "survival budget" — not vague "possessions." This was her next meal. The word choice makes the stakes concrete: she gave away her physical survival, not an abstract portion of wealth.
[^3]: **KONTEXT** — *devour widows' houses*: This scene follows directly after 20:45-47, where Jesus condemns the scribes who "devour widows' houses." The juxtaposition is deliberate: Jesus has just described the predators — now he observes their prey. The same Temple system he condemned is receiving this widow's last coins.
[^4]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Is this praise — or lament?*: Three readings: (1) Traditional: Jesus praises the widow's extraordinary faith and generosity — she gives everything, trusting God completely. (2) Critical: Jesus laments a system that takes a destitute woman's survival budget — the passage is an indictment, not a commendation. (3) Both: Jesus recognizes the greatness of her act while grieving the system that demands it. The text itself does not resolve the tension. "Listen" (v.3) is neutral — not approving, not condemning. The reader must hold both possibilities.
[^5]: **LEBEN** — *giving beyond their means*: The widow's act is described as remarkable precisely because it is extraordinary. The text marks it as exceptional, not exemplary. Reading it as a model the rest of us should imitate inverts what Luke is doing: he is honoring a particular act of trust, not legislating a giving standard. And the immediate context (20:47, where Jesus has just denounced religious leaders who "devour widows' houses") makes the picture even sharper. The institution receiving her coins is the same institution Jesus has just identified as exploitative of widows. Both things are true at once: her gesture is luminous, and the system absorbing it is under judgment. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where in your life have you felt religious or community pressure to give beyond what you could actually afford — and felt vaguely guilty when you held back? The tithe expectation that didn't account for the medical bills. The fundraising appeal that escalated the closer to deadline. The cause whose worthiness was used to override the small voice that said this isn't sustainable. The verse is not against generous giving. It is against the moral architecture that turns the widow's exceptional gesture into a baseline anyone holding back has failed. — A low-threshold practice this week: one place where you give from obligation rather than freedom — money, time, emotional availability — try setting a different number, the one that comes from rest rather than fear. Watch what comes up. Often the answer is grief, not greed. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage runs through Paul's careful instruction in 2 Corinthians 8-9: "each one as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver." The medieval church's eventual codification of the tithe (Council of Mâcon, 585) created exactly the system Luke 20:47 had warned against. The Reformation's recovery of free conscience in giving was a return to this text. — The widow is praised. The system that took her last coins is condemned. The Gospel can hold both. — This week: notice where you give from freedom and where you give from fear, and let the distinction stand.
[^6]: **KULTUR** — *the fine stones*: Herod's Temple was one of the ancient world's architectural marvels: massive white limestone blocks (some weighing over 500 tons), a golden vine over the entrance, and votive offerings accumulated over centuries. Josephus (Antiquities 15.394-95) records that the Temple's white stone and gold "reflected so fierce a blaze of fire that the eye was forced to turn away." The disciples' admiration was the normal response. Jesus' prediction of total destruction was scandalous.
[^7]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Was this prophecy fulfilled?*: In 70 CE, Roman legions under Titus destroyed the Temple completely. Josephus (Jewish War 7.1-4) describes the systematic demolition: "Caesar ordered the whole city and the Temple to be razed to the ground." Archaeological evidence confirms the destruction — massive stones from the Temple platform have been found where they fell. The prophecy "not one stone upon another" describes observed historical reality.
[^8]: **TEXT** — *I am the one!*: The Greek \fq Ἐγώ εἰμι\fq* ("I am") echoes the divine name revealed to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14, LXX). The false claimants are not merely saying "I'm the messiah" — they are appropriating the language of divine self-revelation. The gravity of the deception is proportional to the language they steal.
[^9]: **LEBEN** — *it's not the end*: The sentence is almost startling in its plainness. Jesus is asked about the end, and he answers: when you see wars and political collapses, do not be terrified — "it is not the end." The Greek \fq οὐκ εὐθέως\fq* — "not immediately" — closes the door on a particular kind of religious reading that has shadowed Christianity for two thousand years: the impulse to map current events onto a prophetic timeline and confirm that this generation is the one. The verse is not antieschatological; it is anti-credulous. — A question that lands without theological warmup: when was the last time a news cycle convinced you that the world was tipping over the edge? The pandemic. The election. The war. The climate report. The feeling is not wrong; the events are real. But the verse is doing something specific with the feeling. It is asking whether the panic is producing wisdom or paralysis, whether it is sharpening your attention to what is in front of you or evaporating it into apocalyptic abstraction. Jesus does not deny the wars. He denies that they are the signal to stop living. — A low-threshold practice this week: a deliberate fast from doomscrolling for forty-eight hours. Not from being informed — read a single news summary in the morning if you must. But the compulsive refresh, the late-night feed scroll, the dread loop. Just two days. Notice what your attention does when it is not being trained on the next catastrophe. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage of failed end-time predictions is long enough to be a discipline of its own. Montanus in the second century. Joachim of Fiore in the twelfth. William Miller in 1844. Hal Lindsey in the seventies. Harold Camping in 2011. Every generation has read its wars as the wars. Every generation has been wrong. The pattern is the lesson. — The verse does not promise the world is fine. It promises that the believer's posture is steady attention, not panicked decoding. — This week: forty-eight hours of unmediated reality.
[^10]: **KONTEXT** — *words and wisdom*: "I will give you a mouth and wisdom" places Jesus in the role of the divine speech-provider — the tradition of Moses ("I will be with your mouth," Exodus 4:12) and Jeremiah ("I have put my words in your mouth," Jeremiah 1:9). The early church understood this as fulfilled in the eloquence of martyrs before Roman courts: Polycarp, the Scillitan martyrs, Perpetua.
[^11]: **TEXT** — *win yourselves back*: Two issues: (1) The Greek \fq ψυχάς\fq* means "selves/lives" as integrated persons — NOT "souls" in a body-soul dualist sense. The self is what endurance wins back. (2) A significant textual variant exists: \fq κτήσασθε\fq* (aorist imperative — "Win yourselves back!") vs. \fq κτήσεσθε\fq* (future indicative — "You will win yourselves back"). The first is a command; the second is a promise. Both are textually well attested. Either way, the verb \fq κτάομαι\fq* means active acquisition: the self is won through faithfulness, not automatically preserved.
[^12]: **LEBEN** — *endure your suffering*: The setting matters. Luke's audience is a minority community facing imperial state violence — synagogues that have expelled them, magistrates that drag them before governors, the imminent shadow of Nero and Domitian. The endurance Jesus commends is the endurance of a person who refuses to disappear under official pressure. It is not the endurance of a person trapped in a private abuse, told by a pastor to stay because suffering is sanctifying. The misuse of this verse to silence victims of intimate harm or institutional church abuse inverts what Luke is doing: he is preparing people to speak up against unjust power, not down to submit to it. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where in your life have you confused two different kinds of difficulty — the difficulty of staying faithful to something true under pressure, and the difficulty of staying inside something that is harming you? They feel similar from the inside. They look opposite from the outside. The first is endurance; the second is entrapment dressed in the vocabulary of virtue. — A low-threshold practice this week: if there is a situation in your life where the language "I just have to bear this" has been doing heavy lifting, ask one trusted person — outside the situation — whether it sounds like faithfulness or like something else. Just one honest read from outside. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage is precise. The martyrs of Lyon (177 CE) endured the colosseum because they would not deny their faith — and refused to encourage anyone to seek martyrdom. The Confessing Church under Nazism endured because it spoke against the regime; it did not endure because it kept quiet. The contemporary survivor movements within Catholic and Protestant churches are this verse spoken into a long misuse of it: testimony (v. 13), not silence. — Endurance is not the absence of resistance. It is the steadiness that makes resistance possible. — This week: name what you are enduring, and ask whether it is calling you to speak or to stay.
[^13]: **KONTEXT** — *Jerusalem surrounded by armies*: Luke's most significant editorial decision in the entire discourse. Mark 13:14 has "the abomination of desolation standing where it ought not (let the reader understand)" — a Danielic code phrase (Daniel 9:27) requiring insider knowledge to decode. Luke replaces this entirely with plain military language: "Jerusalem surrounded by armies." This makes the prophecy historically actionable and verifiable — no apocalyptic decoder ring required.
[^14]: **TEXT** — *days of judgment*: The Greek \fq ἡμέραι ἐκδικήσεως\fq* literally means "days of vindication/retribution." English "vengeance" sounds personally vindictive; the Greek describes divine judicial action — God acting as judge through historical events. The destruction of Jerusalem is framed as fulfilling prophetic warnings (Jeremiah 7; Deuteronomy 28), not as divine spite.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *the times of the Gentiles*: Luke's unique phrase (\fq καιροὶ ἐθνῶν\fq*) — found nowhere else in the New Testament. Three major interpretive options: (1) The period of Gentile political domination over Jerusalem, ending at the parousia; (2) The period of Gentile mission and conversion (cf. Romans 11:25, "the fullness of the Gentiles"); (3) The providential time allotted to Gentile powers before God's final intervention. The crucial word is "until" (\fq ἄχρι\fq*) — whatever the "times" are, they have a divinely appointed limit. This phrase does NOT endorse any modern political arrangement regarding Jerusalem.
[^16]: **KULTUR** — *fall by the edge of the sword*: Josephus (Jewish War 6-7) confirms the 70 CE fulfillment with remarkable specificity: approximately 1.1 million killed during the siege, 97,000 taken captive (many sent to arena deaths, mines, and slavery throughout the empire). The famine was so severe that Josephus records cases of cannibalism. The phrase "by the edge of the sword" matches his account of mass slaughter; "dragged off as prisoners to every nation" matches the mass deportation.
[^17]: **KONTEXT** — *signs in the sun, moon, stars*: Cosmic disruption language — sun, moon, stars shaken — is conventional Old Testament prophetic vocabulary for divine intervention, not astronomical prediction. Joel 2:10, 31 ("the sun will be turned to darkness and the moon to blood"); Isaiah 13:10 ("the stars of heaven will not show their light"); Ezekiel 32:7-8 (similar imagery for the fall of Egypt). Ancient listeners heard prophetic poetry, not science fiction.
[^18]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *powers of the heavens shaken*: Cosmic disruption — sun darkened, moon failing, stars falling, heavenly powers shaken — is the hardest category for modern readers. Three readings: (1) Literal: actual astronomical and cosmic events preceding the end of history — the universe physically coming apart. (2) Prophetic poetry: conventional Old Testament imagery for God intervening in history (Joel 2:10, Isaiah 13:10, Ezekiel 32:7), understood by ancient audiences as dramatic theological language, not a weather forecast. When Isaiah says the stars "will not show their light" at Babylon's fall (13:10), no one expects literal stellar failure. (3) Symbolic-political: "powers of the heavens" as the spiritual forces behind earthly empires — Rome's cosmic order collapsing before God's kingdom. The text does not choose between these. What it does do is reverse the expected emotional response: while the nations faint with terror, Jesus tells his followers to stand tall. Whatever these signs are, they are not cause for dread but for hope.
[^19]: **KONTEXT** — *the Son of Man coming in a cloud*: Daniel 7:13-14: "One like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven... given dominion and glory and a kingdom." In Daniel, the "son of man" figure represents the faithful community who receive the kingdom after the empires (depicted as beasts) are judged. Jesus identifies himself as this figure. Note Luke's singular "cloud" (\fq ἐν νεφέλῃ\fq*) — the specific cloud of divine presence (the Shekinah, Exodus 13:21), not generic atmospheric phenomenon.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *Stand tall. Lift your heads. Your liberation*: Three translation decisions: (1) \fq ἀνακύψατε\fq* — BDAG: "take heart in expectation of deliverance." Not merely "look up" but the posture of the freed, standing tall. (2) \fq ἐπάρατε τὰς κεφαλάς\fq* — the oppressed look down; the liberated look up. This is a physical-spiritual posture command. (3) \fq ἀπολύτρωσις\fq* — from slave-buying language: the price paid to release a person from captivity. Luke's only use of this word. The parousia is not judgment for the persecuted community — it is liberation day.
[^21]: **TEXT** — *this generation*: The most contested verse in Luke's eschatology. Four major interpretive options: (a) Jesus' contemporaries — the generation alive in ~30 CE; "all things" refers to the 70 CE events (most linguistically natural reading); (b) The Jewish people as a nation — \fq γενεά\fq* as "race/people" (rare usage); (c) An evil/faithless generation type that will persist (no contextual support); (d) The final generation alive at the eschaton (conflicts with the demonstrative "this"). The partial preterist solution: "all things" in v.32 refers to vv.20-24 (historically fulfilled in 70 CE), while vv.25-28 (cosmic events) remain future. This preserves both historical fulfillment and still-future parousia.
[^22]: **LEBEN** — *this generation*: The phrase "this generation" (\fq ἡ γενεὰ αὕτη\fq*) has carried a heavier modern history than almost any other in Luke. Hal Lindsey's Late Great Planet Earth (1970) read it as the generation born after 1948, the founding of the modern state of Israel, and predicted the end before 1988. Forty million copies sold. Predictions failed. Harold Camping repackaged the calculation for 2011. Failed. Each cycle leaves real damage: people who sold houses, abandoned careers, told children there would be no future. The pastoral wreckage is documentable. — A question that lands without theological warmup: where does the urge to know what is coming next pull at you? The anxiety about which way the country tips, which way the climate moves, which way your own life arcs after the next decision. The verse is not asking you to suppress the question; it is asking you to notice what the question does to your life when no answer is granted. Jesus himself, asked directly, refuses dates (v. 7 → v. 8: "do not be deceived"). The refusal is the teaching. — A low-threshold practice this week: one decision you have been postponing until you "know more about how things will turn out" — make it. Not because the future is clear, but because deferring action to a clarity that will not arrive is its own form of being deceived. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage of date-setters is a humbling discipline. The Montanists of Phrygia (2nd century). Joachim of Fiore (12th). The Anabaptists of Münster (1534). The Millerites (1844 — the "Great Disappointment"). Jehovah's Witnesses' multiple revisions. The pattern is not a series of unfortunate miscalculations; it is a structural feature of reading apocalyptic texts as encrypted calendars. — "This generation" is most plausibly read as a Lukan emphasis on continuity — the things Jesus says will continue to be true across time — not as a code to be cracked. — This week: live one day as if the future is not knowable, and notice the difference.
[^23]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *my words will never pass away*: The most radical authority claim in the chapter — and perhaps in the entire Gospel. Jesus places his own words above the permanence of the cosmos. Three readings: (1) Christological: Jesus speaks with divine authority; his words share the permanence of God's own speech. (2) Prophetic: Like the Torah and the Prophets, Jesus' teaching will endure because it carries divine truth. (3) Skeptical: This is a later theological attribution, placing cosmic authority claims on the lips of the historical Jesus. The claim is either the most extraordinary statement ever made, or the most presumptuous. The text presents it without qualification.
[^24]: **LEBEN** — *the anxieties of ordinary life*: The syntax is the teaching. \fq κραιπάλῃ καὶ μέθῃ καὶ μερίμναις βιωτικαῖς\fq* — dissipation AND drunkenness AND the anxieties of ordinary life — joined by a single, repeated \fq καί\fq*. The third item is not a lesser concern smuggled in at the end. It sits on the same syntactic line as the moral vices and carries the same warning. Luke is doing something pastorally important here. He is naming that the people most at risk of having their hearts captured are not only the indulgent — they are also the responsible, the careful, the parents and workers who never get to the end of the list. — A question that lands without theological warmup: at what point in the day does the low hum of worry first switch on? For some people it is the moment of waking, before the alarm. For others it is the commute. For some it is the second cup of coffee, when the mind clears enough to remember everything that is unfinished. Notice the moment. Notice what happens to the body when the hum starts — the shoulders, the breath, the slight forward lean. This is the daily anxiety the verse names. It is not a moral failing. It is a spiritual condition that requires the same kind of watchfulness as the conditions on either side of it. — A low-threshold practice this week: a single fixed point in the day — choose it now — where the worry list is put down for ten minutes. Not solved. Not optimized. Set aside, like setting down a heavy bag at the door. — For readers inside the tradition, the lineage runs through Jesus' own teaching in Luke 12:22-31 ("do not be anxious about your life") and forward into the desert tradition's nepsis — watchful sobriety of the heart. Brother Lawrence in the seventeenth century practiced the presence of God in the monastery kitchen because that is where the daily anxieties lived. The contemporary literature on chronic stress confirms the diagnosis from a different vocabulary: sustained worry narrows attention until nothing larger is visible. — The eschatological vigilance Jesus commends requires freedom from indulgence and from anxiety alike. — This week: ten minutes a day, the bag down.
[^25]: **KONTEXT** — *to stand before the Son of Man*: This is the goal of the entire discourse. The widow stood before the treasury (v.1). The persecuted stand before kings and governors (v.12). Now the faithful stand before the Son of Man — not cowering, not fleeing, but standing firm (\fq σταθῆναι\fq*, from \fq ἵστημι\fq*). The posture is confidence in the presence of the liberator, echoing v.28's "stand tall, lift your heads." The chapter that began with a woman giving everything ends with a community standing in the presence of everything.

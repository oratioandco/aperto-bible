## Kapitel 22


### The Plot Against Jesus


**1** The Feast of Unleavened Bread — the Passover — was drawing near.
**2** The chief priests and the scribes had been looking for a way to do away with him, but they were afraid of the people.
[^1]

**3** Then Satan entered Judas. The one called Iscariot. One of the Twelve.
[^2]
[^3]

**4** He went and conferred with the chief priests and the temple officers about how he might hand him over to them.
[^4]
[^5]
**5** They were delighted. They agreed to give him money.
[^6]
**6** He accepted. From that moment he began looking for an opportunity to hand him over to them apart from the crowd.
[^7]

### Preparing the Passover


**7** The day of Unleavened Bread arrived — the day the Passover lamb had to be sacrificed.
[^8]
**8** Jesus sent Peter and John. "Go," he said. "Prepare the Passover for us to eat."

**9** They said, "Where?"

**10** He said: "When you enter the city, you will meet a man carrying a jar of water. Follow him into the house he enters,
[^9]
**11** and say to the owner: 'The Teacher says to you — where is the guest room where I can eat the Passover with my disciples?'
[^10]
**12** He will show you a large upper room, furnished and ready. Make the preparations there."
[^11]

**13** They went. Everything was exactly as he had said. They prepared the Passover.

### The Last Supper


**14** When the hour came, he reclined at table with the apostles.

[^13]
**15** And he said to them: "With deep longing I have longed to eat this Passover with you before I suffer.
[^14]
**16** I tell you, I will not eat it again until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God."
[^15]

**17** He received a cup, gave thanks, and said, "Take this and divide it among yourselves.
[^16]
**18** For I tell you, from now on I will not drink of the fruit of the vine until the kingdom of God comes."

**19** He took bread. He gave thanks. He broke it. He gave it to them.

**21** "But look — the hand of the one handing me over is with me, on the table.
**22** The Son of Man goes according to what has been determined — but how terrible for that man through whom he is handed over."
[^22]

**23** They began asking one another which of them it could be.

### The Dispute About Greatness


**24** Then a rivalry broke out among them: which of them was to be regarded as the greatest.
[^23]

**25** He said to them, "The kings of the nations lord it over their people, and those in authority over them are called 'benefactors.'
[^24]
**26** Not so with you. Let the greatest among you become as the youngest. Let the leader become as the one who serves at table.
[^25]

**27** For who is greater? The one at the table, or the one serving? Isn't it the one at the table? Yet here I am among you — as the one who serves.
[^26]
[^27]

**28** "You are the ones who have stayed with me in my trials.
**29** And just as my Father has conferred a kingdom on me, I confer one on you —
[^28]
**30** so that you may eat and drink at my table in my kingdom, and sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel."

### Jesus Predicts Peter's Denial


**31** "Simon, Simon. Satan has demanded to have you — all of you — to sift you like wheat.
[^30]
[^31]
**32** But I have prayed for you, that your faith will not give out. And when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers."
[^32]

**33** "Lord," Peter said, "I am ready to go with you to prison. To death."

**34** He said, "I tell you, Peter. Before the rooster crows today, you will deny three times that you know me."

### The Swords


**35** He said to them, "When I sent you out without a purse or a bag or sandals — did you lack anything?" "Not a thing," they said.
**36** "But now — if you have a purse, take it. A bag, take it. And if you have no sword, sell your cloak and buy one.
**37** I tell you, what was written must be fulfilled in me: 'He was counted among the lawless.' What was written about me is reaching its end."
[^34]

**38** They said, "Lord, look — here are two swords." He said, "Enough."

### Gethsemane


**39** He came out and went, as was his custom, to the Mount of Olives. The disciples followed him.
**40** When he reached the place, he said to them, "Pray that you will not fall into temptation."
[^37]

**41** Then he tore himself away from them — about a stone's throw — and knelt down and prayed.
[^38]

**42** "Father, if you are willing — take this cup away from me. Yet not my will — may yours be done."
[^39]
[^40]

**43** [[*An angel from heaven appeared to him, strengthening him.*
**44** *In his agony he prayed more intensely, and his sweat fell like drops of blood to the ground.*]]
[^41]

**45** When he rose from prayer and went back to the disciples, he found them sleeping — from grief.
[^42]

**46** "Why are you sleeping?" he said. "Get up and pray — that you will not fall into temptation."

### The Arrest


**47** While he was still speaking — suddenly, a crowd. And the one called Judas, one of the Twelve, was leading them. He came up to Jesus to kiss him.
**48** Jesus said to him, "Judas — with a kiss you hand over the Son of Man?"
[^43]
**49** When *those around Jesus* saw what was coming, they said, "Lord, should we strike with the sword?"
**50** And one of them struck the high priest's slave and cut off his right ear.

**51** But Jesus said, "No more of this!" And he touched the man's ear and healed him.
[^44]
[^45]

**52** Then Jesus said to the chief priests and temple officers and elders who had come for him, "Have you come out with swords and clubs as if I were a bandit?
[^46]
**53** Every day I was with you in the Temple, and you did not lay a hand on me. But this is your hour — and the power of darkness."
[^47]

### Peter's Denial


**54** They seized him and led him away, bringing him to the high priest's house. Peter followed — at a distance.
**55** *They built* a fire in the middle of the courtyard and sat down together. Peter sat among them.
**56** A servant-girl saw him sitting in the firelight. She stared at him. "This man was with him too," she said.
[^48]
**57** Peter denied it. "Woman, I don't know him."
**58** A little later, someone else saw him. "You're one of them too." Peter said, "Man, I am not."
**59** About an hour later, another person kept insisting: "This man was definitely with him. He's a Galilean."
**60** Peter said, "Man, I don't know what you're talking about." And while he was still speaking — the rooster crowed.

**61** The Lord turned and looked at Peter.

**62** And he went out and wept bitterly.
[^49]
[^50]

### The Trial


**63** The men who were holding Jesus mocked him and beat him.
**64** They blindfolded him and demanded, "Prophesy! Who hit you?"
**65** And they said many other things against him, reviling him.
[^51]

**66** When day came, the council of the elders of the people gathered — both chief priests and scribes — and they brought him before their council.
[^52]
[^53]
[^54]

**67** "If you are the Messiah," they said, "tell us." He said to them, "If I tell you, you will not believe.
**68** And if I ask you, you will not answer.
**69** But from now on, the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the Power of God."
[^55]

**70** They all said, "So you are the Son of God?" He said to them, "You say that I am."
[^56]

**71** They said, "What further testimony do we need? We have heard it ourselves — from his own mouth."

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *the Feast of Unleavened Bread — the Passover*: Passover (Nisan 14) and the Feast of Unleavened Bread (Nisan 15-21) were treated as a single eight-day festival in common speech, as Luke reflects here. The festival celebrated Israel's liberation from slavery in Egypt — the night God "passed over" the marked households. Under Roman occupation, this was the most politically charged week on the calendar: Jerusalem's population swelled five- to tenfold with pilgrims carrying living memories of divine liberation from empire.
[^2]: **KONTEXT** — *Satan entered Judas*: Luke uniquely places Satan's entry into Judas before the Last Supper (John 13:27 places it during the meal). This completes a narrative circle: in 4:13, after the temptation in the wilderness, Satan departed from Jesus "until an opportune time." Now the opportune time has come. In 22:6, the word for "opportunity" (εὐκαιρία) shares the same Greek root as "opportune time" (καιρός) in 4:13. Luke frames the entire passion as the resumption of the cosmic conflict that began in the wilderness.
[^3]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Satan entered*: "Satan entered Judas" is language most modern readers find difficult. The text presents a personal, external evil force taking up residence in a human being — not metaphorical influence or psychological temptation, but spatial entry (the verb εἰσέρχομαι is concrete: "went into"). Interpretive approaches: (1) Literal — a personal spiritual being exercised real agency through Judas, as the text presents. (2) Psychological — "Satan" names the internal process by which greed, disillusionment, or ambition overwhelmed Judas's loyalty. (3) Narrative-theological — Luke uses Satan as a literary-theological character to frame the passion as cosmic conflict, not merely human politics. All three readings take the text seriously. What the text refuses to do is let Judas off the hook: 22:22 pronounces judgment on "that man," holding human responsibility alongside the cosmic framing.
[^4]: **KULTUR** — *temple officers*: The στρατηγοί were commanders of the Levitical temple guard — a Jewish police force under priestly authority, not Roman soldiers. Luke is the only Gospel writer to mention them here. They were the armed enforcement arm that would carry out the nighttime arrest in Gethsemane (22:52).
[^5]: **TEXT** — *hand him over*: The Greek παραδίδωμι — "hand over, deliver up" — is the passion's defining verb, used twelve times in Luke 22-24. English "betray" captures one dimension, but the word also carries a legal register (handing a prisoner to authorities) and, in Paul's theology (Romans 8:32), the sense of God's own self-giving. The same verb describes Judas's act, Pilate's decision, and God's purpose.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *they were delighted*: The Greek ἐχάρησαν is the same verb used for joy over a repenting sinner in Luke 15 (the lost sheep, the lost coin, the lost son). The ironic inversion is sharp: the religious leaders celebrate the instrument of their own judgment.
[^7]: **KONTEXT** — *apart from the crowd*: The crowd is Jesus's political shield. The leaders cannot act publicly because popular support protects him (19:47-48; 20:19; 22:2). The operational constraint — "apart from the crowd" — explains why the arrest must happen at night, in a garden, away from the pilgrim-packed city. The people's absence is the precondition for the powerful to act.
[^8]: **KULTUR** — *the Passover lamb had to be sacrificed*: On the afternoon of Nisan 14, worshipers brought their Passover lambs to the Temple. The lambs were ritually slaughtered by the worshipers themselves, their blood collected by the priests and dashed against the altar. The carcass was then carried home to be roasted for the evening meal. The word "had to" (ἔδει) expresses divine necessity — a key Lukan term for events within God's plan.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *a man carrying a jar of water*: Water-carrying was typically women's work. A man carrying a water jar would have been conspicuous — easy to spot in a crowd. The detail has the logic of a prearranged signal: Jesus had arranged the meeting place in advance, giving Peter and John a visual marker. The elaborate secrecy may indicate that Jesus kept the location from most of the disciples, possibly to prevent Judas — who was already conspiring with the authorities — from revealing it.
[^10]: **TEXT** — *guest room*: The Greek κατάλυμα means a guest room in a private house, not a commercial inn. The same word appears in Luke 2:7 for the room where there was "no place" for the infant Jesus. The owner's immediate cooperation suggests a prior arrangement.
[^11]: **KULTUR** — *a large upper room, furnished*: An ἀνάγαιον — a formal upper-storey dining room furnished with couches for reclining. At Passover, participants reclined on their left elbow in the posture of free people. Slaves served standing; reclining was a statement of liberation. The image most English speakers carry — Leonardo da Vinci's painting of upright figures at a long table — reflects Renaissance dining customs, not first-century practice.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *reclined at table*: The Greek ἀνέπεσεν means "reclined" — the Passover freedom-posture described in the note on 22:12. Free people reclined; slaves served standing. Jesus and the apostles are performing liberation. This is where the meal's political meaning is embodied.
[^13]: **TEXT** — *the apostles*: Luke uses "the apostles" here, not the broader term "disciples." Whether women were present at the meal is not stated. The women who traveled with Jesus and supported his ministry from their own resources (8:1-3) are neither confirmed nor denied at this table.
[^14]: **TEXT** — *With deep longing I have longed*: The Greek Ἐπιθυμίᾳ ἐπεθύμησα is a Semitic cognate dative — an intensifying construction that doubles the verb with its own noun form (cf. Genesis 31:30 LXX). It is the strongest available way to express desire in Greek. "I have wanted" or "I have desired" are total losses. The doubled form is deliberate: this is not casual desire but the longing of someone who can see what is coming and has chosen to walk into it.
[^15]: **KONTEXT** — *until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God*: Jesus frames this Passover as the last on the old side of the kingdom. The next time he eats this meal, it will be in the fulfilled kingdom of God. This is simultaneously a farewell and a promise: the meal points forward to a final banquet that has not yet come.
[^16]: **TEXT** — *He received a cup*: Luke alone among the Gospels records two cups at the Last Supper: this cup before the bread (22:17) and a second cup after the meal (22:20). Mark and Matthew record only one cup, after the bread. The first cup may correspond to the Kiddush cup that opened the Passover meal; the second to the "cup of blessing" after the main course. Luke and Paul (1 Corinthians 11:23-26) appear to share a common liturgical source distinct from Mark's tradition.
[^17]: **TEXT** — *He took bread. He gave thanks. He broke it. He gave it to them.*: These four verbs — taken, thanked, broken, given — form Luke's Eucharistic signature. The same pattern appears at the feeding of the five thousand (9:16) and at the Emmaus meal where the risen Jesus is recognized "in the breaking of the bread" (24:30-31, 35). The four-verb sequence structures Luke's entire theology of meals and recognition.
[^18]: **TEXT** — *This is my body*: These four words have generated more theological controversy than perhaps any other sentence in the New Testament. Catholic tradition (transubstantiation): the bread becomes the body of Christ in its deepest reality, while retaining the appearance of bread. Lutheran tradition (real presence): Christ is truly, bodily present "in, with, and under" the bread. Reformed tradition (spiritual presence): Christ is truly present to faith through the Holy Spirit, though the bread remains bread. Memorial tradition: the bread represents Christ's body; the meal is an act of proclamation and remembrance. The 1982 World Council of Churches document Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry found significant convergence around the concept of anamnesis (living memorial) as a bridge between these positions.
[^19]: **TEXT** — *as my living memorial*: The Greek ἀνάμνησις does not mean "remember me in your minds." In the Jewish liturgical tradition (Hebrew zikkaron), it refers to a living memorial act — a liturgical re-presentation that makes a past saving event present before God and the community. The Passover meal itself was this kind of memorial: not nostalgia for the Exodus but a covenantal act of making the liberation present again. The present imperative "do this" (ποιεῖτε = "keep doing this") establishes the meal as a repeated, ongoing practice of covenant renewal.
[^20]: **TEXT** — *Textual note*: The words "given for you; do this as my living memorial" (v. 19b) and the second cup (v. 20) are found in all major Greek manuscripts, including the earliest papyrus (P75, c. 200 CE) and Codex Vaticanus, as well as in Paul's account (1 Corinthians 11:24-25). A very small number of manuscripts (notably Codex Bezae) omit these words, possibly because early Christians protected the full Eucharistic formula from outsiders during a period when Christian rituals were suspected of cannibalism. The longer text is almost certainly original to Luke.
[^21]: **KONTEXT** — *the new covenant in my blood*: "New covenant" echoes Jeremiah 31:31-34, God's promise of a relationship written on the heart rather than on stone tablets: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts." The "blood" language echoes Exodus 24:8, where Moses sealed the original covenant by splashing sacrificial blood on the people and declaring, "See the blood of the covenant." Jesus is performing a new covenant-sealing: not with the blood of animals at Sinai but with his own blood at a Passover table in Jerusalem. The present participle "poured out" (ἐκχυννόμενον) signals that the act is happening now, not retrospectively.
[^22]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *according to what has been determined — but how terrible*: The theological paradox is stated without resolution: the passion follows a determined divine plan (κατὰ τὸ ὡρισμένον) AND the betrayer bears genuine moral responsibility (οὐαί — a prophetic judgment-word). Jewish and early Christian thought held divine sovereignty and human responsibility simultaneously without requiring one to cancel the other. The text refuses to choose between determinism and free will. Both are real.
[^23]: **KONTEXT** — *a rivalry broke out*: The Greek φιλονεικία (NT hapax — used only here in the New Testament) literally means "love of strife" — a word that carries authorial contempt. This dispute occurs at the Passover table, after the covenant cup (22:20) and the betrayal announcement (22:21-22). The timing is uniquely Lukan: in Mark 10:41-45, the parallel dispute takes place on the road to Jerusalem. Luke places it at the most sacred meal in the story.
[^24]: **KULTUR** — *benefactors*: Εὐεργέτης (euergetes) was not a generic word for a kind person. It was a formal Roman imperial honor title, inscribed on coins, statues, and public monuments. Augustus, Nero, and Hadrian all held it. The benefactor system was the ideological architecture of Roman power: wealthy patrons gave public gifts (temples, baths, festivals), and in return received public honor, client loyalty, and political influence. Jesus names this system by its technical term and categorically rejects it for his community. He is performing counter-imperial discourse at a Passover table — the meal that celebrates liberation from empire.
[^25]: **KULTUR** — *as the youngest*: The νεώτερος (youngest) in ancient household economies had no table precedence — the lowest seat, served last, no voice in decisions. "Become as the youngest" means: become the person with zero standing. The present imperative γινέσθω signals a continuous posture, not a one-time act.
[^26]: **KONTEXT** — *the one who serves*: The Greek ὁ διακονῶν means "the one who serves at table" — literally, the waiter. This is concrete, physical table-service, not abstract "servant leadership" (a modern management concept that has evacuated this word of its original shock). The same verb root (διακονέω) describes the women's service in 8:3: the women who "served" (διηκόνουν) Jesus and the Twelve from their own resources throughout the Gospel have been living the identity Jesus now claims for himself.
[^27]: **LEBEN** — *servant leadership*: "Servant leadership" has become a corporate buzzword — a management technique designed to maximize productivity by making leaders more approachable. That is not what this text describes. Jesus is not recommending a leadership style. He is identifying himself as the waiter — the person who brings food to the table. The passage has also been weaponized to counsel silence and submission to abusive authority: "follow Jesus's example of humble service." But Jesus is not being served upon here; he is choosing to serve. The text describes voluntary self-lowering by the person with the most power, not compulsory submission by the person with the least.
[^28]: **TEXT** — *conferred (διατίθεμαι)*: The verb shares the same root as διαθήκη ("covenant") in 22:20. Jesus "covenants" a kingdom to his apostles by the same kind of act through which the Father "covenanted" a kingdom to him. The farewell gift is royal: the departing king bequeaths his kingdom to his table companions.
[^29]: **KONTEXT** — *eat and drink at my table*: The kingdom is described as a meal. The eschatological vision is table fellowship, not military triumph. The thrones and the judgment of the twelve tribes are real authority — but the path to them runs through the waiter's apron, not the general's saddle. "Judging" (κρίνοντες) carries the Old Testament sense of governing and restoring (as in the book of Judges), not condemning.
[^30]: **KONTEXT** — *Satan has demanded to have you*: The Greek ἐξῃτήσατο means "demanded for himself" — the language of a legal petition, not a casual request. The same verb appears in the Greek translation of Job 2:6, where Satan petitions God for permission to test Job. Satan has filed a claim in a heavenly court, demanding custody of the disciples. The "you" (ὑμᾶς) is plural in Greek: Satan demanded all the disciples, not just Peter. English does not distinguish singular and plural "you," but the plural is critical — the entire community is under assault.
[^31]: **TEXT** — *Simon, Simon*: The doubled vocative signals gravity and urgency — the same pattern used for "Martha, Martha" (10:41) and "Jerusalem, Jerusalem" (13:34). Jesus uses "Simon," not "Peter" — the name he carried before Jesus called him. He addresses the natural self, not the commissioned apostle. The shift back to "Peter" in v.34 is deliberate.
[^32]: **TEXT** — *when you have turned back*: The Greek ἐπιστρέψας ("when you have turned back/returned") assumes the failure. Jesus does not pray that Peter will stand firm; he prays that Peter's faith will survive the collapse. The commission — "strengthen your brothers" — follows the failure, not an alternative to it. Peter's authority will be grounded in his experience of falling and being restored, not in his strength.
[^33]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *before the rooster crows*: Jesus predicts Peter's denial with startling specificity: three denials, before dawn, today. The prediction is fulfilled precisely in 22:54-62. Interpretive approaches: (1) Prophetic foreknowledge — Jesus, as God's anointed, sees what is coming with supernatural clarity. (2) Psychological insight — Jesus knows Peter deeply enough to anticipate how bravado collapses under real pressure; the specifics (three, rooster) may reflect the story's shaping in oral tradition. (3) Post-event narration — the early church, knowing what happened, structured the prediction to match the fulfillment. All four Gospels record this prediction and its fulfillment, making it one of the most multiply attested episodes in the passion tradition. The literary effect is devastating regardless: when the rooster crows in 22:60, the reader already knows.
[^34]: **KONTEXT** — *counted among the lawless*: Jesus cites Isaiah 53:12 — the Suffering Servant who is "numbered with transgressors." The sword instruction makes sense only in light of this citation: Jesus must be arrested looking like the leader of an armed band. The swords create the pretext for treating him as a criminal. The purpose is scriptural, not military.
[^35]: **TEXT** — *Enough*: The Greek Ἱκανόν ἐστιν is almost certainly a conversation-stopper — "Enough of this!" — not an endorsement of two swords as sufficient weaponry. The disciples have taken a scriptural point as a literal call to arms. Jesus ends the conversation. When a sword is actually used during the arrest (22:50), Jesus immediately heals the wound (22:51) and stops the violence.
[^36]: **LEBEN** — *two swords*: This passage has been grotesquely misused to authorize Christian violence. Pope Boniface VIII's Unam Sanctam (1302) used the "two swords" as a proof-text for papal temporal authority. Bernard of Clairvaux developed the allegory. Christian nationalist movements cite this text to justify armed faith. The text itself shows the opposite: Jesus ends the sword conversation, and when a sword is actually drawn, he heals the wound it inflicts.
[^37]: **KONTEXT** — *Pray that you will not fall into temptation*: This echoes the Lord's Prayer that Jesus taught in 11:4 — "do not lead us into temptation." The same instruction opens and closes the prayer scene (22:40 and 22:46), framing the entire Gethsemane episode. Jesus is modeling the prayer he taught.
[^38]: **KULTUR** — *knelt down*: Jewish prayer was normally performed standing, with hands raised. Going to one's knees was a posture of extreme distress, not routine devotion. Ancient listeners would have recognized this as a signal that something was breaking inside Jesus. Luke's Jesus kneels; Matthew's falls on his face. Both communicate crisis through posture.
[^39]: **TEXT** — *may yours be done*: The Greek verb γινέσθω is in the optative/prayer form — "may it be done" — not the flat declarative "your will be done." This is a prayer, not a statement of resignation. Jesus asks honestly for what he wants (the cup removed), then offers the decision to the Father. The "cup" echoes the cup of the Last Supper (22:20): the cup of blessing has become the cup of suffering.
[^40]: **LEBEN** — *take this cup away from me*: This prayer dismantles the idea that faith means stoic acceptance. Jesus does not suppress his fear or pretend to want what is coming. He names what he wants — the cup removed — and then surrenders the outcome. The structure is: honest desire first, then trust. Much religious teaching inverts this, counseling believers to skip the honest part and move straight to submission. Gethsemane models something harder and more human: bringing the full truth of what we feel before God, and then releasing control. The prayer is not answered the way Jesus asks. The cup is not removed. But the one who prays it walks into the passion with a composure that holds through arrest, trial, and crucifixion.
[^41]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *angel ... sweat like drops of blood*: These verses are placed in double brackets because they are absent from the oldest and most reliable manuscripts, including Papyrus 75 (c. 200 CE), Codex Vaticanus, and Codex Alexandrinus. They are likely a very early addition to Luke's text — present in some manuscripts from the 2nd century onward — but almost certainly not part of what Luke originally wrote. "Agony" (ἀγωνία) comes from the Greek word for an athletic contest — intense, focused struggle, not passive anguish. "Like drops of blood" is a simile (ὡσεί = "as if"), not necessarily a medical description. These verses have profoundly shaped Christian piety and art, but their textual status requires this acknowledgment.
[^42]: **TEXT** — *sleeping — from grief*: Luke alone among the Gospels explains the disciples' sleep as caused by grief (ἀπὸ τῆς λύπης). Mark implies negligence. Luke extends compassion: their bodies shut down from sorrow, not indifference.
[^43]: **KULTUR** — *with a kiss*: A kiss between teacher and student was a greeting of respect and intimacy — the gesture of someone who reveres the one he approaches. Judas uses this gesture as the identification signal for the arrest. The weaponization of intimacy is what makes the betrayal devastating. Jesus does not accuse; he asks a question. The question is worse than any accusation.
[^44]: **KONTEXT** — *touched the man's ear and healed him*: This is the only healing during the entire passion — and it is unique to Luke. In the act of being arrested, with his own freedom disappearing, Jesus heals the wound inflicted on an enemy. The violence fell on the lowest-status person in the scene: not a soldier but a slave. Jesus's last act before captivity is mercy.
[^45]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *healed him*: A severed ear, instantly restored by touch. Only Luke records this miracle — Mark, Matthew, and John mention the ear being cut off but not the healing. Interpretive approaches: (1) Historical — Jesus performed a physical healing under arrest, consistent with the healing ministry Luke has documented throughout the Gospel. (2) Theological — Luke adds the healing to make a narrative point: Jesus's response to violence is restoration, not retaliation. The historical question (did the ear literally regrow?) and the theological point (Jesus heals even his captors) can be held simultaneously. What is beyond dispute: Luke wants readers to see that Jesus's last free act is mercy toward an enemy.
[^46]: **TEXT** — *as if I were a bandit*: The Greek λῃστής means an armed insurrectionist or violent rebel — the same word used for Barabbas (23:19) and the crucified criminals. Jesus challenges the absurdity: he taught publicly in the Temple every day, and they treated him as a peaceful teacher. They come for him at night, armed, as though he were leading a revolt.
[^47]: **TEXT** — *the power of darkness*: This phrase (ἡ ἐξουσία τοῦ σκότους) is unique to Luke. ἐξουσία means authority or jurisdiction — darkness has been given temporary power. The arresting party does not act from its own authority but under the jurisdiction of a cosmic force it cannot see. Jesus names the real power behind the nighttime arrest. The implication: their authority is borrowed and temporary.
[^48]: **KULTUR** — *servant-girl*: The Greek παιδίσκη means a young female domestic slave — the lowest social position in the household. The most powerful disciple is identified by the least powerful person present. The irony is structural: Peter, who declared himself ready for prison and death (22:33), cannot hold his ground before a slave girl's observation.
[^49]: **TEXT** — *The Lord turned and looked at Peter*: This moment is unique to Luke. No other Gospel records the look. The Greek verb ἐνέβλεψεν means a penetrating, intentional gaze — the same word used when Jesus looked at the rich young man (18:24). Jesus is being led through or past the courtyard at the precise moment of the third denial and the cock's crow. Luke does not describe what the look conveyed — no anger named, no disappointment, no forgiveness. Only the look. And the memory it triggered. The silence is the power.
[^50]: **LEBEN** — *wept bitterly*: Peter's story does not end here. In Luke 24:34 and Acts 1-2, Peter is restored, commissioned, and becomes the spokesperson for the early church. His authority is not built on strength but on having failed completely and been restored. This pattern — catastrophic failure followed by grace — is central to how the New Testament understands leadership. Peter does not qualify for his role by never falling; he qualifies by falling and being caught. The bitter weeping is not the end of the story. It is the hinge between the old Peter (who trusted his own courage) and the new one (who knows he cannot).
[^51]: **KONTEXT** — *Prophesy!*: The irony is annihilating. Jesus has been prophesying with complete accuracy throughout the chapter: the betrayal (22:21-22), the denial (22:34), the arrest circumstances (22:37). The guards demand a demonstration of the very ability he has already demonstrated. Their mockery is self-condemning.
[^52]: **KONTEXT** — *council of the elders*: Luke's trial takes place at dawn — not at night as in Mark and Matthew. A daybreak session of the priestly leadership is historically more plausible as an emergency consultation than a formal capital trial in the dead of night. The assembled body consists of elders, chief priests, and scribes — specific institutional leaders, not the Jewish people as a whole.
[^53]: **LEBEN** — *the Sanhedrin trial and antisemitism*: The passion narrative's portrayal of Jewish leaders opposing Jesus has been weaponized against Jewish communities for centuries — from medieval passion plays to pogroms to the theological framework that enabled the Holocaust. This reading distorts the text. Luke's trial involves specific institutional actors protecting their institutional position. Luke 23:27, 48 depicts the Jerusalem crowd with sympathy toward Jesus. No reading that assigns collective guilt to the Jewish people is historically accurate or theologically legitimate. The Second Vatican Council (Nostra Aetate, 1965) explicitly repudiated the charge of collective Jewish responsibility for the death of Jesus.
[^54]: **APOLOGETICS** — *when day came*: Luke's dawn trial is historically more plausible than Mark's nighttime proceeding. The Mishnah (Sanhedrin 4:1) prohibits capital trials at night — though these rules may postdate 70 CE. Luke describes an emergency consultation of priestly leadership at first light, not a formal capital trial under later rabbinic rules. The institutional specificity — "chief priests and scribes" gathered at dawn — aligns with what is known about Second Temple priestly authority: the high priest's household controlled Temple operations, revenue, and the Levitical guard (the στρατηγοί of 22:4, 52). Luke's account also omits false witnesses (present in Mark 14:56-59), streamlining the proceeding to a single exchange about messianic identity. Historians note that Luke's version shows signs of independent tradition, not mere editing of Mark.
[^55]: **TEXT** — *from now on*: The phrase "from now on" (ἀπὸ τοῦ νῦν) is distinctively Lukan. In Mark 14:62 Jesus says "you will see the Son of Man coming on the clouds" — a future vision. Luke says the Son of Man "will be seated" at the right hand of God starting now. The exaltation begins at the passion itself. The moment of apparent defeat is the beginning of the enthronement. "The Power of God" (ἡ δύναμις τοῦ θεοῦ) is a Jewish reverential circumlocution for the divine name. The declaration combines Daniel 7:13-14 (the Son of Man receiving dominion) with Psalm 110:1 (seated at God's right hand) in a single claim.
[^56]: **TEXT** — *You say that I am*: Jesus's response is a qualified affirmation in Greek idiom — not a flat denial, but not the direct "I am" of Mark 14:62 either. The formula "You say that I am" (Ὑμεῖς λέγετε ὅτι ἐγώ εἰμι) lets the questioners' own words carry the weight of the confession. They have drawn the conclusion; Jesus does not argue with it. The effect is that the council speaks the truth in the act of condemning it.

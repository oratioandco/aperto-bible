## Kapitel 24


### The Empty Tomb


**1** At the deep edge of dawn, on the first day of the week, the women came to the tomb carrying what they had prepared — the spices *for his body*.
[^1]
**2** The stone had been rolled away.
**3** They went in. The body *of the Lord Jesus* was gone.
[^2]

**4** They stood there, at a loss. Then two men were standing beside them, *their* clothes blazing like lightning.
[^3]
[^4]
**5** The women dropped their faces to the ground.

"Why do you search for the Living One among the dead?" the men said.
[^5]
**6** "He is not here. He was raised. Remember — he told you this, back in Galilee*:*
[^6]
[^7]
[^8]
**7** *that* the Son of Man had to be handed over to sinful men. Had to be crucified. And on the third day, rise."

**8** Then they remembered. His words came back to them.

**9** They returned from the tomb and told everything to the Eleven and to *all* the rest.
**10** It was Mary Magdalene who said these things, and Joanna — *whose* husband ran Herod's household — and Mary the mother of James, and others with them. *They told the apostles all of it.*
[^9]

**11** *But* the apostles heard delirium. They kept refusing to believe *them*.
[^10]
[^11]

**12** Peter, though, got up. He ran to the tomb. He bent down and peered inside — nothing but *the* linen cloths, lying by themselves. He walked away, marveling at what had happened.
[^12]

### The Road to Emmaus


**13** That same day two of them were on the road to Emmaus, a village about seven miles from Jerusalem.
[^13]
**14** They talked as they walked — talked and talked, the same ground covered and re-covered, the same questions with no answers.
**15** Jesus himself came alongside them and walked with them,
**16** but something held their eyes. They did not know him.
[^14]

**17** He said, "What is all this you're discussing as you walk?"

They stopped. Their faces dark.

**18** The one named Cleopas said to him, "Are you the only person staying in Jerusalem who hasn't heard what happened these last few days?"
[^15]

**19** "What happened?" he said.

"Jesus of Nazareth," Cleopas said. "A prophet. Powerful in what he did and what he said — before God and everyone.
**20** Our chief priests and rulers handed him over to be condemned, and they crucified him." He paused.
**21** "We were hoping he was the one who would redeem Israel.
[^16]

"*And now* it's been three days.
**22** Some of the women in our group went to the tomb before dawn this morning. They didn't find the body.
**23** They came back saying they'd seen a vision — angels, who told them he was alive.
**24** Some of our people went to the tomb after that. They found it just as the women said. But him they did not see."

**25** *The stranger* said, "How foolish you are. How slow your hearts are to trust everything the prophets said.
**26** Wasn't this how it had to be — for the Messiah to suffer these things and enter his glory?"
[^17]
**27** Then, starting from Moses and moving through every prophet, he opened the Scriptures to them, interpreting everything written about himself.
[^18]

**28** They drew near the village. He walked on as if he would keep going.

**29** They pressed him. "Stay with us — please. It's getting dark. The day is almost gone."
[^19]

He went in with them.

**30** At the table he took the bread. Blessed *it*. Broke *it*. Gave *it* to them.
[^20]

**31** Their eyes were opened. They knew him.

And he vanished from their sight.
[^21]
[^22]

**32** They turned to each other. "Weren't our hearts burning inside us the whole time? While he talked to us on the road? While he opened the Scriptures?"
[^23]

**33** They got up that very hour and went back to Jerusalem. *When they arrived,* they found the Eleven and the others gathered together,
**34** already saying: "The Lord has truly been raised. He has appeared to Simon."
[^24]

**35** And the two told what had happened on the road — and how they had recognized him in the breaking of the bread.
[^25]

### Jesus Appears to the Disciples


**36** They were still telling the others everything — the road, the stranger, the bread broken and the sudden knowing — when Jesus himself was standing there, in the middle of them. *"*Peace to you,*" he said.*
[^26]

**37** They panicked. They were sure it was a ghost.
[^27]

**38** He said, "Why are you shaken? Why do doubts keep rising in your hearts?
**39** Look at my hands. Look at my feet. It's me. Touch me — feel for yourselves. A ghost does not have flesh and bones. But look: I do."
[^28]

**40** He held out his hands. He showed them his feet.
[^29]

**41** But they still couldn't believe it. The joy was too much — it locked them in place. So he asked them, "Is there anything here to eat?"
[^30]

**42** They handed him a piece of broiled fish.

**43** He ate it. In front of all of them.
[^31]

### The Commission


**44** He said to them: "This is what I meant — what I told you while I was still with you. Everything written about me in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms had to be fulfilled."
[^32]

**45** Then he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures.
[^33]
**46** He said, "This is what stands written: the Messiah would suffer and rise from the dead on the third day,
**47** and repentance and forgiveness of sins would be proclaimed in his name to all nations — beginning from Jerusalem.
[^34]

**48** "You are witnesses to all of this.
[^35]

**49** "Now — I am sending you what my Father promised. But stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high."
[^36]

### The Ascension


**50** Then he led them out as far as Bethany, and he lifted his hands and blessed them.
[^37]
**51** While he was still blessing them, he parted from them — and was carried up into heaven.
[^38]
[^39]

**52** They worshiped him. They returned to Jerusalem with great joy.
[^40]
**53** And they were continually in the Temple, blessing God.
[^41]

## Anmerkungen

[^1]: **KULTUR** — *at the deep edge of dawn*: Luke's phrase ὄρθρου βαθέως ("deep dawn") places the resurrection at the precise hour Scripture associates with divine action: God looked down on Egypt at dawn (Exodus 14:24), the angel struck the Assyrian camp before morning (2 Kings 19:35), and "joy comes in the morning" (Psalm 30:5). The women arrive earlier than Mark's "after sunrise" — Luke's precision signals independence from Mark and underscores the liminal, divine-action quality of the hour.
[^2]: **TEXT** — *of the Lord Jesus*: The phrase "of the Lord Jesus" is absent from Codex Bezae (D) and some Old Latin manuscripts — one of the so-called "Western non-interpolations." The earliest papyrus witness, P75 (c. 175–225 CE), includes it. The phrase is characteristically Lukan in style and is retained in UBS5.
[^3]: **TEXT** — *two men*: Luke says "two men" (ἄνδρες δύο), not "angels" — a deliberate reticence. Their dazzling clothing (the verb ἀστράπτω, "to flash like lightning," is used of the transfiguration garments in 9:29) marks them as heavenly beings without Luke using the word. The doubling may echo the legal requirement of two witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15): the resurrection is attested by sufficient testimony.
[^4]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *clothes blazing like lightning*: Angelic beings — figures in dazzling clothes delivering messages from God — are among the hardest elements for modern readers. Three frames: (1) The literary reading sees these as a biblical convention for divine communication, not unlike a narrator's aside: the story signals "God is speaking now." (2) The phenomenological reading takes the experience seriously on its own terms: something happened to these women that they described using the only categories available — luminous figures delivering a message. (3) The theological reading holds that God communicates through agents, and these were real encounters with real beings, even if their nature lies beyond our categories. What is striking is Luke's restraint: he does not say "angels." He says "two men" — and lets the blazing clothes do the work. The text refuses to over-explain what it cannot fully describe.
[^5]: **KONTEXT** — *the Living One*: "The Living One" (ὁ ζῶν) is not merely descriptive — it is a divine title. Daniel 6:20 addresses God as "the living God"; Revelation 1:18 has the risen Christ say, "I am the Living One; I was dead, and now look, I am alive for ever and ever." The angelic question is a near-rebuke: if Jesus is the Living One, the tomb was never his proper location.
[^6]: **TEXT** — *He was raised*: The divine passive ἠγέρθη ("was raised") — not "he rose" (active) — signals that God is the agent: God raised Jesus from the dead. This is the standard resurrection language in Luke (24:34, 46) and Paul (1 Corinthians 15:4). The resurrection is divine vindication of the one unjustly condemned by human courts.
[^7]: **APOLOGETICS** — *He is not here*: The phrase "He is not here, but was raised" is absent from Codex Bezae and some Old Latin manuscripts, but present in P75 (c. 175–225 CE), Vaticanus, and Sinaiticus. The earliest evidence decisively supports the longer reading. The Western tradition's omission of the explicit resurrection announcement may reflect second-century theological caution rather than an older text.
[^8]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *He was raised*: The bodily resurrection of a dead person is the central claim of Christianity — and the one modern readers find hardest to accept. Several frameworks for thinking about it: (1) The naturalistic view holds that the disciples experienced grief visions or hallucinations. Against this: hallucinations are individual, not shared; they do not produce empty tombs; and the earliest Christians distinguished resurrection from ghost appearances (as Luke 24:37–39 explicitly does). (2) The mythological view reads the resurrection as a symbolic narrative expressing the ongoing significance of Jesus. Against this: Paul's creed in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 (dated to within five years of the crucifixion) lists specific witnesses including 500 people "most of whom are still alive" — an appeal to living testimony, not myth. (3) The historical-theological view holds that something happened to the body of Jesus that the earliest witnesses could describe only as "God raised him" — an event without precedent or analogy, but with real consequences in history. N.T. Wright argues that no combination of an empty tomb (which would produce only a missing-body mystery) and post-mortem appearances (which would produce only a ghost story) accounts for what actually emerged: the sudden, unshakable conviction that Jesus was bodily alive and that a new creation had begun. The text presents the resurrection not as a resuscitation (return to mortal life) but as transformation — a body that eats fish yet passes through locked doors.
[^9]: **KULTUR** — *Joanna*: Joanna appears only in Luke (here and 8:3), where she is identified as the wife of Chuza, Herod Antipas's household manager — a woman with connections to the royal court. Her inclusion among the first witnesses suggests Luke had access to a source with ties to Herodian circles. The three named women represent different social locations: Mary Magdalene from Galilee, Joanna from the court, Mary the mother of James from the family circle.
[^10]: **KULTUR** — *delirium*: The Greek word λῆρος is not merely "nonsense." It is a medical term used in ancient clinical literature for the incoherent babbling of a fever patient (4 Maccabees 5:11; Aristophanes; Josephus, Jewish War 3.405). The apostles do not simply doubt the women — they pathologize their testimony, diagnosing it as a symptom of mental disorder. Women's testimony was generally inadmissible in Jewish legal proceedings. Luke records this dismissal as the apostles' error, not as a normative teaching about women — the narrative will prove the women right and the apostles wrong.
[^11]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *they kept refusing to believe*: The imperfect tense ἠπίστουν indicates sustained, ongoing refusal — not a momentary lapse of faith. Three approaches: (1) The historical-critical reading notes that the criterion of embarrassment strongly supports authenticity — no inventor of the resurrection story in a first-century context would choose women as primary witnesses and then record the male leadership dismissing them. (2) The theological reading sees divine irony — the most important news in history arrives through the least credible messengers by the standards of the day. (3) The social-justice reading notes that Luke records this as failure: the institutional center was wrong; the marginalized witnesses were right. The text vindicates the women.
[^12]: **TEXT** — *Peter ... marveling*: This verse is absent from Codex Bezae (D) and the Western tradition. P75 (c. 175–225 CE) includes it, and its vocabulary is distinctly Lukan rather than simply borrowed from John 20:3–10. Luke carefully distinguishes Peter's "marveling" (θαυμάζων) from faith (πιστεύω). The empty tomb produces reverent wonder — not yet belief. Faith awaits the encounter with the risen Jesus himself.
[^13]: **KULTUR** — *Emmaus*: The identification of Emmaus remains archaeologically unsettled. The distance — sixty stadia, about seven miles — fits several sites: el-Qubeibeh (traditional Franciscan site), Abu Ghosh/Kiriath Yearim (proposed by archaeologists Israel Finkelstein and Thomas Römer in 2019), and Emmaus Nicopolis (though this requires the variant reading of 160 stadia). What matters narratively is the direction: away from Jerusalem. To leave Jerusalem is to leave the community, the hope, the site of the events.
[^14]: **TEXT** — *something held their eyes*: The passive ἐκρατοῦντο ("were being held/restrained") is a divine passive: God prevented them from recognizing Jesus. The non-recognition is not a failure of perception but a divinely orchestrated delay — recognition must come at the right moment, in the right way (at the table, in the breaking of bread). This is Luke's theology of recognition: it cannot be forced or hurried.
[^15]: **KULTUR** — *Cleopas*: Cleopas (Κλεοπᾶς) is a contracted Greek form of Kleopatros. Patristic tradition (Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.11, 32) identifies him with the Johannine "Clopas" (John 19:25) and as a brother of Joseph, making him Jesus's uncle by marriage. Richard Bauckham (Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2017) proposes that the unnamed companion may be Mary, Cleopas's wife — which would make this a husband-and-wife pair. If correct, this would mean a woman's firsthand account of the most extended post-resurrection appearance has been transmitted anonymously while the male companion's name was preserved.
[^16]: **TEXT** — *we were hoping*: The imperfect ἠλπίζομεν is one of the most poignant grammatical forms in the New Testament: hope stated in the past tense — hope that has stopped being present. "We were hoping" (not "we had hoped") preserves the sense of an ongoing expectation that was interrupted. The redemption they expected (λυτροῦσθαι τὸν Ἰσραήλ) was political-national: a new Exodus liberation from Roman domination. They received instead what they interpreted as execution — the fate of failed messiahs.
[^17]: **KONTEXT** — *had to be*: The δεῖ ("it was necessary") of divine necessity is Luke's signature theological word — used 18 times in this Gospel. The suffering and resurrection did not happen accidentally; they happened according to the determined plan of God. This verse extends the divine necessity beyond suffering (as in earlier passion predictions) to include entry into glory: suffering and glory are not sequential steps but a single redemptive movement. No Second Temple Jewish text anticipated a Messiah who would die and rise — this is the early Christian innovation that required a complete rereading of Israel's Scripture.
[^18]: **KONTEXT** — *starting from Moses*: This is the first of three "openings" (διανοίγω) in Luke 24 — the chapter's most important structural pattern. Here: Scriptures opened (v. 32, retrospectively). At Emmaus: eyes opened (v. 31). In Jerusalem: minds opened (v. 45). Eyes → Scriptures → Minds: the risen Christ opens the human capacities for recognition, for reading, and for understanding. The verb διερμήνευσεν ("he interpreted") is intensive (δια- prefix): a comprehensive, beginning-to-end exposition. Luke does not specify which texts Jesus cited — their content is preserved in the speeches of Acts (chapters 2, 3, 7, 13), where early Christians expound the Scriptures christologically.
[^19]: **LEBEN** — *Stay with us*: "Stay with us" (Μεῖνον μεθ' ἡμῶν) is among the most beloved prayers in Scripture. Bach set it as the opening chorus of Cantata BWV 6 ("Bleib bei uns," Easter Monday 1725). It is the prayer of those who have glimpsed something in the interpretation of Scripture and want the presence to continue — who know that darkness is falling and do not want to be left alone. The urgency of the verb παρεβιάσαντο ("they pressed/urged strongly") reflects the ancient Near Eastern obligation of hospitality, especially near nightfall when travel was dangerous. But it also models a spiritual posture: the encounter with God requires pressing, insisting, refusing to let the moment pass.
[^20]: **KONTEXT** — *he took ... blessed ... broke ... gave*: The fourfold eucharistic action — λαβών, εὐλόγησεν, κλάσας, ἐπεδίδου (took, blessed, broke, gave) — uses the same four verbs as the Last Supper (22:19) and the Feeding of the Five Thousand (9:16). The verbal repetition is Lukan sacramental theology: the risen Jesus is recognized in the gesture that constitutes the community. Note the role reversal: Jesus arrived as a guest but now acts as host — taking, blessing, breaking, giving. Recognition comes not through the face, not through the teaching, but through the breaking of the bread.
[^21]: **TEXT** — *vanished*: The word ἄφαντος is a hapax legomenon — it appears only here in the entire New Testament. Literally "un-visible" (ἀ-φαντος), it does not describe departure but imperceptibility: Jesus did not walk away; he became un-seeable. The vanishing is as theologically important as the recognition: once known, the risen Christ cannot be held or possessed. He appears and withdraws on his own terms. His resurrection body is not subject to human control.
[^22]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *he vanished from their sight*: A person who was visibly, physically present — eating bread at a table — suddenly becomes invisible. This strains any naturalistic framework. Three approaches: (1) The skeptical reading treats it as legendary embellishment. Against this: the detail is so specific (mid-meal, after recognition, using a word found nowhere else in the NT) that it resists the patterns of legendary growth. (2) The phenomenological reading notes that the vanishing describes not what Jesus did but what the disciples experienced — a sudden absence after a moment of intense presence. (3) The theological reading sees the vanishing as essential to Luke's resurrection theology: the risen Christ is real but not controllable. He appears on his own terms and withdraws on his own terms. The body that can eat fish can also become un-seeable. The resurrection is not a return to ordinary life but an entry into a new mode of existence that intersects with ordinary life without being bound by it.
[^23]: **LEBEN** — *hearts burning*: The "burning heart" (καιομένη ἦν ἡ καρδία) is one of Scripture's most arresting experiential statements. The grammar — a periphrastic imperfect — emphasizes that the burning was continuous during the walk: it was happening the whole time. Critically, the burning preceded recognition. The disciples felt something they could not name while Jesus interpreted Scripture; only in retrospect do they identify it as encounter with the living Christ. This models a form of spiritual discernment that works backward: you recognize the presence after the fact, in the looking-back. The question is not "What do I feel now?" but "What was burning in me then?"
[^24]: **TEXT** — *appeared to Simon*: This is a pre-Lukan kerygmatic formula matching Paul's credal tradition in 1 Corinthians 15:5 ("he appeared to Cephas"). Luke records the appearance to Simon without narrating it — the most important private post-resurrection appearance is reported, not described. The name "Simon" (birth name) rather than "Peter" (apostolic name) may signal a personal, not vocational, encounter — restoration of the man who denied Jesus three times (22:54–62). The emphatic ὄντως ("truly, really") is the community's confession: this is not delirium. It is fact.
[^25]: **KONTEXT** — *in the breaking of the bread*: The phrase "the breaking of the bread" (ἡ κλάσις τοῦ ἄρτου) becomes a technical term for eucharistic table fellowship in Acts (2:42, 46; 20:7, 11). The verb ἐξηγοῦντο ("they narrated") is the etymological root of the English word "exegesis." The disciples' narration of the Emmaus encounter — how they recognized Jesus in the breaking of the bread — is the first act of Christian testimony: personal experience of the risen Christ, told to the community.
[^26]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *Jesus himself was standing there*: A man who was publicly executed two days earlier is now standing in a room with his followers. The resurrection appearance is not a vision or a feeling — Luke presents it as a physical, witnessed event. The disciples' first reaction is not joy but terror: they think they are seeing a ghost (v. 37). Jesus then systematically demolishes every alternative to bodily presence: look at my hands and feet (visual), touch me (tactile), flesh and bones not ghost (anatomical), watch me eat fish (metabolic). This four-stage argument is the most rigorous anti-hallucination, anti-ghost, anti-vision apologetic in the New Testament. The challenge for modern readers: either something extraordinary happened in that room, or the earliest Christian community fabricated a story that was designed to withstand exactly the kind of skepticism they themselves initially displayed.
[^27]: **KULTUR** — *a ghost*: The disciples' immediate category is πνεῦμα — a disembodied spirit, a shade of the dead. Belief in ghosts was common in both Jewish and Greco-Roman popular culture: the Septuagint has the witch of Endor raise the shade of Samuel (1 Samuel 28); Greco-Roman literature is full of apparition stories. The disciples are not stupid — they are applying the most available cultural category. Jesus's systematic physical counter-evidence (look, touch, flesh-and-bones, eating fish) dismantles this category step by step.
[^28]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *flesh and bones*: The phrase "flesh and bones" (σάρκα καὶ ὀστέα) — deliberately not "flesh and blood" (σάρκα καὶ αἷμα) — is one of the most theologically precise statements in the resurrection narratives. Paul writes that "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Corinthians 15:50). The resurrection body has flesh and bones — physical, tangible — but the life that sustains it is no longer biological blood (which was poured out in death, Luke 22:20) but the power of God. Three readings: (1) The physicalist reading takes this as literal description: the risen body is materially continuous with the crucified body, minus the blood. (2) The theological reading sees this as anti-docetic polemic: against those who claimed Christ only appeared to have a body. (3) The transformationist reading holds that the resurrection body is genuinely physical but differently sustained — real flesh and bone, but animated by divine power rather than biological processes. Luke invites touch as the test.
[^29]: **TEXT** — *He held out his hands*: This verse is absent from Codex Bezae (D) and several Old Latin manuscripts. P75 includes it. The specificity of "hands and feet" (not "hands and side," as in John 20:20) is distinctly Lukan and anti-docetic: the hands and feet bear the crucifixion wounds (nails, not the spear wound of John). If a copyist had inserted this verse from John, "side" would appear. Its absence argues for Lukan originality.
[^30]: **LEBEN** — *the joy was too much*: "Disbelieving from joy" (ἀπιστούντων ἀπὸ τῆς χαρᾶς) is one of the most humanly recognizable descriptions in the New Testament. The news is too good to take in. Luke is not criticizing the disciples — he is recording a psychologically real response to overwhelming positive reality. This models something important about the reception of grace: the deepest gifts are often the hardest to believe. The practice of receiving joy — not treating overwhelming grace as too good to be true — is itself a form of faith.
[^31]: **APOLOGETICS** — *he ate it*: The eating of broiled fish is Luke's most mundane and most powerful anti-docetic argument. In most Jewish traditions, angels did not eat earthly food; spirits had no need for food. Jesus eats — publicly, witnessed (ἐνώπιον αὐτῶν, "before them") — the most ordinary possible meal. The resurrection is not ghostly survival or spiritual ascent. It is a body that eats fish. Luke's four-stage apologetic — visual inspection (v. 39a), haptic examination (v. 39b), anatomical claim (flesh and bones), and eating (v. 43) — systematically forecloses every alternative to bodily resurrection.
[^32]: **KONTEXT** — *the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms*: This is the only New Testament passage that names all three divisions of the Hebrew Bible: Torah (Law of Moses), Nevi'im (Prophets), and Ketuvim (Writings — here specified as "Psalms," which headed that collection). This may be the earliest extant reference to the Hebrew canon in roughly its final form. By naming the Psalms specifically, Jesus points to the Psalter as the primary witness to his suffering and vindication — Psalms 22, 31, 69, and 110 were the foundational passion and exaltation texts in early Christian interpretation.
[^33]: **KONTEXT** — *he opened their minds*: This is the third and climactic occurrence of the verb διανοίγω ("to open") in Luke 24. The pattern: eyes opened (v. 31, at Emmaus), Scriptures opened (v. 32, retrospective), minds opened (v. 45, in Jerusalem). The risen Christ is the one who opens. In verse 45, the verb governs τὸν νοῦν ("the mind") — the rational, deliberative faculty. This is an epistemological claim of the highest order: resurrection faith requires not just new information but a cognitive transformation that only the risen Christ can effect. The capacity to read Israel's Scripture as a coherent witness to the Messiah did not exist before this opening.
[^34]: **KONTEXT** — *repentance and forgiveness of sins*: This is Luke's Great Commission — and it is distinctively Lukan. Matthew's commission (28:19–20) centers on baptism and discipleship; Luke's centers on the proclamation of μετάνοιαν καὶ ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν. This pair directly echoes Luke's programmatic statements: John's baptism of "repentance for the forgiveness of sins" (3:3), Jesus's mission statement of "release to the captives" (4:18), and the table fellowship with sinners that characterizes Jesus's entire ministry. The commission is the capstone of the Gospel's theological programme.
[^35]: **KULTUR** — *witnesses*: The Greek μάρτυρες carries legal weight: these are eyewitnesses giving testimony to what they have seen. This self-designation structures the entire book of Acts (1:8: "you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth"). The apostles are not theologians constructing a doctrine; they are witnesses reporting an event. The Greek μάρτυς eventually gives the English word "martyr" — those who witness unto death. The connection between testimony and suffering is already latent here.
[^36]: **KONTEXT** — *clothed with power from on high*: The clothing metaphor for the Spirit (ἐνδύσησθε, "you are to clothe yourselves") echoes Isaiah 32:15, where the Spirit is "poured out from on high," and 1 Chronicles 12:18, where the Spirit "clothed" Amasai. The command to "stay in the city" reflects the necessity of gathered waiting — the Spirit's coming requires the community's assembled prayer (Acts 1:14). This is the bridge to Acts: everything in Luke 24:44–49 is recapitulated in Acts 1:1–8.
[^37]: **KULTUR** — *he lifted his hands and blessed them*: The gesture of lifting hands in blessing is unmistakably priestly — the Aaronic blessing posture of Leviticus 9:22, Sirach 50:20–21, and Numbers 6:24–27. The final image of Jesus in Luke's Gospel is the High Priest at the completion of sacrifice, interceding for the people. That the departure occurs near Bethany — the village of Mary, Martha, and Lazarus (Luke 10:38–42) — means it happens in a place of friendship and love, contrasting with the crucifixion site outside the city walls.
[^38]: **TEXT** — *was carried up into heaven*: The phrase "and was carried up into heaven" (καὶ ἀνεφέρετο εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν) is absent from the original hand of Codex Sinaiticus and from Codex Bezae. P75 and Vaticanus include it. The imperfect passive ἀνεφέρετο presents the ascending as ongoing and God-initiated — not "he ascended" (active) but "he was being carried up." Luke 24 presents the ascension within the Easter Sunday narrative frame; Acts 1:3 specifies forty days between resurrection and ascension. The apparent discrepancy resolves when the literary function of each passage is understood: the Gospel compresses for narrative closure; Acts provides the fuller chronological account.
[^39]: **UNGLAUBLICH** — *carried up into heaven*: The ascension — a physical body rising into the sky — is perhaps the most difficult image for modern readers. Three frames: (1) The cosmological reading notes that "heaven" in ancient thought was the realm of God's presence, not a location above the clouds; the ascension is a spatial metaphor for entry into God's presence. (2) The theological reading (emphasized in Eastern Orthodox tradition) sees the ascension as the completion of theosis — humanity itself, in the person of Christ, carried into the presence of God. Christ does not leave humanity behind; he takes it into glory. (3) The historical reading notes that whatever the disciples experienced, they interpreted it through available spatial categories. All three readings preserve the essential claim: Jesus's departure was not abandonment but glorification — the precondition for Pentecost and the Spirit's universal presence.
[^40]: **TEXT** — *they worshiped him*: The phrase "having worshiped him" (προσκυνήσαντες αὐτόν) is absent from Codex Bezae. P75 and Vaticanus include it. The act of προσκυνέω is full divine worship — the same posture offered to God. Its inclusion makes explicit what the narrative implies: the disciples worship the risen, ascending Jesus as divine.
[^41]: **KONTEXT** — *continually in the Temple, blessing God*: The Gospel ends where it began. Zechariah's vision in the Temple opened the Gospel (1:5–25); the disciples' continual worship in the Temple closes it. The circular frame is deliberate: Luke's Gospel is a great arc from Temple to Temple, blessing to blessing. The verb εὐλογέω ("to bless") creates a chain across the final four verses: Jesus blesses the disciples (v. 50), while blessing them he departs (v. 51), and the disciples bless God (v. 53). Blessing circulates — from Christ to community to God — as it does in liturgical worship. The Gospel does not merely end. It commissions. And the chapter that commissions prepares for Acts, which begins in Jerusalem and expands to the ends of the earth.
